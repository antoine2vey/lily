Most peperomia problems come from too much water. Their thick, fleshy leaves store water, so the compost should partly dry out between waterings, and roots kept wet rot quickly. Leaves dropping, yellowing or turning soft usually point back to the pot. The rest are light, cold and a short list of sap-sucking pests. Peperomias grow best at 18 to 30°C (65 to 86°F) in bright, indirect light.

## Peperomia problems: check the compost first

Push a finger 2 to 3 cm (1 in) into the compost before you change anything. The [RHS peperomia guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/peperomia/how-to-grow-peperomia) says to let the compost partially dry out between waterings, always checking with a finger rather than watering on a routine. It warns that watering too much, or leaving the pot standing in water, can lead to root rot that kills the plant.

Also check the pot. The RHS notes that a pot too big for the root ball stays wet for longer and can rot the roots, and [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/peperomia-peperomia-spp-indoor-plant-care-and-growing-guide/) recommends moving up to a pot only slightly larger than the grower's pot.

## Why is my peperomia dropping leaves?

**1. Overwatering.** This is the most common cause. Clemson says plainly that an overwatered peperomia drops its leaves. Healthy-looking leaves falling off at a touch, often from the lower stems, with damp compost, is the typical pattern. Let the compost dry well before you water again, and empty the saucer after each watering.

**2. Root rot.** If leaves are dropping and the stems are soft or dark at the base, the roots have started to rot. The RHS describes rot as causing yellowing and dropping leaves, and eventually the collapse of the whole plant. See the rot section below.

**3. Cold or draughts.** The RHS advises keeping peperomias out of cold draughts and away from radiators. A sudden chill next to a cold window or an open door in winter can make leaves fall.

## Why are my peperomia leaves turning yellow?

**1. Overwatering.** NC State Extension lists root rot and yellowing or curling leaves as signs of overwatering. Yellow leaves that feel soft, with compost that is still wet days after watering, point here. Water less often and check that the pot drains.

**2. Old leaves.** One lower leaf yellowing and falling now and then, while new growth looks healthy, is normal ageing.

**3. Low light.** A peperomia kept far from any window grows weak and pale, and older leaves can yellow. The RHS says too much shade gives poor, straggly growth.

Our guide to [why plant leaves turn yellow](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) covers the general patterns.

## Why is my peperomia drooping?

**1. Root rot.** A peperomia that droops while the compost is wet is in trouble. Water cannot reach the leaves through rotten roots, so the plant wilts as if it were dry. Check the roots right away.

**2. Thirst.** NC State notes that underwatering causes wilting. If the compost is dry and the pot feels light, water thoroughly and let it drain. The leaves should firm up again once the plant has taken up water.

**3. Heat.** A plant next to a radiator or in a hot, sunny window can droop from heat and dry air. Move it to a cooler, bright spot.

To tell 1 from 2: feel the compost. Wet compost and limp leaves mean rot. Dry compost and limp leaves mean thirst.

## Why are my peperomia stems soft or mushy?

That is rot, and the RHS and Clemson both name root rot from overwatering as the main disease of peperomia. Act fast.

1. Take the plant out of its pot and remove the old compost.
2. Cut away all black, brown or mushy roots and stems with clean scissors.
3. If healthy stems remain, take cuttings from them as a backup. Many peperomias root from stem or leaf cuttings.
4. Repot what is left into a clean, slightly small pot of fresh, free-draining mix.
5. Water lightly, then wait until the compost has partly dried before watering again.

Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) walks through each step.

## Why does my peperomia have brown leaf tips or edges?

**1. Fluoride in tap water.** The RHS notes that peperomias are sensitive to fluoride in tap water, which can turn the leaf tips brown, and recommends rainwater or filtered water at room temperature.

**2. Dry air.** The RHS also suggests raising the humidity if the leaves turn brown. Most peperomias are fine in normal home humidity, but central heating can dry the air a lot in winter. Grouping plants or running a humidifier helps.

**3. Sun scorch.** Brown, dry patches in the middle of leaves facing a window are sunburn. See below.

## Why are my peperomia leaves curling?

NC State lists curling, along with yellowing, as a sign of overwatering. Curling can also follow a long dry spell. As always, the compost tells you which. Wet compost means hold off on water and check the roots. Dry compost and thin, wrinkled leaves mean water.

## Why does my peperomia have scorched or faded patches?

Direct sun. The RHS warns that summer sun can scorch peperomia leaves, though weaker winter sun is usually welcome. NC State also notes that direct sunlight can scorch the leaves. Bleached, papery or brown patches on the window side are the sign. Move the plant to an east- or west-facing window or set it back from a south-facing one, as the RHS suggests. Scorched patches do not heal, but new leaves will grow in clean.

## Why is my peperomia leggy?

Long, bare stems with small leaves spaced far apart mean the plant is reaching for light. Move it to a brighter spot with indirect light, and pinch or cut back the stretched stems in spring to make it bushier. The cut tips can be rooted as cuttings.

## Why does my peperomia have spots on its leaves?

Clemson lists fungal leaf spots among peperomia diseases and says they can be prevented by keeping the leaves dry when you water. NC State adds good air circulation. Water the compost, not the leaves, and remove badly spotted leaves.

## What pests attack peperomias?

Clemson lists mealybugs, spider mites, fungus gnats, scale and whiteflies. The RHS names mealybugs, which it describes as sap-sucking insects.

- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony clusters in leaf joints and under leaves. Dab them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol and check every week.
- **Spider mites.** Fine webbing and pale speckles on the leaves, worse in warm, dry rooms. Rinse the plant thoroughly in the shower, upper and lower leaf surfaces, and repeat weekly.
- **Scale.** Small brown bumps on stems and leaf undersides that scrape off with a fingernail.
- **Whiteflies.** Tiny white insects that flutter up when you touch the plant.
- **Fungus gnats.** Small flies around the compost. Their larvae live in compost that stays damp, so letting the top dry out between waterings is the main fix.

## Is peperomia safe for cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/baby-rubber-plant) lists the baby rubber plant, *Peperomia obtusifolia*, as non-toxic to dogs, cats and horses.
