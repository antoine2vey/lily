How to care for a terrarium comes down to reading the condensation. A closed terrarium recycles its own water and usually needs only a little water a few times a year, while an open one needs light watering more often. Give it bright, indirect light for at least 6 hours a day, keep it out of direct sun, and remove dead leaves before they turn to mould.

## How to care for a terrarium depends on its type

The [RHS guide to terrariums and bottle gardens](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/houseplants/bottle-gardens-and-terrariums) describes two kinds.

- **Closed terrariums** have a lid or a narrow neck. Humidity stays high and water recirculates, so small, slow-growing tropical plants, miniature ferns and mosses do well. The RHS suggests plants such as peperomias, fittonias, polka dot plant (*Hypoestes*), small pileas and *Selaginella*.
- **Open terrariums** have a wide, uncovered top. Moisture escapes, so they suit plants that do not need high humidity, such as succulents and cacti.

Do not put succulents or cacti in a closed terrarium. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/creating-a-closed-terrarium/) says they do not make good closed terrarium plants and should be saved for open terrariums or dish gardens.

Clemson Extension notes that open terrariums are drier and less prone to disease, which makes them easier to keep healthy.

## How to water a terrarium

**Closed terrariums.** The RHS says a closed terrarium usually needs water just a few times a year, and only a little. [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/over-garden-fence/2023-10-09-diy-bottle-terrariums-make-great-projects-gifts) puts a tightly sealed container at about once every four to six months. Penn State gives three signs that it is time to water:

- the condensation on the glass has stopped,
- the plants start to droop,
- the soil feels dry.

When you water, go gently. The RHS suggests using a mister and letting the water run down the inside of the glass, which also cleans it. Check again a couple of days later. Light condensation means there is just enough water.

**Open terrariums.** These dry out faster. Clemson says they need occasional watering, though less often than other houseplants. It advises light waterings only, since heavy ones leave water standing at the bottom, and says it is better to be a little too dry than too wet.

## Reading the condensation

Condensation is your gauge in a closed terrarium. Penn State describes the target as a light fog on the inside of the glass that runs back down to water the soil.

- **No condensation at all.** The terrarium is drying out. Check the soil and water sparingly.
- **A light fog.** The balance is right. Leave it alone.
- **Heavy droplets that hide the plants.** Too wet. The RHS says to wipe some away with a cloth or leave the lid open for a while. Penn State suggests taking the lid off for a day or two.

Penn State also recommends opening a tightly sealed terrarium for up to 15 minutes once a week to let fresh air in.

## Light and placement

The RHS recommends bright, indirect light for at least six hours a day. A spot near a north- or east-facing window suits most of the year, and a south- or west-facing window works in winter. Clemson says it should sit close to a bright window but not in direct sun.

Keep it out of direct sun. Penn State warns bluntly that a terrarium in direct sunlight will cook. Keep it away from radiators and other heat sources too.

If you have no suitable window, the RHS suggests grow lights, because ordinary household bulbs are not enough for plants. Illinois recommends turning the container once a week so the plants grow evenly rather than leaning toward the light. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge the light in a room.

Choose plants with similar needs. Clemson warns against mixing a low-light plant with one that needs high light, because one of them will do poorly.

## Pruning and tidying

A terrarium only stays in balance if the plants stay small.

- **Pinch tips early.** Clemson says frequent pinching gives more balanced growth than occasional hard cutbacks.
- **Trim leaves that touch the glass** or grow out of bounds, as Penn State suggests.
- **Remove dead or yellow leaves straight away.** The RHS says fallen debris quickly turns to rot and mould in the humid air.
- **Replace a plant that outgrows the space.** The RHS points out that many standard tropical houseplants, even young ones, become too vigorous for a terrarium over time.

Use long tweezers or small scissors in narrow containers. Our guide on [how to prune houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-prune-houseplants) covers clean cuts.

## Feeding

Terrarium plants are meant to grow slowly, so feed very little. Clemson says not to fertilise for at least a year after planting, and then only if the plants look yellowish and weak, using about one tenth of the usual houseplant dose. Penn State suggests a quarter-strength feed in spring after the first year, and none in winter.

## Common terrarium problems

**Mould and rot.** The usual cause is too much water. The RHS lists yellowing or dying leaves, usually from overwatering, and excess condensation, which makes plants rot. Clemson says to remove any rotting plant and leave the cover off to let the terrarium dry. Penn State notes that disease usually takes hold in the first few weeks, so watch a new terrarium closely. Our guide to [houseplant fungal diseases](/en/blog/houseplant-fungal-diseases) explains the common moulds.

**Leggy, pale plants.** Not enough light. Move the terrarium closer to a window, but still out of direct sun.

**Scorched or wilting plants on a sunny day.** Too much sun. Move it out of direct light at once.

**A plant taking over.** Pinch it back, or replace it with something slower. The sources disagree on ferns. The RHS suggests small-leaved ferns for closed terrariums, while Clemson advises against ferns because they take over. If you use one, pick a compact type and trim it often.

## About the gravel layer

Most guides start a terrarium with a layer of gravel for drainage, and the RHS and Clemson still recommend one. Penn State disagrees. It says a rock layer is no longer thought to improve drainage, and instead mixes a handful of charcoal into the potting mix. Either way, a terrarium has no drainage hole, so the safest habit is the one all three agree on: water sparingly.
