Boston fern care comes down to three things: bright light without direct sun, compost that stays evenly moist and never dries out, and air that is more humid than most heated homes. It does best at 16 to 21°C (60 to 70°F). Most Boston ferns that shed piles of crisp brown leaflets dried out once, or spent the winter beside a radiator.

## Boston fern care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect. A metre or so from an east or north window; no midday sun |
| Water | When the surface just starts to feel dry. Never let the whole pot dry out |
| Humidity | Higher than the 40 to 50% most houseplants accept. A humidifier in winter |
| Temperature | 16 to 21°C (60 to 70°F), away from radiators and cold draughts |
| Soil | Peat-free houseplant compost that holds moisture, in a pot with drainage |
| Feeding | Balanced liquid fertiliser at half the label rate, monthly, spring to late summer |
| Toxicity | Non-toxic to cats and dogs |

The Boston fern is a selection of *Nephrolepis exaltata*, a fern of humid, shaded places. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/nephrolepis-exaltata/) sums up what it needs indoors: bright indirect light, warmth, high humidity, and moist soil that should never be allowed to dry out. Everything below follows from that.

## How much light does a Boston fern need?

Bright, indirect light. Good spots are close to a north-facing window, a metre or so back from an east-facing one, or behind a sheer curtain at a south or west window. Clemson Extension puts Boston fern in its medium-light group, alongside African violet and dumb cane.

Too much direct sun bleaches the fronds and burns them brown and crisp. Too little light gives sparse, pale, stretched fronds and a plant that thins out from the centre. A hanging basket in a bright bathroom or kitchen window is a classic spot because it gives light and humidity at once.

## How often should you water a Boston fern?

Water as soon as the surface of the compost starts to feel dry, and water thoroughly: pour until it runs out of the drainage hole, then empty the saucer after 15 minutes. In a warm, bright room in spring and summer, that is often every two to four days for a hanging basket and once or twice a week for a pot on a shelf. Small pots and baskets dry out fastest.

A Boston fern punishes the first time it dries out completely. The leaflets turn grey-green, then brown and crisp, and drop when you touch the plant. Lift the pot after watering to learn its weight; a light pot means it needs water now.

In winter the plant rests. NC State advises reducing watering then, because overwatering a dormant fern leads to root rot. Keep the compost just moist rather than wet. ## How much humidity does a Boston fern need?

More than your living room probably has in winter. [Iowa State Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/air-our-home-extremely-dry-winter-should-i-mist-houseplants) notes that many houseplants prefer 40 to 50% relative humidity while heated homes in winter can drop to 10 to 20%. Ferns want the upper end of that range or more, and dry air shows up as brown leaflet tips and edges.

What works, in order of effect:

1. **A humidifier** in the same room. It is the only method that raises humidity reliably.
2. **A bathroom or kitchen** with a bright window. NC State suggests both.
3. **Grouping plants** together. Their transpiration raises the humidity around them a little.
4. **A pebble tray**: a saucer of gravel kept topped up with water, with the pot on the gravel and not in the water.

Misting is the method most often recommended and the least useful. Iowa State explains that the water evaporates within minutes, so you would need to mist several times a day to change anything. A cheap hygrometer tells you what you are actually working with. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares each method.

## What temperature does a Boston fern like?

NC State gives 16 to 21°C (60 to 70°F). Ordinary room temperature suits it, as long as it is away from the dry heat of radiators and heating vents and from cold draughts at a door or single-glazed window in winter. Heat combined with dry air is harder on a Boston fern than mild cold.

## Can a Boston fern go outside in summer?

Yes, once nights stay warm. A shaded porch or the north side of the house suits it, out of direct sun and wind. Outdoors it dries out much faster than indoors, so expect to water every day in hot weather, and check under the fronds for insects before you bring it back in at the end of summer.

## Boston fern care in winter

Winter is when most Boston ferns decline, because indoor air is driest and light is lowest.

- Move it to the brightest spot that is not near a heater.
- Run a humidifier, or group it with other plants in a cooler, brighter room.
- Water less often, keeping the compost just moist, never soggy and never dry.
- Stop feeding until spring.
- Expect some yellowing and leaflet drop on the oldest fronds. Cut them off at the base.

## Soil, feeding and repotting

Use a peat-free houseplant compost that holds water without staying soggy, in a pot with a drainage hole. Boston ferns spread by thin runners and fill a pot quickly. Repot in spring when the roots fill the pot or the plant dries out within a day of watering, going up only one pot size.

You can divide a large fern at the same time. Cut the root ball into two to four pieces with a sharp knife, each with fronds and roots, and pot them separately. Runners that touch compost also root and can be cut off as new plants.

Feed from spring to late summer only. A balanced liquid fertiliser at half the label strength once a month is plenty; overfeeding leaves salts in the compost that brown the frond tips. Our guide to [fertilising houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) explains how to flush those salts out.

## Common Boston fern problems

- **Brown, crisp leaflets dropping everywhere:** the compost dried out, or the air is too dry. Water thoroughly and raise the humidity.
- **Yellow fronds and a sour smell from wet compost:** overwatering, especially in winter. Let the surface dry before watering again and check the roots for rot.
- **Rows of brown dots under the fronds:** spores. NC State warns not to mistake them for insects. They are normal.
- **Fine webbing or tiny bumps on the stems:** spider mites or scale. NC State notes both appear when the air is too dry.
- **Pale, scorched patches:** direct sun.

Each of these has its causes and fixes in our article on [Boston fern problems](/en/blog/boston-fern-problems).

## Is a Boston fern safe for cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/boston-fern) lists Boston fern as non-toxic to cats, dogs and horses. It is one of the safer choices for a hanging basket a cat can reach.
