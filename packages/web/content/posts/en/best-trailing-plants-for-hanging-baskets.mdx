The best trailing plants for hanging baskets indoors are pothos, heartleaf philodendron, string of pearls, string of hearts, hoya, spider plant, Swedish ivy, tradescantia and burro's tail. Outdoors in summer, the RHS suggests trailers like creeping Jenny, *Dichondra* 'Silver Falls', trailing lobelia, petunias and ivy-leaved pelargoniums. Whatever you pick, a hanging basket dries out faster than a pot on the floor, and an outdoor basket in hot weather can need water every day.

## Best trailing plants for hanging baskets indoors

These all trail well from a basket hung near a window. Light and water are the starting points to match a plant to your spot. Pet safety is from the ASPCA plant list, except string of pearls, which comes from NC State Extension.

| Plant | Light | Water | Cats and dogs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pothos | Bright indirect, tolerates lower light | When the top few cm are dry | Toxic |
| Heartleaf philodendron | Bright indirect, tolerates lower light | When the top few cm are dry | Toxic |
| String of pearls | Bright indirect | Let the mix dry, cactus mix | Mildly toxic |
| String of hearts | Bright indirect | Let it dry out completely | Not on the ASPCA list |
| Hoya | Bright indirect, some gentle sun | Let the mix dry between waterings | Non-toxic |
| Spider plant | Bright indirect | Keep lightly moist | Non-toxic |
| Swedish ivy | Bright indirect | Keep lightly moist | Non-toxic |
| Tradescantia | Bright indirect | When the top few cm are dry | Toxic, skin irritation |
| Burro's tail | Bright, some direct sun | Let the mix dry, cactus mix | Non-toxic |
| English ivy | Bright indirect, cool room | Keep lightly moist | Toxic |

**Pothos** is the easiest place to start. It grows fast, tolerates a dimmer room than most trailers and shows when it is thirsty by drooping slightly. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/golden-pothos) lists it as toxic to cats and dogs because of insoluble calcium oxalates, so hang it well out of reach. See our [pothos care guide](/en/blog/pothos-care-guide).

**Heartleaf philodendron** looks similar, with softer, heart-shaped leaves, and needs much the same care. The ASPCA also lists it as toxic.

**String of pearls** is a succulent with bead-like leaves on thin stems. NC State Extension says it does best indoors in bright, indirect light, in cactus mix or potting soil with sand or perlite, and that soggy soil causes root rot. It notes the leaves and sap are mildly toxic and can irritate skin. See our [string of pearls care guide](/en/blog/string-of-pearls-care-guide).

**String of hearts** has small, marbled heart-shaped leaves on very long, thin stems. NC State recommends bright, indirect light and letting it dry out completely between waterings, with less water during its winter rest. See our [string of hearts care guide](/en/blog/string-of-hearts-care-guide).

**Hoya** has thick, waxy leaves and clusters of scented, star-shaped flowers on mature plants. The ASPCA lists the wax plant as non-toxic. See our [hoya care guide](/en/blog/hoya-care-guide).

**Spider plant** sends out arching stems with baby plants at the tips, which hang down from a basket. It is non-toxic according to the ASPCA. See our [spider plant care guide](/en/blog/spider-plant-care-guide).

**Swedish ivy** is not an ivy at all but a *Plectranthus*, with rounded, scalloped leaves. The ASPCA lists it as non-toxic.

**Tradescantia**, also called inch plant or wandering dude, grows fast and comes in purple, silver and striped forms. The ASPCA lists it as toxic to cats and dogs, mainly causing skin irritation.

**Burro's tail** is a sedum with long, rope-like stems of plump leaves. It needs more light than the others and drops leaves easily if knocked, so hang it where it will not be brushed. The ASPCA lists it as non-toxic.

**English ivy** trails well and suits a cooler, bright room. The ASPCA lists it as toxic. See our [English ivy care guide](/en/blog/english-ivy-care-guide).

## Best trailing plants for outdoor hanging baskets

For summer baskets outside, the [RHS hanging basket guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/container-gardening/hanging-baskets) suggests a mix of flowering and foliage plants. Its trailing picks include:

- **Creeping Jenny** (*Lysimachia nummularia*), with round lime-green or golden leaves.
- ***Dichondra* 'Silver Falls'**, a curtain of small silver leaves.
- **Trailing lobelia** (*Lobelia erinus*), with masses of small blue, white or purple flowers.
- **Petunias**, including trailing types.
- **Pelargoniums**, especially ivy-leaved types.
- **Liquorice plant** (*Helichrysum petiolare*), with soft grey leaves.
- **Verbena** (*Glandularia*).
- **Tumbling tomatoes**, if you want something to eat.

For winter baskets, the RHS suggests ivy, *Carex*, primulas, small cyclamen, violas and winter heathers. For a basket that lasts more than one season, it lists ivy, *Carex*, sage, thyme, *Heuchera*, houseleeks (*Sempervivum*), Mexican fleabane (*Erigeron karvinskianus*) and strawberries.

## How to plant a hanging basket

The [RHS planting guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/container-gardening/how-to-plant-a-hanging-basket) recommends a "thriller, filler and spiller" mix: something showy for the centre, bushier plants to fill out the basket, and trailing plants around the edge to soften the sides.

1. Line the basket. The RHS suggests a layer of moss about 1.5 cm (½ in) thick, or a ready-made liner.
2. Cut planting holes in the liner at least 10 cm (4 in) up from the base if you want to plant through the sides.
3. Fill with peat-free multipurpose compost.
4. Put the upright plants in the middle and the trailing plants around the rim, angled slightly outwards.
5. Water slowly but thoroughly before hanging the basket up.

The RHS advises planting summer baskets from late April but keeping them somewhere frost-free until late May or early June, once the risk of frost has passed.

Indoors, you can skip the liner. Use a hanging pot with drainage and a built-in drip tray, or a plain pot with holes inside a decorative hanging cover. Match the mix to the plant: a cactus mix for string of pearls and burro's tail, ordinary potting mix for pothos and spider plants.

## Watering and feeding hanging baskets

Hanging baskets dry out faster than pots on the ground. They are exposed to wind and sun on every side and hold little compost. The RHS says to check outdoor baskets every day in summer and water unless the compost is still moist. Lifting the basket from underneath is a quick test, because a dry basket is much lighter. Keep the compost moist but not soggy, and aim the water at the compost rather than the leaves and flowers.

Indoors, warm air collects near the ceiling, so a basket hung high often dries faster than the same plant on a shelf. Check it with a finger before watering, and let succulent trailers dry out fully.

For outdoor summer annuals, the RHS recommends a couple of high-potassium liquid feeds during the season to keep them flowering, along with regular deadheading. Plants that will stay in the basket for more than a year benefit from a sprinkle of general granular fertiliser each spring.

## Common hanging basket problems

The RHS says most hanging basket problems come down to watering. It also lists aphids, slugs and snails, vine weevil and powdery mildew as the usual outdoor pests and diseases. Indoors, the most common trouble is the same as in any pot: overwatering succulent trailers, or letting leafy ones dry out completely because the basket is out of sight. Leggy stems with long gaps between leaves mean the plant needs more light. Trim them back to make it bushier, and root the cuttings to fill gaps in the basket.

[Lily](/en) schedules watering per plant and, outdoors, adjusts it for rain and heat, which helps with summer baskets that need water every day in a heatwave and can be skipped after a downpour.
