Rubber plant care comes down to bright light, a thorough watering whenever the top of the soil feels slightly dry, and a room between 16 and 27°C (60 and 80°F) with no cold drafts. Feed every 2 weeks in spring and summer, wipe the leaves when they get dusty, and prune in spring if the plant grows too tall. *Ficus elastica* is one of the easiest large houseplants; most of its problems come from overwatering, low light or sudden cold.

## Rubber plant care at a glance

| Need | What it wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright indirect light; morning sun from an east window is ideal. Tolerates lower light but grows slowly and drops leaves |
| Water | Water thoroughly, then let the soil dry slightly to the touch. Water less from autumn to late winter |
| Humidity | Prefers humid air but tolerates the dry air of most homes |
| Temperature | 16 to 18°C (60 to 65°F) at night, 24 to 27°C (75 to 80°F) by day. Nothing below 13°C (55°F) |
| Soil | A free-draining houseplant mix in a pot with drainage holes |
| Feeding | Water-soluble houseplant fertiliser every 2 weeks in spring and summer |
| Size indoors | Usually 0.6 to 3 m (2 to 10 ft), depending on pot size and pruning |
| Toxicity | The milky sap irritates skin and the stomach. Keep away from pets and young children |

Sources for the values: [Clemson Extension's rubber plant factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/rubber-plant/) and [NC State Extension's *Ficus elastica* page](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ficus-elastica/).

## Light

Clemson says rubber plants prefer bright light but adapt to low light, and grow best with morning light from an east window. NC State recommends bright indirect light or partial shade, with protection from afternoon sun.

In practice that means:

- **Best spot:** within a metre or two of an east window, or near a south or west window behind a sheer curtain.
- **Too little light:** long gaps between leaves, small new leaves, a plant that leans hard toward the window, and lower leaves dropping. Clemson lists too little light among the causes of leaf loss.
- **Too much direct sun:** bleached or scorched patches on leaves that face a hot afternoon window.

Clemson notes that the cultivar 'Robusta', with leaves up to 45 cm (18 in) long, is good in low light. Turn the pot a quarter turn every week or two so the plant grows straight. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge the light at a window.

## Watering

Clemson's advice is to water rubber plants thoroughly, then let the soil dry slightly to the touch before watering again. The pot needs drainage holes, and any water left in the saucer should be emptied after watering. NC State adds: water regularly but avoid overwatering, and reduce watering from autumn to late winter, when the plant is resting.

- **Check before you water.** Push a finger 2 to 3 cm (1 in) into the soil. If it feels dry at the tip, water.
- **Water until it drains.** A small splash wets only the top layer and leaves the lower roots dry.
- **Water less in winter.** A large rubber plant in a cool room can go two weeks or longer between waterings.

Clemson names too much or too little water as one of the main problems, and says yellowing leaves can follow soil that stays too wet. Root rot usually comes from a mix that drains slowly or from watering too often. NC State says overwatering can make the plant drop leaves. Our [overwatering vs underwatering guide](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) helps you tell the two apart, and the [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers what to do if the roots have already gone soft.

## Temperature and humidity

Clemson gives 16 to 18°C (60 to 65°F) at night and 24 to 27°C (75 to 80°F) by day as ideal, and warns against anything below 13°C (55°F), sudden temperature drops and cold drafts. NC State says the rubber plant prefers to stay in one place and does not do well with drafts or cold.

That makes placement in winter important:

- Keep the plant away from doors that open onto the cold, and from single-glazed windows on frosty nights.
- Avoid putting it right next to a radiator or a heating vent, which dries the leaves.
- Once it has settled, avoid moving it around. A sudden change of spot or temperature is a common reason for a sudden drop of several leaves.

Clemson says rubber plants prefer humid conditions but tolerate the dry air common in homes, and lists dry air with low light and cold drafts as causes of leaf loss. NC State suggests medium humidity. If your air is very dry in winter, a humidifier or a group of plants helps; see our guide to [raising humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants).

## Soil and repotting

Clemson recommends a well-drained houseplant mix; NC State suggests a soil-based potting mix. Either way, the mix should drain quickly and the pot must have holes. A general-purpose potting mix with some added perlite or fine bark works for most plants.

Clemson advises repotting in late winter or early spring, when needed. Signs a rubber plant needs a bigger pot:

- Roots circling the inside of the pot or coming out of the drainage holes.
- Soil that dries out within a few days of watering in summer.
- A tall plant that tips over because the pot is too light.

Move up only one pot size, about 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider. A much larger pot holds wet soil around a small root ball. NC State notes the plant can become heavy and is best for large spaces, so a wide, stable pot helps with tall specimens.

## Feeding

Clemson recommends a water-soluble houseplant fertiliser every two weeks during active growth in spring and summer, and less often for plants in lower light. Stop feeding in autumn and winter, when growth slows. Water first, then feed, so the fertiliser does not hit dry roots.

## Pruning and shaping

Indoors, rubber plants tend to grow tall and lanky. Clemson suggests staking them or pruning in spring to rejuvenate the plant. NC State explains the choice: prune the main stems to encourage a bushier habit, or leave them unpruned for a tall, narrow, tree-like form.

How to prune:

1. **Prune in spring**, when the plant is starting to grow again.
2. **Cut just above a leaf or a leaf node** with clean, sharp secateurs. New shoots usually grow from the nodes just below the cut.
3. **Expect sap.** NC State notes that cut leaves and stems drip milky sap. Wear gloves, protect the floor, and dab the cut with a damp paper towel until it stops.
4. **Do not remove more than about a third of the leaves at once**, so the plant keeps enough leaves to recover.

The cut tops do not need to go to waste; see the next section.

## Propagation

Clemson lists stem cuttings, tip cuttings and air layering as ways to propagate a rubber plant, and NC State recommends layering.

- **Tip cuttings.** Take a 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in) piece with a few leaves, let the sap stop, then root it in water or in moist, free-draining mix in a warm, bright spot.
- **Air layering.** Useful for a tall plant you want to shorten. You wound the stem, wrap it in moist sphagnum moss and plastic, and cut below the new roots once they show. It takes several weeks, but the new plant starts out large.

Our [guide to propagating plants from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers both methods in detail.

## Leaf cleaning

The large, glossy leaves catch dust, which reduces the light they receive. Clemson recommends washing the leaves with water when they get dusty; NC State suggests a damp soft cloth or sponge. Support each leaf from below as you wipe. Avoid leaf-shine products.

## Common problems

Clemson says diseases are very rarely a problem indoors. The main troubles are watering, light and insects:

- **Yellow lower leaves.** NC State says it is normal for some bottom leaves to yellow and drop. Several at once, on wet soil, points to overwatering.
- **Sudden leaf drop.** Overwatering, a temperature drop or a cold draft, according to NC State. Clemson adds low light and dry air.
- **Pests.** Clemson names mealybugs; NC State adds scale, spider mites and thrips. Check the undersides of leaves and the leaf joints when you clean them.

Our [rubber plant problems guide](/en/blog/rubber-plant-problems) goes through each symptom and its fixes.

## Toxicity

Clemson warns that the sticky white sap may irritate skin, or the stomach if eaten, and advises keeping rubber plants away from pets and young children that chew plants. The ASPCA has no entry for *Ficus elastica* itself, but lists the related weeping fig, *Ficus benjamina*, as [toxic to dogs and cats](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/indian-rubber-plant).
