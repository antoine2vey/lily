Most Boston fern problems trace back to three things: air that is too dry, soil that dries out, and light that is either too dim or too strong. Nephrolepis exaltata wants bright indirect light, 16 to 21 °C (60 to 70°F), high humidity and soil that never dries out completely, according to [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/nephrolepis-exaltata/). Indoors in winter, with the heating on, it gets none of the first three easily, which is why brown, crispy, shedding ferns are so common.

Find your symptom below. Each section lists the likely causes in order, how to tell them apart and the fix.

## Why is my Boston fern turning brown and crispy?

1. **Dry air.** The most common cause indoors, especially in winter. The tips of the leaflets brown first, then whole fronds go crisp, often on the side facing a radiator or heating vent. The soil may be fine.
2. **The soil dried out.** One complete drying can brown a lot of fronds within days. The pot feels light, the soil has pulled away from the edge of the pot, and browning appears across the whole plant at once.
3. **Too much direct sun.** Brown, scorched patches on fronds facing a sunny window, with paler, washed-out growth around them.
4. **Fertiliser salts.** Brown tips with a white crust on the soil or pot rim after regular feeding. Flush the pot with plenty of water and feed less.
5. **Old fronds.** A few fronds at the base turning brown while new ones keep coming from the centre is normal ageing. The [NYBG plant information service](https://libanswers.nybg.org/faq/222598) recommends removing old, browning fronds as they appear.

Brown fronds do not turn green again. Cut them at the base with clean scissors and fix the cause; new fronds will grow from the crown.

To raise humidity, a humidifier near the fern works best. A bathroom or kitchen with a bright window also helps, as NC State suggests, or a tray of wet pebbles under the pot. Misting is often recommended, but it only wets the fronds for a few minutes. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the methods.

## Why are my Boston fern leaves turning yellow?

1. **Low humidity.** NYBG notes that leaves turn yellow when humidity is too low. Yellowing then browning, starting at the tips and spreading inward, is typical.
2. **Overwatering, especially in winter.** NC State notes the fern is dormant in winter and that overwatering then leads to root rot. Yellow fronds on soil that stays soggy, with a sour smell, point here. Check the roots (see root rot below).
3. **Too much light.** NYBG describes a yellowish-green, bleached-out look as a symptom of too much light. The fern cannot take hot summer sun.
4. **Old fronds.** As with browning, a few yellow fronds at the outside of the plant are normal.
5. **Hungry, root-bound plant.** A fern that has filled its pot with roots dries out fast and turns pale overall. Repot in spring into a pot one size larger.

## Why is my Boston fern shedding leaves?

Tiny leaflets dropping all over the floor are a classic Boston fern problem.

1. **Low light.** NYBG notes that low light makes the fern continually shed leaves. A fern in a dim room will drop leaflets steadily even with perfect watering. Move it to bright, indirect light.
2. **A recent move.** Ferns grown in bright, humid greenhouses often shed when they arrive in a darker, drier home, or when they come inside after a summer outdoors. Some loss for a few weeks is expected while the plant adjusts. Our guide on [how to acclimate new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) explains how to soften the change.
3. **Dry soil.** Leaflets dry and drop after the pot dries out once or twice.

## Why is my Boston fern drooping?

- **Dry soil, light pot:** the fern is thirsty. Water slowly until water runs from the drainage hole, or set the pot in a bowl of water for 20 to 30 minutes if the root ball has shrunk away from the pot and water runs straight through. It should perk up within hours.
- **Wet soil, heavy pot:** the roots may be rotting. Do not water more. Read the next section.

## How do you know if a Boston fern has root rot?

Signs: fronds yellow or wilt even though the soil is wet, the centre of the plant (the crown) feels soft, the soil smells sour, and roots are brown, soft and come apart when pulled instead of being light brown and firm.

1. Take the fern out of its pot and shake off the wet soil.
2. Cut away every soft, dark root and any rotten part of the crown.
3. Replant in fresh, peat-based or coir-based potting mix in a pot with a drainage hole, only as big as the remaining root ball.
4. Water lightly and keep it in bright, indirect light while it recovers. Cut back damaged fronds.

Do not put a layer of gravel or pebbles at the bottom of the pot for drainage. It is common advice, but it raises the waterlogged zone into the root area instead of draining it. Our guide to [root rot recovery](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers the process in more detail.

## What are the brown spots under my Boston fern fronds?

Before treating for pests, look at the pattern.

- **Spores:** small, round, dark brown or black dots arranged in neat rows along the edges of the leaflets on the underside of mature fronds. NC State notes that these are spores and should not be mistaken for an insect problem. They are a sign of a healthy adult fern. Leave them alone.
- **Scale insects:** irregular, oval brown bumps on the stems and anywhere on the fronds, often with sticky residue on the leaflets or the floor below. They scrape off with a fingernail. NC State notes that scale or spider mites can appear when the air is too dry.

## What pests attack Boston ferns?

- **Spider mites:** fine webbing, pale speckled leaflets, fronds that dull and dry. They multiply in warm, dry air. Rinse the whole fern in a lukewarm shower, raise humidity, and repeat weekly. Our guide to [spider mite treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) has the full routine.
- **Scale:** scrape off what you can, cut off badly infested fronds, and check weekly.
- **Mealybugs:** white cottony clusters at the base of the fronds. Remove them with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol.
- **Fungus gnats:** small black flies from soil that stays wet. Let the surface dry slightly between waterings.

If you use insecticidal soap or horticultural oil, check that the label lists ferns, and spray one frond first, waiting a few days to see whether it marks the leaflets. Basil or mint placed next to the fern probably will not keep pests away. There is no good evidence that companion planting protects houseplants.

## Why is my Boston fern growing long, plain fronds?

Occasionally a fern sends up a long frond with fewer, less divided leaflets. NYBG recommends cutting these out as soon as they appear so they do not take over the plant.

Long, thin, leafless strings hanging over the pot are different: those are runners, which the plant uses to spread. They are normal. You can cut them off or pin their tips to a small pot of soil to root new plants.

## How do you prevent Boston fern problems?

- Bright, indirect light, never hot direct sun.
- 16 to 21 °C (60 to 70°F), away from heating vents and cold draughts; NYBG gives 10 °C (50°F) as the minimum.
- Soil that stays evenly moist but not soggy; NC State says it should never be allowed to dry out. Water less in winter.
- High humidity, from a humidifier, a bright bathroom or a pebble tray.
- Repot in spring when roots fill the pot.

## Quick answers

**Is a Boston fern safe for cats and dogs?** Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/boston-fern) lists it as non-toxic to both.
