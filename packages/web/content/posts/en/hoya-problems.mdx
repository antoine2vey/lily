Most hoya problems come from watering. Yellow leaves and leaf drop usually mean the soil stays wet too long, though a hoya left bone dry for weeks drops leaves too. Soft, wrinkled leaves mean the plant cannot take up water, either because the soil is dry or because the roots have rotted. Below, each symptom has its likely causes in order, how to tell them apart, and the fix.

## Hoya problems: check the roots and soil first

Hoyas are epiphytes. In the wild, many grow on trees, with their roots in bark and leaf litter where air reaches them. Indoors, [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/hoya-carnosa/) advises a loose, fast-draining mix, a pot with good drainage, and letting the soil dry between waterings. When a hoya struggles, start with two checks: how wet the mix is at 5 cm (2 in) down, and whether the pot has a drainage hole and an empty saucer. Most cases below come back to one of those.

## Why are my hoya leaves turning yellow?

**1. The mix stays wet.** This is the most common cause. Leaves turn yellow and soft, often starting low on the vine, and the mix is still damp a week after watering. Dense potting soil, a pot without a hole, water left in the saucer or watering on a fixed schedule are the usual reasons. Let the mix dry almost completely before watering again, and if it stays wet for more than ten days, repot into a chunkier mix (see the root rot section below).

**2. Long dry spells.** NC State notes that both too wet and too dry soil can make the leaves drop. A hoya that goes weeks without water uses up the reserves in its leaves; the oldest ones yellow, wrinkle and fall. The mix will be dry right through and pulling away from the pot.

**3. Too much direct sun.** Pale yellow or bleached patches on the leaves facing a window mean more sun than the leaf can take. NC State recommends bright indirect light year-round. Move it back from the glass or filter the light with a sheer curtain.

**4. Old leaves dying off.** One leaf yellowing now and then, low on an old vine, is normal. Remove it and move on.

**5. Cold.** Leaves yellowing or blackening after a cold snap, or on the side touching a window pane, point to cold damage. NC State advises bringing hoyas indoors when nights fall below 10°C (50°F).

## Why is my hoya dropping leaves?

Leaf drop has the same causes as yellowing, and NC State names the main one directly: soil that is too wet or too dry. Feel the mix. If it is wet and the leaves are soft, treat it as root trouble. If it is dry through and the leaves are limp and wrinkled before they fall, water more consistently.

Two other causes are worth ruling out:

- **Cold water or cold air.** NC State warns that cold water can shock a hoya and recommends watering at room temperature. A sudden draught from a door or an air-conditioning vent can also bring down leaves.
- **A big change of place.** A hoya moved to a much darker or brighter spot, or just brought home, may shed a few leaves while it adjusts. Keep conditions steady and do not overcorrect with extra water.

## Why are my hoya leaves wrinkled, soft or drooping?

Hoya leaves are thick and store water. When they wrinkle or go limp, the plant is short of water inside the leaf, which has two opposite causes.

- **The mix is dry.** The pot feels light, the mix is dry at 5 cm (2 in), and stems are firm. Soak the pot until water drains through. The leaves should firm up over a few days. Wrinkles on old leaves may never fully smooth out.
- **The roots have rotted.** The mix is damp, the leaves are soft anyway, and stems near the soil may be dark. Rotten roots cannot absorb water, so the plant shows thirst while sitting in wet mix. Do not water. Check the roots.

## How do I know if my hoya has root rot?

Slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy hoya roots are firm and white to light brown. Rotten roots are dark brown or black, mushy, and the outer layer slips off when pinched. The mix often smells sour.

1. Remove the old mix and cut off every soft, dark root with clean scissors.
2. Cut off any stems that are black and soft at the base.
3. Repot in a fresh, loose mix: for example, equal parts potting mix, orchid bark and perlite. Use a pot with drainage that fits the remaining roots. NC State notes hoyas do not mind being pot-bound and advises a new pot no more than 5 cm (2 in) larger.
4. Wait a few days before watering, then water only when the mix is dry.

If almost no healthy roots remain, take cuttings from firm stems and root them instead. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers severe cases, and our guide to [plant propagation from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) explains how to start over.

## Why are there brown or black spots on my hoya leaves?

**1. Sunburn.** Dry, brown, sunken patches on the leaves nearest a sunny window. They will not heal. Move the plant and leave the marked leaves until new growth replaces them.

**2. Cold damage.** Dark, water-soaked areas that turn black after a cold night or contact with a cold window.

**3. Rot spreading from the stem.** Black, soft patches that start where the leaf joins the stem, on a plant in wet mix. Cut back to healthy tissue and treat as root rot.

**4. Water droplets left on leaves in cool conditions.** These can lead to small leaf spots, probably fungal. Water the soil, not the foliage, and give the plant some air movement.

## Why won't my hoya flower?

**1. It is too young.** NC State says most hoyas do not flower until they are 2 or 3 years old. A plant grown from a recent cutting needs time.

**2. Too little light.** A hoya in a dim spot grows leaves but rarely sets buds. Bright indirect light, close to an east window or set back from a south or west one, is the usual fix.

**3. The flower spurs were cut off.** Hoyas flower from short stalks called peduncles or spurs. NC State advises leaving them on the plant, because each one flowers again over several years. Cutting them removes future flowers.

**4. It was just repotted or moved while in bud.** Buds often drop after a disruption. NC State advises repotting only when the plant is not in bud or flower.

If a hoya flowers and leaves sticky drops below, that is nectar from the flowers, which NC State notes can be sticky. Sticky leaves without flowers nearby point to pests instead.

## What pests attack hoyas?

NC State names three:

- **Mealybugs.** The most common. White, cottony clumps on stems, under leaves and in the flower spurs. Remove them with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then spray with insecticidal soap every 7 to 10 days until none return.
- **Fungus gnats.** Small dark flies around the pot, attracted to mixes that stay too wet. Their presence is itself a sign of overwatering. Let the mix dry out and catch adults on yellow sticky traps.
- **Spider mites.** Fine pale speckling and thin webbing under the leaves, which NC State links to air that is too dry. Rinse the plant and see our guide to [spider mite treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment).

Keep an infested plant away from your others until it is clean. Our article on [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers identification in more depth.

## Quick answers

**Is a hoya toxic to cats and dogs?** No. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/wax-plant) lists the wax plant, *Hoya carnosa*, as non-toxic to both.

**Should I remove yellow hoya leaves?** Yes, once they are fully yellow. They will not turn green again. Leave the stem and any flower spurs in place.

**How do I stop these problems coming back?** Use a loose mix and a pot with drainage, water only when the mix has dried, use room-temperature water, and keep the plant in bright indirect light. The full routine is in our [hoya care guide](/en/blog/hoya-care-guide).
