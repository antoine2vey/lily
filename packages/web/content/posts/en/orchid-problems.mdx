Most orchid problems on a moth orchid (*Phalaenopsis*) come from water in the wrong place: roots kept wet in old, broken-down bark rot, and water left standing in the centre of the plant rots the crown. The American Orchid Society's guide to [what can go wrong with Phalaenopsis](https://www.aos.org/orchids/articles/growing-phalaenopsis.-what-can-go-wrong) puts these two first, followed by sunburn, cold damage, and three pests: mealybugs, scale and mites. Find your symptom below, then check the causes in the order given.

## Orchid problems: check the roots first

Before you treat a leaf symptom, slide the plant out of its pot and look at the roots. Firm, silvery or green roots rule out the most serious cause. Brown, soft or hollow roots point to rot, whatever the leaves look like.

## Why are my orchid leaves turning yellow?

**1. An old leaf is being shed.** A moth orchid grows new leaves from the centre and drops old ones from the bottom. If only the lowest leaf yellows slowly, while the rest are firm and green and the roots look healthy, this is normal. Let it fall off on its own or pull it away once it has dried.

**2. The roots are rotting.** Several leaves yellow, often quickly, starting from the bottom, and the plant may wobble in its pot. This is the most common serious cause. Check the roots as described below.

**3. Sunburn.** A yellowish or whitish patch appears on the part of the leaf facing the light, later turning tan or black. The AOS explains that this happens when leaves overheat, often after a sudden increase in light, such as the change of season or a move to a sunnier window. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/phalaenopsis/) recommends low light, with an east-facing windowsill as the best spot. Move the plant out of direct midday sun. Burned patches do not recover, but the AOS only suggests removing badly damaged leaves.

**4. Crown rot.** The youngest leaf in the centre yellows or blackens and pulls out easily. See the crown rot section below.

## Why are my orchid leaves wrinkled, limp or floppy?

Healthy moth orchid leaves are thick and firm. The AOS describes limp, wrinkled leaves as a sign that the plant is not getting enough water. NC State says the same thing about shrivelled leaves, and notes it can come from a dry potting medium or from rotted roots.

The two causes need opposite fixes, so check before you act. Slide the plant out of its pot.

- **Firm, silvery or green roots in dry, loose bark:** the plant is simply thirsty. Water more often. NC State suggests watering about once a week, or more often in summer heat.
- **Brown, mushy or hollow roots in damp, crumbly medium:** the roots have rotted, so they cannot take up water. Watering more makes it worse. Go to the next section.

Leaves that wrinkled for a long time stay a little creased even after the plant recovers. New leaves will come out smooth.

## How do you know if an orchid has root rot?

The AOS gives two causes of root rot: watering too often, and potting mix that has broken down. Orchid bark decomposes over a year or two into a dense, soggy mass that holds water against the roots. NC State recommends repotting about every 2 years for that reason.

Healthy roots are firm. They are silvery-white when dry and green when wet. Rotted roots are brown or black, soft, and often hollow: the outer layer slides off when pinched and leaves a thin thread behind.

To treat it:

1. Take the plant out of its pot and wash off all of the old medium.
2. Cut off every soft, brown or hollow root with clean scissors. Leave firm roots, including the aerial ones that grow outside the pot.
3. Repot into fresh orchid bark, in a pot just big enough for the remaining roots, with good drainage. NC State notes that sphagnum moss is hard to rewet once dry, so bark is easier to manage.
4. As the AOS advises, water lightly until the plant has re-established.

If only a few firm roots remain, the plant may still recover, but slowly. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) explains the general approach. Our article on [how often to water an orchid](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-orchid) covers how to avoid a repeat.

## Why is the centre of my orchid rotting?

That is crown rot. The AOS explains that water left standing in the crown, the growing point where new leaves emerge, lets bacteria multiply and rot the growing tip. The newest leaf turns yellow, then brown or black and soft, and pulls out with little effort.

To prevent it, water the bark rather than pouring over the leaves, and water in the morning so any splash dries by evening. If water does land in the crown, tilt the plant to drain it or dab it dry with a paper towel. The AOS also suggests a few drops of hydrogen peroxide in the crown to kill bacteria.

Once the growing tip has rotted, it will not produce new leaves. If the plant has enough energy, it may grow a new plantlet from the base below the damaged tissue. Keep it, water carefully, and wait.

## Why are there spots on my orchid leaves or flowers?

- **Small black or light brown spots on the flowers:** Botrytis, a fungal infection that NC State and the AOS both describe. It spreads in cool, humid, still air. Remove spotted flowers, since they are a source of new infection, and improve air movement.
- **Water-filled blisters on the leaves, later turning brown:** oedema. The plant absorbed more water than its leaves could lose. The AOS links it to watering late in the day when nights are cool. Water in the morning.
- **Soft, watery, spreading patches that smell bad:** bacterial soft rot. The AOS warns it can rot a whole plant in two to three days. Cut out the infected tissue straight away, with about 2.5 cm (1 in) of healthy tissue around it, using a sterilised blade, and keep the plant dry and isolated.
- **Sunken, pitted or discoloured patches after a cold spell:** cold damage, which the AOS links to low temperatures and to watering with cold water or ice cubes. Use water at room temperature.

## Why are my orchid buds falling off?

Buds that turn yellow and drop before opening is called bud blast. NC State attributes it to changes in temperature, humidity, moisture or location. The usual triggers are carrying the plant home on a cold day, moving it once the buds have formed, a draught from a door or window, a heating vent blowing on it, or letting the bark dry out completely while it is in bud.

Choose a spot before the buds form and leave the plant there. Keep watering steady while it is in bud and in flower.

## Why won't my orchid rebloom?

A moth orchid usually needs a cooler period to start a new flower spike. NC State recommends nights around 13°C (55°F) for about a week in autumn. The AOS advises that the autumn chill should not go below about 10 to 13°C (50 to 55°F). A room with the heating turned down, or a spot near a cool window that does not freeze, is usually enough.

The other causes are too little light, a plant that has not been fed, and a plant still recovering from root loss. NC State recommends feeding at half strength twice a month, or at quarter strength weekly. Our [orchid care guide](/en/blog/orchid-care-guide) covers the full routine.

When the flowers have fallen, NC State suggests cutting the spike above the first node below the faded flowers. A secondary spike sometimes grows from that node.

## What pests attack orchids?

The AOS names mealybugs, scale and mites as the most common:

- **Mealybugs** look like white cottony masses. They hide in crevices, under leaves, in flower buds and even on the roots.
- **Scale** forms small yellowish to dark brown shells on leaves, flower spikes and sometimes roots. It often leaves sticky honeydew.
- **Mites** are tiny. The signs are fine pale stippling on the leaves or a pitted, silvery surface.

For a few mealybugs or scale, the AOS suggests removing them with a cotton swab dipped in isopropyl (rubbing) alcohol. For heavier infestations, use repeated applications of a contact pesticide such as insecticidal soap, following the label. In warm weather mites can produce a new generation in about six days, so repeat any treatment several times. Isolate the plant until it is clear. Our [houseplant pests guide](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers identification in more detail.

The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/phalaenopsis-orchid) lists moth orchids as non-toxic to dogs, cats and horses.
