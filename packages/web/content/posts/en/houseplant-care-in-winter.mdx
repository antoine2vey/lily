Houseplant care in winter comes down to doing less: water when the soil is dry rather than on your summer schedule, which often means half as often or less, and stop fertilising until spring. At the same time, give plants more light by moving them closer to the window, and keep them about 30 cm (1 ft) from radiators and cold glass. Most winter losses come from overwatering a plant that has slowed down.

## Why houseplants slow down in winter

Indoor plants don't hibernate the way a tree drops its leaves, but most of them slow down sharply from late autumn to early spring. Days are shorter and the sun is lower, so a spot that was bright in June can get a fraction of that light in December. With less light, a plant makes less sugar, grows less and pulls far less water from its pot.

That slowdown is often called dormancy. Some plants go further:

- **Alocasias and caladiums** may drop their leaves entirely and rest as an underground corm or tuber. The [University of Vermont Extension](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/houseplant-care-during-winter-months) notes this is normal. Our [alocasia care guide](/en/blog/alocasia-care-guide) covers what to do during that rest.
- **Cacti and many succulents** need a cool, dry rest to flower the next year.
- **Most tropical foliage plants** (pothos, philodendron, monstera, peace lily) just grow slowly or stop.

A plant that stops putting out new leaves in winter is not sick. A plant whose soil stays wet for two weeks is at risk.

## Autumn: getting plants ready for winter

The changes start in autumn, before the heating comes on.

- **Bring outdoor plants in early.** [University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/moving-indoor-plants-outside) advises bringing plants indoors when night temperatures drop below 16°C (60°F), often in mid-September. Check them for aphids, spider mites and scale first, and treat with insecticidal soap or horticultural oil before they come in.
- **Stop fertilising** by early autumn so the plant isn't pushed into soft growth it cannot support.
- **Clean the windows** and dust the leaves. Both let more of the weak winter light reach the plant.
- **Finish repotting.** Leave anything that needs a bigger pot until spring.

## How often should you water houseplants in winter?

Water only when the soil is dry to the depth your plant needs, not on a fixed day. UVM recommends watering only when the mix is truly dry, and testing with a wooden chopstick or a moisture meter rather than guessing.

As a starting point:

- **Tropical foliage plants:** water when the top 5 cm (2 in) is dry. Expect every 10 to 21 days instead of every week.
- **Succulents and cacti:** once a month or less. Many cacti need almost none from November to February.
- **Plants that like to stay moist,** such as ferns and peace lilies: check weekly, water when the surface just starts to dry.

Use room-temperature water, water thoroughly until it drains, then empty the saucer. Watering less often is right, but giving small sips every few days is not: the bottom of the pot stays wet while the top looks dry.

If you are unsure whether a plant is suffering from too much or too little water, our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) compares the signs side by side. [Lily](/en) adjusts each plant's indoor watering schedule for the season's daylight, so intervals stretch automatically as the days shorten.

## Light: move plants closer to the window

UVM notes that winter light is both shorter and weaker, so plants need help using it.

- Move plants closer to the brightest window, within about 1 m (3 ft) of the glass.
- Plants that needed shade from a south-facing window in summer can often sit right in it in winter.
- Wipe dust off broad leaves with a damp cloth.
- Turn pots a quarter turn every week so the whole plant gets light.
- If a plant still stretches and pales, a grow light on a timer for 10 to 12 hours a day fills the gap.

Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge how much light a window gives.

## Temperature: avoid draughts and heat sources

Most tropical houseplants are comfortable between 16 and 24°C (60 and 75°F) and suffer below 10°C (50°F). Two places cause most winter damage:

- **Cold glass and draughts.** Leaves touching a window on a frosty night, or a plant near a door that opens to the outside, can be marked by cold within hours. Pull plants back from the glass at night, and never trap them between a closed curtain and the window.
- **Heat sources.** Radiators, heating vents and wood stoves dry leaves and soil fast. UVM advises keeping plants away from them and keeping temperatures steady.

Keep about 30 cm (1 ft) between any plant and a radiator or cold pane.

## Humidity: fight the dry air from heating

Heating dries indoor air, often well below 40% relative humidity. Crispy brown leaf tips and spider mites are the usual results. UVM suggests a humidifier, grouping plants together, or pebble trays.

A humidifier is the only option that raises humidity by a measurable amount. Grouping plants helps a little. Misting does not, because the water evaporates in minutes. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the methods.

## Pests to watch for in winter

- **Spider mites** love warm, dry air. Look for fine speckling and webbing under leaves. UVM links them to low humidity.
- **Fungus gnats** breed in soil that stays wet, so they are a sign of overwatering. Let the top of the soil dry and use yellow sticky traps to catch adults.
- **Mealybugs, scale and aphids** can arrive on plants brought in from outside.

Check leaf undersides each time you water. UVM also suggests a lukewarm shower to clean plants and knock pests off.

## Houseplant care in winter: a checklist

- Water only when the soil is dry to the right depth for the plant.
- Stop fertilising until new growth appears in spring.
- Don't repot unless roots are rotting.
- Move plants closer to the light and clean the leaves.
- Keep them 30 cm (1 ft) from radiators and cold glass.
- Raise humidity with a humidifier.
- Inspect for spider mites and fungus gnats weekly.

When days lengthen in March and new leaves appear, it is time to restart feeding. Plants that have outgrown their pots can move up a size then, as the [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) explains.
