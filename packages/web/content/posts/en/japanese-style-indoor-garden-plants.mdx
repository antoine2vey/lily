The best Japanese style indoor garden plants are the ones that keep the look and still grow indoors: lady palm, Japanese aralia (fatsia), cast iron plant, lucky bamboo, ginseng ficus and sago palm. Four of the six cope with shade or indirect light, and only the ginseng ficus needs warmth all year, above 20 °C (68°F) according to [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ficus-microcarpa/). Two of them, lucky bamboo and sago palm, are toxic to pets, and the sago palm seriously so.

The classic plants of a Japanese garden, such as Japanese maples, flowering cherries, azaleas and true bamboo, are outdoor plants. They need cold winters, full outdoor light or both, and they decline in a living room. Indoors, you get the same feel from a few plants with strong shapes, simple pots, stone and moss.

## Japanese style indoor garden plants at a glance

| Plant | Light | Water | Pets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lady palm (*Rhapis excelsa*) | Indirect light, tolerates shade | Regular, less in winter | Non-toxic |
| Japanese aralia (*Fatsia japonica*) | Part to full shade | Moist, never soggy | Non-toxic |
| Cast iron plant (*Aspidistra elatior*) | Low light, dense shade | Sparingly, especially in winter | Non-toxic |
| Lucky bamboo (*Dracaena sanderiana*) | Bright indirect light | Grown in water, changed weekly | Toxic |
| Ginseng ficus (*Ficus microcarpa* 'Ginseng') | Bright light, some direct sun | Moist, well drained | Treat as toxic |
| Sago palm (*Cycas revoluta*) | 4 to 6 hours of filtered sun | Moist, never overwatered | Highly toxic |

Light, water and toxicity details below come from each plant's NC State Extension page unless another source is named.

## Lady palm

The lady palm is a clumping palm with fan-shaped leaves on thin, upright canes, which gives it the look of a small bamboo grove. NC State describes it as native to the understory of wet forests in southern China and Vietnam and calls it an easy-to-grow houseplant that needs indirect light.

- **Light.** Indirect light. It tolerates partial shade, so a spot a few metres from a window works.
- **Water.** Regular watering in well-drained potting soil, reduced in winter.
- **Temperature.** NC State says potted plants must come inside before temperatures drop below 10 °C (50°F).
- **Humidity.** Low humidity needs, which makes it easier than most palms in a heated home.

It grows slowly, so buy the size you want. NC State lists mites, mealybugs and scale as its pests, and it propagates by dividing the clump. It is non-toxic to cats and dogs.

## Japanese aralia (fatsia)

Fatsia has large, glossy, deeply lobed leaves and is native to Japan and Korea, according to NC State. It is mostly grown outdoors in mild climates, but NC State notes it can also be grown as a houseplant.

- **Light.** Part to full shade. NC State warns that the leaves turn brown in full sun.
- **Water.** Moist, well-drained soil. Too much moisture causes root rot, so let the surface dry slightly between waterings.
- **Pests.** Indoors, watch for aphids, mealybugs, spider mites, scale and thrips.

A single fatsia in a plain, low pot fills a corner the way a shrub would outside. It is non-toxic to cats and dogs.

## Cast iron plant

The cast iron plant grows straight, dark green leaves from the soil, with no stem. Placed against a plain wall, it looks like the clumps of foliage used under trees in a Japanese garden. NC State says it tolerates dense shade, which makes it one of the few plants for a hallway or a north-facing room.

- **Light.** Partial to deep shade. NC State says it does not tolerate direct sun.
- **Water.** Regularly from spring to autumn and sparingly in winter.

It grows slowly. The ASPCA lists the [cast iron plant as non-toxic](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/cast-iron-plant) to cats and dogs.

## Lucky bamboo

Lucky bamboo is not a bamboo. NC State explains that it is a dracaena from West Africa, sold as straight, spiralled or woven stems standing in water.

- **Light.** Bright but indirect light all year.
- **Water.** If you grow it in water, use chlorine-free water and change it every week. NC State suggests pebbles at the bottom of the container, which give the roots room and keep the stems from tipping over.
- **Pets.** NC State lists it as toxic to cats and dogs, with low severity. Keep it out of reach.

A group of stems in a shallow dish of pebbles gives you vertical lines without the size of real bamboo.

## Ginseng ficus

The ginseng ficus is a cultivar of *Ficus microcarpa* with a thick, swollen root base exposed above the soil and dense, glossy leaves. NC State notes that the species is native to tropical and subtropical Asia and frequently used as an indoor tree and for bonsai, and that 'Ginseng' is the form sold for bonsai. It is the easiest way to have a bonsai indoors.

- **Light.** Full sun to partial shade. Indoors, give it the brightest window you have.
- **Water.** Moist but well-drained soil. NC State warns that overwatering causes root rot, so water when the top of the soil has dried.
- **Temperature.** Above 20 °C (68°F) all year, which is why it is sold as a houseplant.
- **Humidity.** NC State says high humidity is preferable and seems to encourage aerial roots.

Pests include aphids, spider mites, mealybugs and scale. NC State does not list its toxicity, but the ASPCA lists the weeping fig, another ficus, as toxic to cats and dogs, so keep it away from pets as a precaution.

## Sago palm

The sago palm has stiff, feather-like leaves in a rosette on a short trunk. It is not a true palm but a cycad, native to Kyushu in southern Japan and to southern China according to NC State, and it is often grown as a bonsai.

- **Light.** NC State recommends 4 to 6 hours of filtered sun indoors and warns that harsh sun can damage the leaves. A bright window with a sheer curtain works.
- **Water.** Moist, well-drained soil. NC State notes it does not tolerate overwatering or poor drainage, and it grows very slowly, so it uses little water.

**Pets and children.** NC State says every part of the sago palm contains the toxin cycasin, with the highest concentration in the seeds, and that eating any part can cause permanent internal damage or death. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/sago-palm) lists it as toxic to cats and dogs, with liver failure among the signs. Do not grow it in a home with a dog, cat or small child who could chew it.

## Moss and kokedama

Moss is what makes a display look Japanese, and it is the hardest part to keep alive indoors because most mosses need constant moisture and cool air. Use preserved moss to top the soil of a pot, or keep living moss in a closed glass terrarium where the air stays damp.

Kokedama are plants grown in a ball of soil wrapped in moss and string instead of a pot. Water one by soaking the ball in water until it is heavy, then let it drain before you hang it or put it back on its dish. Pick a plant that likes even moisture and indirect light, such as a fern or a small fatsia, rather than a succulent.

## How do you arrange a Japanese-style indoor garden?

- **Use few plants.** Two or three plants with different shapes, such as an upright lady palm, a broad fatsia and a low bonsai, read better than a crowded shelf.
- **Leave empty space.** Space around a plant is part of the arrangement.
- **Choose plain containers.** Low, unglazed or dark glazed pots in stone, clay or dark grey suit the look. Make sure each pot has a drainage hole.
- **Add stone and gravel.** River stones, a flat rock or a layer of grey gravel over the soil give the look of a dry garden. Lift the gravel when you check the soil.
- **Match plants to the light.** Group the shade plants (cast iron plant, fatsia, lady palm) away from the window and the light lovers (ginseng ficus, sago palm) close to it.

## Which plant should you start with?

Start with the cast iron plant or the lady palm. Both cope with less light, need little care and are safe for pets, so they make a forgiving base. Add a ginseng ficus once you have a bright window, and keep the sago palm for a home without pets or small children.
