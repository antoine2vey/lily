Most string of pearls problems come down to water: [Wisconsin Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/string-of-pearls-senecio-rowleyanus/) names root rot from overwatering as the most common reason the plant dies. Shrivelled pearls can mean either too little water or roots that have already rotted, so always check the soil and roots before you water. The other usual causes are too little light (small, widely spaced pearls), direct sun through glass (brown scorch) and a few sap-sucking pests.

## String of pearls problems: check the mix and the pot first

Before you treat any symptom, check two things: how wet the mix is a few centimetres down, and whether the pot has a drainage hole with nothing standing under it. Since overwatering is the most common fatal mistake with this plant, those two checks rule the biggest cause in or out.

## Why is my string of pearls shrivelling?

Wrinkled, deflated pearls mean the plant is short of water inside its leaves. That has two causes with opposite fixes, so check first.

**1. The soil has been dry too long.** This is the usual cause, and the easy one. The mix is dry right through, the pot feels light, and the stems are still firm and green. Wisconsin Extension says to water when the leaves start to look a little shrivelled, so slight wrinkling is your signal, not an emergency. Water thoroughly until water runs out of the drainage hole. The pearls should plump up within a day or two.

**2. The roots have rotted.** The soil is damp, yet the pearls are shrivelled. Roots that have rotted cannot take up water, so the plant dehydrates in wet soil. Watering more makes it worse. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/curio-rowleyanus/) notes that wilting can come from both underwatering and overwatering. Check the roots (see below).

The two sources differ slightly on how dry to let it get. Wisconsin advises letting the mix dry out completely between waterings. NC State warns against letting the plant dry out completely. In practice, let the mix dry nearly all the way through, then water at the first sign of wrinkling, rather than leaving it bone dry for weeks.

## Why are my string of pearls going mushy or yellow?

Soft, translucent or yellow pearls that burst when squeezed are a sign of too much water. They usually appear near the soil first, where the stems sit on damp mix. The causes, in order of frequency:

**1. Watering too often.** Water every week on a schedule in a cool, dim room, and the mix never dries. Stretch the gap and water only when the mix is dry and the first pearls show slight wrinkling.

**2. Soil and pot that hold water.** Ordinary potting compost, a deep pot, a pot without a drainage hole, or a cachepot that collects water all keep the roots wet. Wisconsin recommends a very free-draining mix, such as cactus mix with up to an equal amount of grit, sharp sand or pumice, in a shallow pot. It also notes clay pots dry faster than plastic or glazed ceramic. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) covers succulent mixes.

**3. Winter.** Growth slows in winter, and Wisconsin recommends a cool rest at 13 to 16°C (55 to 60°F) with less water than in summer. The same watering rhythm that worked in July can rot the plant in December.

Remove mushy pearls and stems, let the mix dry right out, and check the roots if more than a few strands are affected.

## How do you know if a string of pearls has root rot?

Signs include mushy pearls near the soil, stems that turn brown or black at the base, a plant that shrivels in wet soil, and strands that come away from the soil when lifted. NC State warns that soggy soil leads to root rot and the plant's death.

Tip the plant out. String of pearls has a small, shallow root system of thin roots. Healthy ones are pale and firm. Rotted ones are brown, soft and break off easily.

With this plant, rescuing the cuttings is often easier than saving the roots. Take several healthy stem tips, 8 to 10 cm (3 to 4 in) long, as Wisconsin describes, and strip the pearls from the bottom few nodes. Lay them on the surface of a pot of slightly moist cactus mix, and cover the bare nodes lightly. Roots form at the nodes. Mist the surface rather than watering until they take hold. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers the general method, and our guide to [propagating from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) has more detail.

## Why is my string of pearls bare or dying at the top?

A plant with long, healthy strands hanging over the side but a thin, bare patch on top has three likely causes:

**1. Rot at the crown.** The stems that sit on the soil surface are the first to rot when the mix stays wet. If the base of the strands is brown and soft, treat it as overwatering.

**2. Too little light on top.** In a hanging pot high up near a window, the strands that hang down get light, while the top of the pot sits in shade. Hang it lower or move it closer to the glass.

**3. Age.** NC State notes that these plants tend to die back after several years and recommends taking cuttings rather than trying to revive an old plant. Lay a few cuttings on the bare soil and press them in lightly. They root where they touch and fill the top in again.

## Why are my string of pearls small and far apart?

Long stems with small pearls spaced far apart mean the plant is stretching for light. Wisconsin recommends bright light. NC State recommends bright, indirect light indoors. In practice, that means right by a bright window, but not in strong afternoon sun through the glass.

Move the plant closer to the light. New growth should come in denser. The stretched stems will not tighten up, so trim them back and replant the tips. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot.

The plant's natural habitat explains this. It is not an open desert plant. Wisconsin describes it as native to dry parts of the Eastern Cape of South Africa, where it often grows under bushes or between rocks that shade it from intense sun. It wants a lot of light, but not baking sun.

## Why are my string of pearls turning brown?

**Brown, dry or bleached pearls on the side facing the window** are sun scorch. NC State notes that direct sunlight scorches the leaves. Plants moved outside in summer, or put in a south-facing window after a dim winter, are most at risk. Wisconsin says outdoor plants need to be gradually acclimatised to prevent sunburn. Move the plant out of direct midday and afternoon sun.

**Brown, mushy pearls at the base** are rot. See the overwatering section above.

**A few dry pearls on old stems** while the tips grow normally are just old leaves. Pick them off.

## What pests attack string of pearls?

Wisconsin names mealybugs and aphids. NC State adds whiteflies, fungus gnats and ants, and notes that yellowing or greying leaves can be a sign of insects.

- **Mealybugs:** white, cottony tufts between the pearls and along stems. Wipe them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol.
- **Aphids:** small green or black insects clustered on the stem tips. Rinse them off or use insecticidal soap.
- **Fungus gnats:** small black flies around the pot. A sign that the mix stays too wet. Let it dry out more.

Isolate an infested plant and repeat the treatment as the label says, because one round does not catch the eggs. Badly infested strands are easier to cut off. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers each one.

## Is string of pearls toxic to pets?

NC State lists it as slightly toxic to people, cats, dogs and horses. Eating the leaves can cause vomiting and diarrhoea, and the sap can irritate skin. Hang it out of reach of pets that chew plants.
