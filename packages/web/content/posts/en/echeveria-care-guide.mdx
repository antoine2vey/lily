Echeveria care is mostly about two things: the brightest spot you have, ideally with several hours of direct sun, and soil that dries out completely between waterings. Keep it above 10°C (50°F) in winter, water it far less from autumn to early spring, and plant it in a gritty cactus mix in a pot with a drainage hole. Nearly every echeveria that dies indoors dies from too little light, too much water, or both.

## Echeveria care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Full sun to partial sun: at least 4 to 6 hours of direct light, or the brightest window you have |
| Water | Soak the pot when the mix is dry all the way down, then wait until it is dry again |
| Humidity | Normal room air. No misting, no pebble tray |
| Temperature | Normal room temperature in the growing season; above 10°C (50°F) in winter |
| Soil | Gritty cactus mix with extra grit or pumice, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Diluted fertiliser once a month in spring and summer, none in winter |
| Toxicity | Non-toxic to cats and dogs |

Echeverias are rosette-forming succulents native to an area stretching from southwest Texas to northwest Argentina, according to [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/echeveria/). They store water in their thick leaves, which is why they handle drought far better than wet soil.

## How much sun does an echeveria need?

More than most people expect. NC State notes that echeveria "doesn't mind being in full sun" and that leaf colour improves with sunlight. Outdoors in summer, a spot with morning sun and some shade in the hottest part of the afternoon suits most varieties. Indoors, put it right against a south- or west-facing window. An east-facing window works if it is unobstructed. A north-facing window is rarely enough on its own.

Older articles often describe echeverias as plants for "bright, indirect light". That is the minimum, not the goal. In indirect light the plant stays alive but slowly loses its compact shape.

The clearest sign of too little light is stretching, called etiolation. The rosette opens up, the gaps between leaves grow, the stem gets taller, and the whole plant leans toward the window. Pink, red and purple varieties also fade to green. Stretched growth does not tighten up again. Move the plant to more light, and if the shape bothers you, cut the rosette off with a short piece of stem, let it dry, and re-root it (see propagation below).

Too much sudden sun causes brown or white scorched patches on the leaves that face the light. This usually happens when a plant moves from indoors to a balcony in one go. Introduce it to direct sun over a week or two, starting with a couple of hours of morning sun. If your windows get little light, a grow light run for about 12 hours a day, at the distance its maker recommends, keeps an echeveria compact through winter.

## How often should you water an echeveria?

Water when the mix is completely dry, not just the surface. Push a wooden skewer to the bottom of the pot: if it comes out clean and dry, it is time. Then water thoroughly until water runs out of the drainage hole, and empty the saucer. This "soak and dry" rhythm suits the plant far better than small, frequent sips.

In a sunny spot in summer, that often means every 7 to 14 days in a small pot. In winter, the RHS advises keeping echeverias cool and dry, with only a little water every few weeks to stop the leaves shrivelling. The weather, pot size and mix change the interval more than the calendar does, which is why our guide on [how often to water an echeveria](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-echeveria) is built around checking the soil.

Pour the water onto the soil, not into the rosette. Water that sits between the leaves in a cool or dim room can start rot at the centre of the plant. Do not mist echeverias. Their leaves are covered in a powdery wax called farina that protects them from sun and water loss, and wet leaves are no help to a desert plant.

How to tell which way you got it wrong:

- **Too much water:** lower leaves turn yellow or translucent and soft, the stem darkens at the base, and leaves drop at a touch.
- **Too little water:** leaves thin out and wrinkle, starting with the oldest ones at the bottom. They plump up again within a day or two of a good soak.

A few wrinkled leaves at the very bottom of the rosette are normal. The plant reabsorbs its oldest leaves as it grows. Pull them off once they are dry; NC State notes that withered lower leaves can become a source of disease if left in place.

## Best soil and pot for an echeveria

Use a gritty, sharply draining mix. The [RHS recommends](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/Echeveria) peat-free cactus compost in a pot with plenty of drainage holes. Bagged cactus mixes are often still quite fine, so add one part pumice, perlite or horticultural grit to two parts of the mix. The result should feel loose and drain within seconds when you water.

Terracotta pots let the mix dry faster than glazed or plastic ones, which is useful indoors. Choose a pot only slightly wider than the rosette. A big pot holds a large volume of wet mix that the roots cannot reach, and that is how root rot starts. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains how drainage and pot size interact.

Repot in spring, every two to three years, or when offsets fill the pot. Let the root ball dry first, shake off the old mix, trim any dark or mushy roots, replant at the same depth in dry mix, and wait about a week before watering so any damaged roots can seal.

## Temperature: summer and winter care

During the growing season, normal room temperatures suit echeverias. Outdoors they handle summer heat if they get some afternoon shade and you water a little more often.

They are not frost hardy. NC State advises bringing them inside for winter anywhere outside a frost-free area, and the RHS says to keep them above 10°C (50°F). A cool, bright room between 10 and 15°C (50 and 59°F) in winter is ideal: the plant rests, uses very little water, and keeps its shape. A warm, dim living room is the hardest place for an echeveria to overwinter, because warmth pushes new growth that the light cannot support, and the result is stretching.

Keep plants off cold windowsills on frosty nights and away from radiators. Dry indoor air in winter is not a problem for echeveria, so you do not need to raise the humidity.

## Feeding an echeveria

Echeverias need very little fertiliser. From spring to late summer, feed about once a month with a cactus fertiliser or a balanced liquid fertiliser at half the label strength, always on soil that is already moist. Stop in autumn and give nothing in winter. Too much nitrogen makes soft, stretched growth that is more prone to rot, so less is better here.

Pale leaves are more often caused by low light than by hunger. Check the light before adding more fertiliser.

## How to propagate an echeveria

NC State lists three methods: offsets, leaf cuttings and stem cuttings. All work best in spring and early summer.

1. **Offsets:** many echeverias produce small rosettes around the base. Once an offset is a few centimetres across, separate it with its own roots if possible and pot it up.
2. **Leaf cuttings:** twist a healthy leaf off sideways so it comes away whole, including the small base where it joined the stem. Leave it on a dry surface for a few days until the end has callused, then lay it on top of barely moist cactus mix in bright light. Roots and a tiny rosette appear after a few weeks. Water lightly once roots have formed.
3. **Stem cuttings (beheading):** cut a stretched rosette off with 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of stem, let the cut dry for several days, and set it on dry mix. Start watering once it resists a gentle tug. The old stem usually sprouts new rosettes too.

Torn leaves without their base rarely root. For more on cuttings in general, see our [plant propagation guide](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings).

## Common echeveria problems

- **Stretched, leaning rosette:** too little light. Move it closer to a sunny window.
- **Soft, translucent or black leaves at the base:** overwatering or rot. Let the plant dry out, cut away any rotten tissue, and re-root healthy parts.
- **White, cottony tufts between leaves or on roots:** mealybugs. Dab them with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol and check the roots when you repot.
- **Small insects on flower stalks:** aphids. Rinse them off or cut the stalk.
- **Scorched patches:** a sudden move into strong sun.

## Is echeveria safe for cats and dogs?

Yes. NC State lists echeveria as non-toxic to cats, dogs and horses, and the ASPCA lists common species such as *Echeveria elegans* as non-toxic. A pet that chews a lot of any plant may still vomit, but echeveria is not poisonous. For other options, see our list of [plants safe for cats](/en/blog/plants-safe-for-cats).
