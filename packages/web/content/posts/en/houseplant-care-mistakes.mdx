Most houseplant care mistakes come down to water, light and the pot. Watering on a fixed schedule, a pot with no drainage hole or one far too big, and a spot too dark for the plant cause more deaths than pests and diseases combined. The [University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/houseplant-care/) puts it plainly: improper watering is a primary cause of houseplant death. Below are the 10 most common mistakes, how to recognise each one, and the fix.

## 1. Watering on a schedule instead of by the soil

A plant does not use the same amount of water every week. It drinks more in bright, warm summer weeks and much less in dim, cool winter ones. Watering every Sunday whatever happens keeps the soil wet in winter and too dry in a summer heatwave.

**How to tell:** leaves yellow and soften, starting low on the plant, and the soil is still damp days after watering.

**The fix:** check before you water. Wisconsin's test is to push a finger about 2.5 cm (1 in) into the soil and water only if it feels dry at that depth. Plants that like to dry out more, such as succulents and cacti, can wait until the soil is dry further down. Plants that like steady moisture, such as ferns and calatheas, want water as soon as the top dries. Our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) shows how to tell the two apart.

## 2. Giving small sips of water

A splash of water every couple of days wets the top of the soil and leaves the roots at the bottom dry. It also lets salts from tap water and fertiliser build up, which shows as brown leaf tips and a white crust on the soil.

**The fix:** when you water, water thoroughly. Wisconsin recommends watering until water seeps from the drainage holes, letting the pot drain for 10 to 20 minutes, then emptying the saucer. Then wait until the soil has dried to the right depth again.

## 3. Using a pot with no drainage hole

Decorative pots without a hole hold water at the bottom where you cannot see it. The roots sitting in it run out of air and rot. A layer of gravel at the bottom does not fix this. It raises the wet zone higher into the pot instead of removing it.

**The fix:** plant in a pot with a drainage hole. If you love a pot without one, use it as a cachepot: keep the plant in a plastic nursery pot inside it, and lift it out to water and drain. Never leave water standing in the cachepot. Our guide on [how to choose a pot for your plant](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) covers materials and sizes.

## 4. Repotting into a pot far too big

It seems kind to give a small plant room to grow. In practice, a large volume of soil holds water that the roots cannot reach, and it stays wet for weeks. Wisconsin warns that an oversized pot holds too much water and can lead to root rot.

**The fix:** move up only one pot size at a time, a pot a few centimetres wider than the old one. Repot when roots circle the bottom of the pot or grow out of the drainage holes, when water runs straight through, or when the plant dries out within a couple of days. Spring is the best time for most plants.

## 5. Putting a plant in the wrong light

Light is the plant's food. A plant in a dim corner cannot use much water, so it also becomes far easier to overwater. Many popular "low-light" plants tolerate low light rather than enjoy it.

**How to tell:** Wisconsin lists the signs. Too little light gives small, faded leaves, stretched and weak stems, slow growth and no flowers. Too much light gives wilting, scorched or burned patches and washed-out colour.

**The fix:** match the plant to the window. Most leafy tropical plants want bright light without hours of hot direct sun. Succulents, cacti and herbs want as much direct sun as you can give. Light drops quickly with distance from the glass, so a plant two metres from a window gets far less light than one on the sill. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a window.

## 6. Using garden soil

Soil dug from the garden packs down hard in a pot, drains poorly and can bring in pests, weeds and diseases.

**The fix:** use a bagged potting mix made for containers. Wisconsin recommends soilless mixes for houseplants. Add perlite or bark for plants that need sharper drainage, and use a gritty cactus mix for succulents. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) gives mixes by plant type.

## 7. Feeding too much, or in winter

Fertiliser is not a cure for a struggling plant. A plant that is sick, newly repotted or resting in winter cannot use it, and the extra salts burn roots.

**How to tell:** brown, crispy leaf tips and edges, a white crust on the soil or the pot rim, and wilting despite moist soil.

**The fix:** Wisconsin advises feeding only while plants are actively growing, from spring to early autumn, every one to two months for most houseplants, and warns that overfertilising damages them. Always follow the label dose, or use less. If salts have built up, flush the pot by running plenty of plain water through it. Our guide on [how to fertilise houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) covers dilution and timing.

## 8. Placing plants in draughts or next to heat

Tropical houseplants dislike sudden temperature swings. A plant next to a door that opens onto winter air, against a cold window at night, near an air-conditioning vent or above a radiator gets hit by cold, heat or very dry air.

**How to tell:** sudden leaf drop, brown or black patches on leaves that touched cold glass, and crisp edges on the side facing a heater.

**The fix:** keep plants a little back from the glass on frosty nights, out of direct draughts, and away from radiators and heating vents. Most common houseplants are comfortable at the same temperatures people are.

## 9. Bringing home pests with a new plant

Most pest outbreaks start with a new plant. Spider mites, mealybugs, scale and fungus gnats travel on plants from the shop and spread to the rest of your collection.

**The fix:** inspect plants before you buy them, especially the underside of leaves and the leaf joints. Wisconsin suggests keeping a new plant away from others for one to two weeks so problems show up before they spread. After that, check under a few leaves every time you water. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) helps you identify what you find.

## 10. Misting to fix dry air

Misting is one of the most repeated tips, and one of the least useful. The water evaporates within minutes, so the humidity around the plant barely changes, and leaves that stay wet in cool, still air can develop leaf spots.

**The fix:** for plants that really need humidity, such as calatheas and ferns, group plants together, use a pebble tray with the pot above the waterline, or run a humidifier. Most common houseplants cope with normal indoor air. Dusty leaves are a more common problem than dry air: Wisconsin suggests rinsing leaves in the shower from time to time so they can take in light.

## How do you avoid houseplant care mistakes?

Most of them come from treating every plant the same way. Learn what each of your plants needs, check the soil before you water, and change your routine with the seasons. In practice that means three habits:

- **Before watering,** feel the soil at the depth the plant needs.
- **Every season,** move plants closer to the light in autumn and water and feed less in winter.
- **Every time you water,** look under a few leaves for pests.
