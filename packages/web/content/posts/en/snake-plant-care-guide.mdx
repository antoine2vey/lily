Snake plant care is mostly restraint: water only when the soil has dried out completely, which in winter means once every one to two months, keep it above 10°C (50°F), and give it as much indirect light as your room allows. It stores water in its thick leaves and tolerates dry air and dim corners, so the plant is far more likely to be harmed by too much water than by neglect. Overwatering is by far the most common way to lose one.

The snake plant was long sold as Sansevieria trifasciata and is now classified as Dracaena trifasciata. Garden centres use both names, along with mother-in-law's tongue.

## Snake plant care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light, with a few hours of direct sun if you have it. Tolerates low light but barely grows there |
| Water | When the soil is dry all the way down. Roughly every 2 to 4 weeks in a bright, warm room in summer; every 1 to 2 months in winter |
| Humidity | Ordinary room air, even dry winter air. No misting needed |
| Temperature | 16 to 29°C (60 to 85°F). Keep it above 10°C (50°F) |
| Soil | Cactus and succulent mix, or houseplant compost with a third grit or perlite. Pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Half-strength balanced fertiliser 2 or 3 times between spring and late summer. None in winter |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats and dogs (saponins) |

[NC State Extension's plant profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/dracaena-trifasciata/) is the source for the light, winter watering and temperature figures. The plant is native to central Africa and stores water in its leaves, which explains most of what it wants indoors.

## How much light does a snake plant need?

More than its reputation suggests. Snake plants survive in dim rooms, and NC State says they tolerate very low light, but there they stop growing and the soil stays wet for weeks after watering, which is where rot starts. The same profile recommends a spot that gets direct sun for part of the day, 2 to 6 hours.

In practice, the best indoor spot is near a bright window: right beside an east- or west-facing one, or a little back from a south-facing one. A plant that has lived in a dark corner should be moved toward the light over a week or two rather than in one go, or the leaves can scorch. Scorch looks like pale, dry, papery patches on the side facing the glass.

A plant in low light is not wrong, it just needs much less water. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge how bright a spot really is.

## How often should you water a snake plant?

Water when the soil is dry all the way through, not just on top. Push a wooden skewer or chopstick to the bottom of the pot and pull it out: if it comes out clean and dry, it is time. The pot should also feel light when you lift it.

As a starting point:

- **Spring and summer, bright room:** every 2 to 4 weeks.
- **Spring and summer, dim room:** every 4 to 6 weeks.
- **Winter:** NC State says to water only every one to two months.

When you water, water thoroughly. Pour slowly over the whole surface until water runs out of the drainage hole, then empty the saucer after 15 minutes. A thorough soak followed by a long dry spell is what this plant is built for. Small sips every week keep the bottom of the pot permanently damp, which is the worst of both.

Several things shorten the interval: more light, warmth, a terracotta pot, a small pot full of roots, and a gritty mix. A large plastic pot of dense compost in a cool room can stay wet for well over a month. Water by the soil, not the calendar.

Signs you are overwatering: leaves that turn soft, yellow or translucent at the base, that lean or fall over, and soil that smells sour. Signs you are underwatering, which is rare: leaves that wrinkle lengthwise or curl inward and feel thin. Wrinkled leaves plump back up within a week or two of a good soak.

## Does a snake plant need humidity or misting?

No. NC State notes that it tolerates low humidity, and its thick, waxy leaves lose very little water. Normal indoor air is fine, including centrally heated air in winter. Do not mist it: water sitting in the leaf rosette or at the base of the leaves can encourage rot, and misting does nothing useful for this plant. Pebble trays and humidifiers are unnecessary.

## What temperature does a snake plant need?

Normal room temperature suits it, roughly 16 to 29°C (60 to 85°F). NC State says it tolerates cool temperatures around 10°C (50°F). Below that, and especially in cold, wet soil, leaves can be damaged: they turn soft, dark and water-soaked a few days later and do not recover. Keep it away from cold window panes and outside doors in winter. In summer it can go outside in a sheltered, partly shaded spot once nights stay above about 15°C (59°F), and come back in before autumn nights get cold.

## What soil and pot does a snake plant need?

It needs soil that drains fast and dries out completely. NC State calls well-drained soil and careful watering "a must." Use a cactus and succulent mix, or make one with two parts houseplant compost and one part grit, perlite or fine bark. Avoid moisture-retaining mixes sold for tropical plants.

The pot matters as much as the soil:

- **A drainage hole is not optional.** A decorative pot without one is fine only as an outer sleeve that you empty after watering.
- **Terracotta helps.** It dries faster than plastic and its weight stops a tall plant from tipping over.
- **Keep it snug.** A pot much bigger than the root ball holds a lot of wet soil the roots cannot reach. Choose a pot only 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider than the old one.

## When should you repot a snake plant?

Snake plants grow slowly and like being a little crowded, so repot only every 2 to 4 years. Signs it is time: roots coming out of the drainage hole, a plastic pot bulging or cracking, or new shoots pressed against the rim. Do it in spring or early summer.

Water a few days before, ease the plant out, and loosen the outer roots. Set it at the same depth as before; burying the base of the leaves deeper invites rot. Then wait about a week before watering, so any roots broken during the move can heal.

## Should you fertilise a snake plant?

Lightly. In spring and summer, feed two or three times with a balanced houseplant fertiliser at half the label strength, or use a cactus fertiliser. Do not feed in autumn or winter, and never feed a plant whose soil is bone dry; water first. Too much fertiliser builds up as salts and can burn the roots. A plant that is repotted every few years into fresh mix needs very little.

Old posts sometimes blame pale or brown-tipped leaves on a specific nutrient shortage. That is rarely the cause. Look at watering, cold and light first.

## How do you propagate a snake plant?

**Division** is the quickest method. When you repot, cut or pull the clump apart so each piece has leaves and its own roots, and pot each one separately. NC State lists division as a recommended method.

**Leaf cuttings** also work but are slow. Cut a healthy leaf into sections 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 in) long, keep track of which end was the bottom, let them dry for a few days, then push the bottom end 2 cm (1 in) into barely damp gritty mix. New shoots can take several months to appear. Variegated types with yellow edges, such as 'Laurentii', usually lose the yellow edge when grown from a leaf cutting; divide them instead if you want to keep it. Our guide to [propagating plants from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers the general technique.

## Common snake plant problems

Most problems come back to water:

- **Soft, mushy leaves that fall over at the base** usually mean root rot from wet soil, sometimes made worse by cold. Unpot it, cut away soft roots and leaves, and repot in dry, gritty mix. Our guide to [snake plant problems](/en/blog/snake-plant-problems) covers each symptom in detail.
- **Wrinkled, curling leaves** on dry soil mean thirst.
- **Brown, dry patches** on leaves facing a window are sun scorch after a sudden move into bright light.
- **Pests** are uncommon. NC State lists mealybugs and spider mites as the ones to watch for. Wipe off mealybugs with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol.

You may read that snake plants clean the air. A potted plant in a normal room removes too little to make a measurable difference, as our article on [air-purifying plants](/en/blog/best-air-purifying-plants) explains. Grow one because it is easy and handsome.

If you tend to forget long gaps between waterings, or the opposite, [Lily](/en) schedules watering per plant and stretches the interval in the darker months.

## Quick answers

**Is a snake plant toxic to cats and dogs?** Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/mother-laws-tongue) lists it as toxic to both because of saponins. NC State lists vomiting, nausea and diarrhoea as the usual signs after a pet chews it.

**Why is my snake plant not growing?** Most likely too little light, or winter. In a dim room it can sit unchanged for a year. Move it closer to a window in spring and growth should restart.

**Does a snake plant flower?** Occasionally, usually when it is mature and a bit pot-bound. The flowers are greenish-white, fragrant spikes, and the plant carries on normally afterwards.
