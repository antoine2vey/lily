The common houseplant pests are aphids, mealybugs, scale insects, whiteflies, thrips, spider mites and fungus gnats. Most can be controlled without strong chemicals: [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/pest-and-disease-problems-of-indoor-plants) recommends picking them off, rinsing both sides of the leaves with warm water and dabbing with alcohol on a cotton swab, and keeping insecticidal soap or horticultural oil for heavy infestations. The single best prevention is to isolate every new plant for about 3 weeks before it joins the others.

## How to identify common houseplant pests

| Pest | What you see | Where | Damage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aphids | Small soft green, black or pale insects in clusters | Shoot tips, undersides of leaves | Twisted, distorted new growth; sticky leaves |
| Mealybugs | White, cottony fluff | Leaf joints, stems, undersides, roots | Stunted growth, leaf drop |
| Scale | Oval or round green or brown bumps that do not move | Stems and leaf veins | Sticky honeydew, black sooty mould, weak growth |
| Whiteflies | Tiny white flies that swarm when you touch the plant | Undersides of leaves | Yellowing, weak growth, leaf drop |
| Thrips | Slender insects about 1.5 mm (1/16 in) long | Flowers, undersides of leaves | Silvery streaks, distorted leaves and flowers, buds that do not open |
| Spider mites | Barely visible dots, often with fine webbing | Undersides of leaves | Pale stippling, grey or bronze leaves, leaf drop |
| Fungus gnats | Small dark flies over the soil | Soil surface; larvae in the soil | Larvae feed on roots of young or weak plants |

Descriptions and damage follow Penn State's guide, except fungus gnats, which follow the [University of California IPM program](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/fungus-gnats/).

A sticky film on leaves or on the floor below a plant is honeydew, the sugary waste of aphids, mealybugs, scale and whiteflies. Penn State notes that a black sooty mould can grow on it. The mould is a sign of the insects, not a separate disease, and it wipes off once they are gone.

## Aphids

Aphids are small, soft-bodied insects that cluster on new shoots and under leaves. Penn State notes they cause distorted new growth and lists ivy, herbs and hibiscus among the plants they favour. They multiply quickly, so act as soon as you see them.

**Treatment:** rinse the plant under a lukewarm shower, or wipe the clusters off with your fingers. Repeat every few days for two weeks. For a plant covered in aphids, cut off the worst shoots and spray insecticidal soap on the rest, following the label.

## Mealybugs

Mealybugs look like small tufts of white cotton. Penn State notes they live on stems, leaves and roots, and that their feeding stunts growth and causes leaves to drop early. They hide in leaf joints, under leaves and in the folds of new growth, which is why they come back after a quick clean. NC State Extension lists them as pests of jade plants and string of hearts, for example.

**Treatment:** dab every visible mealybug with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then rinse the plant. Check again every week for at least a month, because eggs hatch after the adults are gone. If mealybugs reappear on a plant that looks clean, unpot it and look at the roots: white fluff on the roots means root mealybugs, and the plant needs the old soil removed and a rinse of the roots before repotting.

## Scale insects

Scale insects look like small bumps on stems and along leaf veins, and many people mistake them for part of the plant. Penn State describes adult soft scales with oval or round green or brown shells; the young crawlers move for a short time, then settle and cover themselves with wax. That wax is why sprays work poorly on adults.

**Treatment:** scrape the bumps off with a fingernail or a cotton swab dipped in alcohol, then wipe the stems. Cut off badly infested stems. Horticultural oil, used as the label says, smothers the young stages. Check weekly, because a few missed scales are enough to start again.

## Whiteflies

Whiteflies are tiny white insects that rise in a small cloud when you brush the plant. Penn State notes that infested plants turn yellow, lose vigour and drop leaves. The young stages are flat and pale, and stay fixed on the underside of leaves.

**Treatment:** yellow sticky cards near the plant catch adults and show how many there are. Rinse the undersides of the leaves, and spray insecticidal soap or horticultural oil on the undersides, where the young stages live. Repeat according to the label.

## Thrips

Thrips are slender insects about 1.5 mm (1/16 in) long. Penn State notes that they cause streaking and distortion of leaves and flowers, and that buds often fail to open. On foliage, the damage looks like silvery or bleached patches with tiny black dots of excrement.

**Treatment:** isolate the plant, rinse it thoroughly, including the undersides of the leaves, and remove damaged flowers where thrips hide. Insecticidal soap works on the stages it touches, so repeated, thorough applications are needed. Our guide on [how to get rid of thrips](/en/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-thrips) covers the full cycle.

## Spider mites

Spider mites are not insects but tiny arachnids. Penn State notes that they do best in hot, dry conditions and feed on the undersides of leaves, causing stippling, grey or bronze discolouration and early leaf drop. Fine webbing between leaves and stems shows a heavy infestation. To check, hold a sheet of white paper under a leaf and tap it: moving specks are mites.

**Treatment:** rinse the whole plant under a lukewarm shower, paying attention to the undersides of the leaves, and repeat every few days. Insecticidal soap or horticultural oil applied to the undersides helps with heavy infestations. Our guide to [spider mite treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) has a full routine.

## Fungus gnats

Fungus gnats are small, dark flies that run over the soil and fly up when you water. UC IPM describes the adults as 1.5 to 3 mm (1/16 to 1/8 in) long; the adults do not harm plants or bite. Their larvae, which have a shiny black head and a whitish body, live in damp soil and can damage roots, especially in young plants and seedlings.

**Treatment:** the main fix is the soil. UC IPM recommends letting the surface of the potting mix dry between waterings, avoiding overwatering and providing good drainage. Yellow sticky traps cut into small squares and set in the pot catch adults. Products containing Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) kill the larvae, but UC IPM notes it does not last long indoors, so repeated applications about 5 days apart may be needed. If the soil stays wet for long, that is often a sign of overwatering; our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) explains how to tell.

## How to treat houseplant pests safely

Whatever the pest, the same steps apply:

1. **Isolate the plant.** Move it away from other plants before you do anything else.
2. **Remove what you can by hand.** Rinse both sides of the leaves with warm water, pick off clusters, and dab hidden ones with alcohol on a cotton swab.
3. **Prune the worst parts.** Penn State recommends pruning infested parts in heavy infestations. Clean your pruners afterwards with a 10% bleach solution or full-strength isopropyl alcohol, as Penn State advises.
4. **Use a contact spray only if needed.** Insecticidal soap and horticultural oil only kill the pests they touch. Spray the undersides of the leaves and follow the label for dose and repeats.
5. **Check weekly for a month.** Eggs and hidden pests survive a first treatment.

Before you spray, check the label for plants it should not be used on, and test the product on a few leaves first. Some plants are sensitive: [Clemson Cooperative Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/jade-plant/), for example, advises against insecticidal soap on jade plants and recommends alcohol for mealybugs instead.

Use a product sold as insecticidal soap rather than washing-up liquid. Dish detergents are not made or labelled for plants, and their strength varies from brand to brand. Older articles also recommend homemade garlic or pepper sprays. There is no reliable dose for these, so they are not worth the risk to the leaves when rinsing and alcohol work.

## How to prevent houseplant pests

- **Quarantine new plants.** Penn State advises keeping new plants apart from your other houseplants for about 3 weeks, and checking the leaves, stems and soil before they join the collection. The same applies to plants coming back in after a summer outdoors. Our guide on [how to acclimate new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) covers that step.
- **Look under the leaves when you water.** Most pests start on the undersides and at the shoot tips. Catching ten mealybugs is much easier than catching a hundred.
- **Avoid soil that stays wet.** It invites fungus gnats and weakens roots.
- **Raise humidity for plants prone to spider mites.** Mites favour hot, dry air, so plants next to a radiator are most at risk in winter.
- **Clean your tools.** Wipe pruners and scissors between plants.

Keeping a record of when you watered and what you saw helps you link a problem to its cause. In [Lily](/en), Ask Lily answers care and diagnosis questions using each plant's history.

## Quick answers

**Are the brown dots under my fern pests?** Not if they sit in neat rows along the edges of the leaflets. Those are spores, a normal part of the fern's life. Scale insects are scattered irregularly and scrape off.

**Can pests live in the soil?** Yes. Fungus gnat larvae live in damp soil, and mealybugs can live on the roots, which is why a plant that keeps getting reinfested needs its roots checked.
