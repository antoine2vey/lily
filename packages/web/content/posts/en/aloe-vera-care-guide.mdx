Aloe vera care comes down to three things: the sunniest window you have, a gritty mix that drains in seconds, and water only once that mix has dried out. Keep the plant between 13 and 27°C (55 and 80°F), water much less from autumn to early spring, and skip misting entirely. Most aloes that die indoors die from too much water, not too little.

## Aloe vera care at a glance

| Need | What it wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Full sun to part sun, in a south- or west-facing window |
| Water | Soak, then wait until the mix is dry all the way down; much less in winter |
| Humidity | Ordinary room air; no misting, no pebble tray |
| Temperature | 13 to 27°C (55 to 80°F); no frost |
| Soil | 1 part potting mix, 1 part coarse sand, 1 part perlite, in a pot with drainage holes |
| Pets | Toxic to cats and dogs if chewed |

## How much light does aloe vera need?

More than most houseplants. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/aloe-a-hardy-houseplant) recommends full to partial sun near a sunny window, and NC State lists it as a full sun to part shade plant. In practice that means a south- or west-facing window, as close to the glass as you can get it. A north window is rarely enough. Grow lights work well if you have no bright window.

The signs of too little light are easy to read once you know them. The plant leans hard toward the window, new leaves come in thinner and paler, and the rosette stretches open until leaves flop over at the base. Rotate the pot a quarter turn every week or two so it grows evenly.

Some older advice says aloe wants bright *indirect* light. That is too cautious for a succulent from dry, open habitats. The real risk is a sudden change. A plant that spent the winter in a dim room can scorch if you move it straight into full summer sun, and the leaves turn brown or bleached in patches. Move it gradually over a week or two, the way you would [acclimate any new plant](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants).

## How often should you water aloe vera?

Water when the mix is dry, not on a calendar. From early spring to early autumn, Penn State says to water thoroughly once the surface is dry, and NC State goes further: let the soil dry completely. A wooden skewer pushed to the bottom of the pot tells you more than the surface does. If it comes out with damp soil stuck to it, wait.

When you do water, water properly. Pour until water runs out of the drainage holes, let it drain, and empty the saucer. Small sips that only wet the top centimetre leave the roots dry and encourage shallow roots.

From autumn through winter the plant grows very little. Penn State's advice is to keep the mix only barely moist, just enough that the leaves do not shrivel. In a cool room that can mean watering once a month or less. A plant next to a radiator will need a little more.

If you are unsure, wait. Penn State's own phrasing is to err on the side of underwatering. A thirsty aloe shows it with thinner, curling leaves and recovers after a good soak. A waterlogged one rots from the base up, and by the time you see it the damage is done.

## What soil and pot does aloe vera need?

Penn State suggests a homemade mix of 1 part potting soil, 1 part sand and 1 part perlite or lava rock. The [RHS aloe guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/aloe) recommends a peat-free cactus compost with extra grit. Either way, the mix should drain almost as fast as you pour.

Terracotta is a good choice because it dries out faster than plastic, and NC State recommends clay pots with several drainage holes. The pot needs large holes in the bottom. A decorative pot without holes is the most common way people drown an aloe. For more on mixes, see our guide to [the best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants).

## What temperature and humidity does aloe vera like?

The RHS gives a range of 13 to 27°C (55 to 80°F), which covers most homes year round. Aloe is not frost tolerant. If it spends the summer on a balcony or patio, bring it in well before the first frost.

Humidity is the place where many older care guides go wrong. Aloe does not want humid air, and the RHS says it does not grow well in shade or in compost that stays wet. Do not mist it, do not stand it on a pebble tray, and do not group it with ferns in a steamy bathroom. Water sitting in the rosette is an invitation to rot. Normal indoor air, even dry winter air, suits it fine.

## Winter care for aloe vera

Winter care is mostly restraint. Keep the plant in the brightest spot you have, since the days are short. Cut watering right back, to just enough that the leaves stay plump. Keep it away from cold glass at night and from the direct blast of a radiator. Do not feed it.

A cooler room in winter is fine, and arguably better, as long as it stays within the range above. The combination to avoid is cold and wet. Cold slows the plant's water use to almost nothing, so damp soil stays damp for weeks.

## Feeding and repotting

Aloe needs very little fertiliser. The RHS suggests a half-strength houseplant feed once in spring and once in late summer. More than that pushes soft growth that flops.

Penn State recommends repotting about every two years, in spring, into a pot only 2.5 to 4 cm (1 to 1.5 in) wider than the old one. A much larger pot holds far more wet soil than the roots can use. While the plant is out of its pot, you can separate the pups, the small offsets at the base, and pot them on their own.

## How to get your aloe vera to grow faster

Aloe is slow by nature. The things that speed it up are the ones above: more light, a pot it has filled with roots, warm summer temperatures, and that light feed in spring. A plant in a dim corner will survive for years and barely grow.

Flowers need patience. NC State notes that only plants at least 3 years old flower. Indoors, with less light than in the wild, flowering is probably less likely.

## Common aloe vera problems

**Soft, mushy, brown or translucent leaves at the base.** This is rot from wet soil, and the RHS names overwatering as the most common problem. Unpot the plant, cut away anything soft, let it dry for a few days and repot into fresh, dry gritty mix. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers the steps.

**Thin, curling or shrivelled leaves.** The plant is using its stored water. If the mix has been bone dry for weeks, water it thoroughly. If the mix is wet and the leaves still shrivel, check the roots for rot, because dead roots cannot take up water.

**Pale, stretched, floppy growth.** Not enough light. Move it closer to a sunny window.

**White cottony spots or small brown bumps.** These are mealybugs and scale insects. The RHS also lists red spider mite. Wipe them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol and check again weekly.

**Orange or brown spots on the leaves.** NC State lists leaf spot and aloe rust among the diseases of aloe. Remove badly marked leaves and keep water off the foliage.

## Is aloe vera toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/aloe) lists aloe as toxic to dogs, cats and horses because of the saponins and anthraquinones in the plant. Chewing it can cause vomiting, lethargy and diarrhoea. NC State also notes that the latex, the yellow sap just under the skin of the leaf, can irritate skin. Keep the plant somewhere pets cannot reach. If you need a sunny-window plant for a home with cats, see our list of [plants safe for cats](/en/blog/plants-safe-for-cats).

## Quick answers

**Should I cut off brown aloe leaves?** Yes, once a leaf is fully dry or mushy it will not recover. Cut it at the base with a clean knife.

**Can aloe vera live in a bathroom?** Only if the bathroom has a bright, sunny window. Humid, dim bathrooms are the wrong place for it.

**How do I propagate aloe vera?** Separate the pups, the small offsets at the base, when you repot in spring, and give each one its own small pot of gritty mix.
