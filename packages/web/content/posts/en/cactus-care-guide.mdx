Cactus care indoors comes down to three things: as much sun as you can give, a gritty mix that dries quickly, and a cool, dry winter. From spring to early autumn, water thoroughly and then let the mix dry out completely. In winter, keep most cacti at about 7 to 13°C (45 to 55°F) with little or no water; that rest is also what makes many of them flower in spring.

## Cactus care at a glance

| Need | What it wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Full sun or bright direct light: a south or west window. Some shade on the hottest afternoons |
| Water | Spring to autumn: soak, then let the mix dry completely. Winter: little or none |
| Humidity | Dry indoor air is fine |
| Temperature | 21 to 27°C (70 to 80°F) in the growing season, 7 to 13°C (45 to 55°F) in the winter rest |
| Soil | Cactus mix with added grit or coarse sand, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Toxicity to pets | Common cacti such as *Mammillaria* are non-toxic, but spines injure |

The figures above follow the [NC State Extension page for *Mammillaria*](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/mammillaria/), the pincushion cacti, a genus of about 150 species of which many are sold as houseplants. The RHS gives the same general picture for desert cacti and succulents: low moisture, dry air, good drainage and warmth. Forest cacti, such as Christmas cactus, grow on trees in the wild and need shade and more water, so they are not covered here.

## How much sun does a cactus need?

More than most windows give. NC State says *Mammillaria* grows best in full sun or bright direct light and appreciates some afternoon shade in hot weather. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/cacti-succulents) notes that most cacti and succulents are sun-lovers and do poorly in low light away from windows.

Indoors, put your cactus right against the glass of a south or west window. An east window works if it gets several hours of direct morning sun. One of the older posts on this site described a south window as giving indirect light; it gives the most direct light in the house, which is exactly what a cactus needs.

Signs of too little light:

- new growth is thinner and paler than the old, so the plant narrows to a point or leans hard toward the window;
- spines on the new growth are sparse or weak;
- the plant does not flower.

Stretched growth does not shrink back. Move the plant to brighter light and the new growth will come in normally above it.

## Can a cactus get sunburnt?

Yes, when it moves suddenly from dim light to strong sun. That happens most often when a plant goes outdoors for summer or comes home from a shop. Sunburn shows as bleached, yellow or brown patches on the side facing the light, and the scars are permanent.

Increase the light over one to two weeks: a few hours of morning sun first, then longer, then full sun. This is the step the older summer advice here got wrong. It told you to move cacti into indirect light in summer. Once a cactus is used to the sun, it should stay in it; NC State only suggests some afternoon shade during the hottest weather.

## How often should you water a cactus?

From spring to early autumn, when the plant is growing, NC State advises letting the soil dry out completely between waterings. Then water thoroughly: pour until water runs from the drainage hole, let it drain, and empty the saucer. In a sunny window in summer, a small pot may need water every week or two; a large one much less often. Check that the mix is dry all the way down, with a wooden skewer or by the weight of the pot, before you water again.

The RHS sums up the balance: too much water can stunt growth or cause rot, too little causes shrivelling. A cactus that is shrivelling, wrinkling or turning soft and yellow at the base is telling you which one. Our guide on [how often to water a cactus](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-cactus) covers pot size, season and the signs in more detail.

## How do you care for a cactus in winter?

Most desert cacti rest in winter. NC State says *Mammillaria* goes dormant in winter and then prefers 7 to 13°C (45 to 55°F), and that this cool rest encourages flowering in spring. It advises suspending watering during dormancy. The University of Arkansas Extension [table of houseplant temperatures](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/yard-garden/home-landscape/docs/Temperature%20Requirements%20of%20Selected%20House%20Plants.pdf) also lists cacti and succulents among cool-temperature plants during their winter rest.

In practice:

1. From mid-autumn, water less and stop feeding.
2. Move the plant to the brightest cool spot you have, such as an unheated room, a cool porch or a sunny windowsill away from radiators.
3. Give little or no water until spring. If the plant starts to shrivel badly, give a small drink on a mild day.
4. In early spring, bring it back to warmth and start watering again, lightly at first.

The RHS warns that cold makes rot more likely, which is why a cool cactus must also be a dry one. Keep it frost-free; NC State notes that *Mammillaria* is cold-intolerant.

If you have no cool room, a cactus can spend winter in a normal heated room. It will keep growing slowly and need occasional water, probably once every three to four weeks, and it will be less likely to flower. Our article on [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers light and heating for the whole collection.

## What soil does a cactus need?

NC State recommends a well-drained cactus mix with added grit or coarse sand. Bagged cactus compost is often too fine and peaty on its own. Mixing in a third to a half of grit, pumice or perlite helps the pot drain in seconds and dry within days.

Use a pot with a drainage hole. Terracotta suits cacti because it dries faster than plastic. Choose a pot only slightly wider than the plant; a large pot of wet mix around a small root system is the usual start of rot. Our guides to [soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) and [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) explain why.

## How do you repot a cactus?

NC State suggests repotting every 2 to 3 years, in spring.

1. Let the mix dry out for a few days first.
2. Hold the cactus with folded newspaper, a strip of cardboard or thick gloves.
3. Ease it out, shake off the old mix and check the roots. Cut off any that are black and soft.
4. Set it in the new pot at the same depth, fill around it with dry mix and tap the pot to settle it.
5. Wait about a week before watering so any broken roots can heal.

## Should you feed a cactus?

Lightly, in the growing season only. A fertiliser made for cacti, or a balanced one at half strength, once a month from spring to late summer is plenty. Stop in autumn so the plant can rest.

## How do you propagate a cactus?

NC State names seeds and offsets, the small pups that form around the base, as the easiest methods for *Mammillaria*. Twist or cut an offset away, leave it on a dry surface for a few days until the cut end has dried and sealed, then set it on top of dry cactus mix. Water lightly once roots form, usually after a few weeks.

## Pests, diseases and pets

NC State lists mealybugs, spider mites and scale insects, and notes that bacterial and fungal diseases can follow overwatering or poor air circulation. Mealybugs hide between spines and in the woolly crown: remove them with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol. Soft brown or black patches near the base are rot. Our article on [cactus problems](/en/blog/cactus-problems) covers each symptom.

NC State describes *Mammillaria* as non-toxic to humans and pets, but warns that the spines are sharp and can cause injury. Place cacti where pets and children will not brush against them.
