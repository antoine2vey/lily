English ivy care indoors comes down to bright light, a cool room, and soil that stays lightly moist without ever turning soggy: water when the top 2 to 3 cm (about 1 in) has dried. Ivy is a hardy outdoor plant in most of Europe and North America, so the warm, dry air of a heated living room suits it less than a cool bedroom or a bright stairwell. Warm, dry air is also when spider mites, its most common pest, take over.

## English ivy care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright light, near a window. Some morning or winter sun is fine; shade from strong summer midday sun |
| Water | When the top 2 to 3 cm (about 1 in) of soil is dry. Roughly weekly in summer, every 1 to 2 weeks in winter |
| Humidity | Low to medium is fine. Very dry, warm air invites spider mites |
| Temperature | Cool. It does best in a room that stays roughly 10 to 21°C (50 to 70°F) |
| Soil | Regular potting mix in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Balanced liquid fertiliser at half strength once a month, spring to late summer |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats, dogs and horses. The sap can irritate skin |

[Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/houseplants/english-ivy) sums up its needs indoors as bright light, cool temperatures, low to medium humidity, regular potting mix and moist soil, and says to inspect for spider mites regularly. The temperature range in the table is a common grower guideline rather than a figure from that page.

## How much light does English ivy need?

More than most trailing houseplants. Ivy in a dim corner grows long, thin stems with small leaves spaced far apart, and drops older leaves. Put it close to a bright window: an east- or west-facing one is ideal, or a north-facing one if nothing blocks it. A south-facing window works if a sheer curtain softens the summer midday sun, which can scorch leaves pressed against the glass.

Variegated ivies, the ones with white, cream or yellow markings, need the most light. The RHS notes that variegated shoots can revert to plain green, and advises removing those shoots as soon as you see them. If a variegated plant keeps producing green growth, it probably wants more light too. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot.

## How often should you water English ivy?

Water when the top 2 to 3 cm (about 1 in) of soil is dry to the touch, then water thoroughly until it runs out of the drainage hole, and empty the saucer after 15 minutes. Ivy dislikes both extremes: a pot that dries out completely makes the leaves crisp and drop, and soil that stays wet rots the roots.

As a starting point:

- **Spring and summer:** about once a week, more often for a small pot in a bright window.
- **Autumn and winter:** every 1 to 2 weeks. In a cool room the soil dries slowly, so check before you water rather than keeping the summer rhythm.

A hanging basket dries faster than a pot on a shelf because it hangs in warmer air and loses water from all sides. Check it more often.

## What temperature and humidity does English ivy need?

Keep it cool. Ivy grows outdoors through cold winters, and indoors it is happiest in a room that rarely goes above about 21°C (70°F). It tolerates normal room temperatures, but the warmer and drier the air, the more it suffers from crisp leaf edges and spider mites. The Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station's pest handbook says spider mite control on ivy should start with keeping the air in the home somewhat humid.

In practice:

- Keep it away from radiators, heating vents and the top of a fridge.
- In winter, a cooler room with good light is better than a warm one.
- If the only spot is warm and dry, a humidifier in the room helps. Misting raises humidity for a few minutes only and is not worth the effort.

Our guide on [raising humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

## How do you care for English ivy in winter?

Indoor ivy does not need special winter preparation, but three adjustments help:

1. **Move it closer to the light.** Days are short and weak, and ivy that was fine a metre back from the window in summer may start dropping leaves. Put it right beside the glass, but not touching it on frosty nights.
2. **Water less often.** Check the soil each time. In a cool room, every 10 to 14 days is often enough.
3. **Stop feeding** from autumn until growth restarts in spring.

Heating is the main winter risk. Check the leaf undersides every week or two for fine speckling or webbing, the first signs of spider mites. Our article on [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers the same adjustments for the rest of your plants.

## What soil and pot does English ivy need?

Ordinary houseplant potting mix works. Illinois Extension recommends regular potting mix. Add a handful of perlite per litre if your mix is dense and stays wet for days. The pot must have a drainage hole.

Repot in spring, only when roots fill the pot and circle the bottom or grow out of the drainage hole, usually every one to two years. Move up just one size, 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider. A big pot of wet soil around a small root ball is a common cause of root rot.

## Should you fertilise English ivy?

Feed once a month from spring to late summer with a balanced liquid houseplant fertiliser at half strength. Do not feed in autumn and winter, and never feed a plant whose soil is dry: water first. Too much fertiliser shows as brown, burnt leaf edges and a white crust on the soil.

## How do you prune and train English ivy?

Ivy responds well to cutting. Illinois Extension advises pinching off the shoot tips often, starting when the plant is small, to get a bushy plant rather than a few long strands. The RHS says to trim excess growth in mid-spring, and that overgrown plants can be cut back hard in early spring because they regrow readily from old wood.

Trim stems that have lost their lower leaves, cut out any plain green shoots on a variegated plant, and wind long stems back over the top of the pot or around a hoop or small trellis. Indoors, ivy will cling to walls with its aerial roots and can leave marks on paint, so give it a support rather than letting it wander. Our guide on [how to prune houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-prune-houseplants) covers the technique.

## How do you propagate English ivy?

Ivy roots easily from stem cuttings. Cut pieces 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) long from healthy shoots, remove the lowest leaves, and put the bare part in a glass of water or in moist potting mix. Keep them in bright light out of direct sun. The RHS suggests taking cuttings in late summer for outdoor plants; indoors, cuttings root at most times of year, slowest in winter. Pot several rooted cuttings together for a fuller plant.

## Can English ivy live outside?

Yes. It is fully hardy outdoors in most of Europe and North America. NC State lists it as hardy from USDA zone 4 to 13. But in much of North America it is invasive: [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/hedera-helix/) notes that it climbs trees and shrubs and eventually kills them, and suggests growing it as a houseplant or in a patio container. A pot of ivy on a balcony in summer is fine; planting it out under trees is a different decision.

## Common English ivy problems

The most frequent ones, briefly:

- **Brown, crisp leaf edges** usually come from warm, dry air or soil that dried out completely.
- **Yellow speckling and fine webbing** are spider mites.
- **Yellow leaves with wet soil** mean overwatering or a pot that is too big.
- **Bare, leggy stems** mean too little light.

## Quick answers

**Is English ivy toxic to cats and dogs?** Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/english-ivy) lists it as toxic to dogs, cats and horses because of triterpenoid saponins, and notes that the foliage is more toxic than the berries. Signs include vomiting, abdominal pain, drooling and diarrhoea. NC State adds that the sap can cause skin irritation, so wear gloves for big pruning jobs.

**Does English ivy clean the air?** Not in a way you would notice. A few potted plants in a room remove far too little to change indoor air quality. Our article on [air-purifying plants](/en/blog/best-air-purifying-plants) explains why.
