The most common spider plant problems are brown leaf tips and yellowing leaves. Brown tips usually come from salts building up in the soil, from fertiliser or from the fluoride and chlorine in tap water, and sometimes from soil that dries out completely. Yellow, limp leaves with wet soil point to overwatering and root rot. Diseases are rare; spider mites and scale are the pests to watch for.

## Spider plant problems: what to check first

The spider plant (*Chlorophytum comosum*) is one of the toughest houseplants, and [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/chlorophytum-comosum/) lists no significant problems for it. When something does go wrong, it is almost always water, salts or light. Before you act, check three things:

1. **The soil.** Push a finger in to the second knuckle. Wet, damp or bone dry?
2. **The water you use.** Tap water, softened water, rainwater or distilled?
3. **The light.** How far is the plant from a window, and does sun hit it at midday?

## Why does my spider plant have brown tips?

Brown, dry leaf tips are the classic spider plant complaint. The causes, from most to least common:

**1. Salts from tap water.** NC State advises against watering with tap water, because the chlorine and fluorides can cause leaf tip burn. Spider plants are known for being sensitive to fluoride. [Clemson's spider plant factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/spider-plant/) adds that water high in soluble salts causes tip burn. Softened water is high in sodium and is worse than plain tap water.

Letting tap water stand overnight, a tip you will see often, lets some chlorine escape but does nothing for fluoride or dissolved salts. Filters vary; most jug filters are not designed to remove fluoride. If your plant keeps making brown tips, use rainwater, distilled water or reverse-osmosis water instead.

**2. Too much fertiliser.** Clemson lists excess fertiliser as the other main cause. Fertiliser salts build up in the pot, especially if you feed often and rarely water heavily enough for water to run out of the bottom. A white crust on the soil or the rim of the pot is a sign of salt buildup.

**3. Soil that dries out completely.** Clemson notes that excessively dry soil also browns the tips. Spider plants store water in thick roots and survive drought, but repeated dry spells show up at the tips first.

**4. Very dry air.** Low humidity can add to tip browning, according to Clemson. It is rarely the only cause, and misting does not raise humidity for more than a few minutes. NC State suggests a humidifier or a tray of pebbles with water under the pot if your air is very dry.

**How to fix it:** flush the pot. Take it to the sink and run plenty of water (rainwater or distilled, if you have it) through the soil, several times the pot's volume, to wash the salts out. Then switch water, feed less often at half strength, and water as soon as the top few centimetres dry. Brown tips do not turn green again. Trim them off with clean scissors, cutting at an angle to follow the leaf's natural point and leaving a sliver of brown so you do not cut into healthy tissue.

## Why are my spider plant leaves turning yellow?

**1. Overwatering and root rot.** Leaves that yellow, go limp and soft at the base, with soil that stays wet, mean the roots are struggling. Clemson says root rot usually comes from a soil mix that does not drain quickly or from excessive watering. Check the roots (see below).

**2. Old leaves dying back.** One or two of the oldest, outermost leaves yellowing and drying while the rest is green is normal turnover.

**3. Too much sun.** NC State calls the spider plant intolerant of direct sunlight, and Clemson warns that midday sun may scorch the leaves. Bleached, yellow-white or brown patches on the leaves nearest the window are sun damage.

**4. Underfeeding or a crowded pot.** A plant that has filled its pot with roots for years and is rarely fed slowly pales all over. Repot and resume light feeding in spring and summer.

For yellowing on any plant, our [yellow leaves guide](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) walks through how to tell these causes apart.

## Why are my spider plant leaves pale or losing their stripes?

**Too little light.** In a dim spot, variegated spider plants produce paler, floppier leaves with weaker stripes, and they rarely make baby plants. NC State says the spider plant tolerates deep shade, but it grows best in medium, indirect light. Move it near a bright window, out of direct midday sun.

**Too much light** does the opposite: washed-out, bleached leaves. If pale leaves sit right in a sunny window, move the plant back a metre or two (a few feet).

## Why is my spider plant drooping or limp?

**1. Thirsty.** Limp leaves on dry, light soil mean it needs water. Clemson suggests letting the soil dry out only briefly between waterings and checking it often. Water thoroughly and it should perk up within a day.

**2. Rotting roots.** Limp leaves on wet soil are the warning sign of rot. More water will not help.

**3. Cold.** NC State says to keep the plant above 7°C (45°F). Clemson suggests nights around 10 to 13°C (50 to 55°F) are ideal and advises moving spider plants a few feet away from windows in winter to protect them from drafts. Leaves damaged by cold go limp, water-soaked and then brown.

## How do I check a spider plant for root rot?

Slide the plant out of its pot. Spider plants have thick, white, fleshy tuberous roots that store water. Healthy ones are firm and pale. Rotten ones are brown, mushy and smell sour.

1. Wash off the old soil.
2. Cut away every soft or dark root with clean scissors.
3. Repot in fresh, free-draining potting mix in a pot with a drainage hole, only a little bigger than the remaining roots.
4. Water lightly and keep the plant out of direct sun while it recovers.

If almost all the roots are gone, root a few of the baby plants instead. They root readily when they touch moist soil. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers severe cases.

## Why is my spider plant not making babies?

Spider plants make most of their offshoots when the days shorten in autumn, according to Clemson. A plant that never produces runners is usually young, in too little light, or recently repotted into a large pot where it grows roots rather than runners. Give it bright, indirect light, let it fill its pot a little, and be patient through one autumn.

## Why are the roots breaking the pot?

The thick, tuberous roots expand fast. Clemson advises dividing and repotting spider plants before the roots crack the container, and says you can repot at any time of year. Soil that dries out within a day or two, roots growing out of the drainage holes, or a pot bulging out of shape are the signs. Divide the clump into two or three pieces, each with leaves and roots, and pot them separately.

## What pests attack spider plants?

Clemson lists spider mites, scale, whiteflies and aphids as the most common pests.

- **Spider mites:** fine pale speckling on the leaves and webbing where the leaves meet, worst in warm, dry rooms in winter. Rinse the plant in the shower and treat with insecticidal soap, repeating weekly. See our [spider mite treatment guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment).
- **Scale:** small brown bumps along the leaves and sticky honeydew below them. Scrape them off and treat repeatedly.
- **Whiteflies:** tiny white insects that fly up when you touch the plant. Yellow sticky traps help monitor them.
- **Aphids:** clusters of small green or black insects on new growth and flower stems. Rinse them off.

More on each in our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests).

## How to keep a spider plant healthy

Bright, indirect light; soil that dries only briefly between waterings; rainwater or distilled water if brown tips are a problem; light feeding during active growth; and a warm room above 7°C (45°F).
