Most cactus problems come down to two things: too much water at the wrong time, and too little light. A cactus that goes soft, dark or mushy at the base is rotting, almost always from wet compost, often in winter or in a pot that is too big. A cactus that grows thin, pale and leans toward the window is short of light.

## Cactus problems: check the base and the compost first

Before reading the symptom sections, do two quick checks.

1. **Press the base of the stem** gently, with a folded cloth or a glove. A healthy cactus is firm all the way down. Softness, a dark ring at soil level, or a sour smell means rot.
2. **Feel the compost** a few centimetres down, and lift the pot. Is it dry and light, or damp and heavy?

The [RHS growing guide for indoor cacti](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/cacti-succulents/houseplants/growing-guide) sums up the needs: gritty compost that never gets waterlogged, a sunny windowsill all year, thorough watering only once the surface is dry in spring and summer, and a cool, nearly dry rest from November to early March. Most of the problems below come from straying from one of those four.

This page covers desert cacti, the spiny kinds that grow in full sun. Forest cacti such as the Christmas cactus (*Schlumbergera*) and orchid cacti (*Epiphyllum*) come from rainforests, like partial shade and more water, and need different care.

## Why is my cactus soft or mushy at the base?

**1. Root or stem rot from wet compost.** This is the most common way cacti die. The base turns soft, then brown or black, the plant may lean or wobble in the pot, and the rot spreads upward. [Clemson's houseplant diseases factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/houseplant-diseases-disorders/) describes the pattern on any plant: stems girdled at soil level by a ring of brown or black tissue, and roots that are brown to black and soft. The usual triggers on a cactus:

- Watering in winter, when the plant is resting and uses almost no water.
- A pot much bigger than the plant. The RHS warns that the extra compost stays wet and causes rot.
- Ordinary potting compost without grit, or a pot without a drainage hole.
- Water left standing in a saucer or decorative pot.
- Cold. The RHS notes that cold increases the risk of rot, and a cold windowsill plus wet compost is the classic combination.

**2. Physical damage that let rot in.** A knock or a split stem can rot from the wound even when watering is right. The soft patch starts at the damage, not at the base.

Rot does not reverse, and fungicides rarely save a rotting cactus. What you can do depends on how far it has spread. See the rescue steps further down.

## Why is my cactus turning yellow?

**1. Rot starting in the roots.** Yellowing that starts at the base and comes with softness is rot. Check the roots.

**2. Too little light.** A cactus kept away from a window turns pale yellow-green overall, often together with thin new growth at the top. Move it to the sunniest window you have, gradually (see the sunburn section).

**3. Too much sudden sun.** A yellow or bleached patch only on the side facing the window, appearing after the plant was moved, is the start of sun scorch.

**4. Hungry compost.** A cactus left for years in the same compost and never fed can pale slowly. The RHS suggests feeding once a month from April to September with a high-potassium liquid feed, such as a tomato feed or a cactus feed.

## Why does my cactus have brown spots or brown patches?

**1. Sun scorch.** Dry, tan, white or brown patches on the side that faces the sun, after a move outdoors or to a brighter window. The damage is permanent but stops spreading once the plant is shaded a little. Move cacti into stronger light over two or three weeks.

**2. Corky scab.** The RHS describes brown, corky patches that shrink into a rough scab, caused by too much humidity or too much brightness. Correct the conditions gradually; the marks stay but new growth is clean.

**3. Normal ageing.** Older columnar cacti often turn brown and bark-like at the base over the years. If the brown area is hard and dry, it is ageing, not disease.

**4. Rot.** Brown that is soft, wet or sunken is rot, covered above.

**5. Cold damage.** The RHS lists dieback and discoloured patches after exposure to cold. Keep cacti off windowsills that drop near freezing on winter nights.

## Why is my cactus growing thin, pale or leaning?

**Too little light.** This is etiolation. The new growth at the top is narrower and paler than the older part, the spines are farther apart, and the whole plant leans toward the window. The RHS lists weak, misshapen growth as the result of too little light. Most cacti want a sunny windowsill all year, ideally south facing in the northern hemisphere.

Etiolated growth will not thicken again. Move the plant to more light, gradually, and the new growth will come in normal. If the stretched part bothers you, you can cut the top off cleanly once the plant has more light, let it dry for a few days, and root it as a cutting.

Watering a cactus in winter while it sits in a warm, dim room makes stretching worse. The RHS warns that winter watering promotes weak growth.

## Why is my cactus wrinkled or shrivelled?

**1. It has used up its water.** A shrunken, wrinkled or puckered cactus on dry compost at the end of summer needs a thorough watering. The RHS lists shrivelling as the sign of too little water.

**2. Winter rest.** Some shrinking during the winter rest is normal. The RHS advises only enough water to stop the plant shrivelling excessively. In a cool room that means an occasional small drink, or none at all.

**3. Dead roots.** If the cactus is shrivelled but the compost is wet, the roots have probably rotted and cannot take up water. Do not water more. Unpot it and look.

## Why won't my cactus flower?

Most desert cacti flower after a cool, dry winter. The RHS says cooler, drier conditions during the dormant period and as much light as possible all year encourage flowering. A cactus kept warm and watered through winter, or kept in a dim spot, usually will not bloom. Some species also need to reach a certain size first.

## What pests attack cacti?

The RHS names three main pests on indoor cacti.

- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony tufts in the creases, between spines and at the base. Some species live on the roots: if a cactus stops growing for no clear reason, check the root ball for white woolly patches. Touch each tuft with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol.
- **Scale insects.** Small brown or grey bumps fixed to the stem that you can flick off with a fingernail. Scrape them off and treat with an insecticide labelled for houseplants.
- **Red spider mites.** Fine, pale or rusty speckling on the skin, sometimes with webbing, in hot, dry rooms. Rinse the plant and repeat treatments, because eggs hatch after the first round.

## How do you save a rotting cactus?

1. Unpot it and shake off the compost. Wear thick gloves, or wrap the cactus in folded newspaper.
2. Look at the roots. Healthy ones are pale and firm. Rotten ones are dark, soft or hollow.
3. With a clean, sharp knife, cut away all rotten roots and tissue. Keep cutting thin slices until the inside looks clean and pale with no brown streaks or spots. Wipe the blade between cuts.
4. Leave the cactus to dry in a bright spot out of direct sun until the cut end has formed a firm callus. The RHS says this takes a day to a week for cuttings, and longer for a large cut.
5. Set it on a mix of 2 parts John Innes No 1 and 1 part grit, or a ready-made cactus compost, in a small pot. Keep it only slightly moist until new roots form, as the RHS advises for cuttings.

If the rot has reached the top, there is nothing to save. If only the base is rotten, cutting off the healthy top and rooting it as a cutting is often the best option. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers the general method.

## How to avoid cactus problems in the first place

- **Compost.** Use cactus compost, or John Innes No 2 with up to 30% grit, as the RHS recommends. See [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants).
- **Pot.** Only slightly larger than the plant, with a drainage hole. Repot in spring, and only when the roots fill the pot.
- **Water.** In spring and summer, water thoroughly once the surface is dry, and let it drain. From November to early March, keep it nearly dry. The RHS recommends tepid rainwater when you can, because minerals in tap water build up in the compost. Our [cactus watering guide](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-cactus) gives starting intervals.
- **Light.** The sunniest window you have, all year.
- **Winter.** A cool rest at 8 to 10°C (46 to 50°F) at night, away from radiators.
- **Skip the misting.** Desert cacti like dry air, and the RHS links too much humidity with corky scab.
