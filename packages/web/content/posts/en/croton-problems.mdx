The most common croton problems are leaf drop and fading colour. Leaf drop usually follows a change: a new home, a move to another room, a cold draught, nights below about 15 °C (60 °F), or soil left too dry or too wet. Fading colour means too little light, since crotons need several hours of bright light a day to keep their reds, oranges and yellows. Brown, crispy edges point to dry air or dry soil, and fine speckling under the leaves to spider mites.

## Croton problems: what this plant will not forgive

The croton, *Codiaeum variegatum*, comes from the humid tropics and dislikes change. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/codiaeum/how-to-grow-codiaeum) describes it as needing high humidity and constant warmth, and says fluctuations in temperature and cold draughts make it drop leaves. The [University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/croton-codiaeum-variegatum/) adds that moving it quickly from one environment to another shocks it into dropping leaves.

So the first question for most croton problems is what changed in the last few weeks. A new spot, the heating switching on, a window opened at night, a missed watering, or a trip home from the garden centre on a cold day are all common triggers.

## Why is my croton dropping leaves?

**1. A recent move or new home.** A croton bought in spring and taken home almost always drops some leaves in its first weeks. Give it the brightest spot you have without hot afternoon sun through glass, keep it there, and wait. New leaves will come once it settles. Avoid repotting during this period. The [guide to acclimating new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) covers the first weeks.

**2. Cold or draughts.** The RHS gives a range of about 18 to 35 °C (65 to 95 °F) and says it should not drop below 15 °C (60 °F). Wisconsin notes leaf drop if it stays below 10 °C (50 °F) and advises protecting it from draughts. A plant beside an often-opened door, an air-conditioning vent or a cold window in winter loses leaves even when everything else is right.

**3. Soil too dry or too wet for too long.** Wisconsin says the croton drops leaves if kept too wet or too dry for long periods. Its advice: water when the top 1 to 2.5 cm (½ to 1 in) of soil is dry, and water less in winter. The RHS lists compost that is too dry among the causes of leaf drop. Push a finger into the soil. Dry and light means it needs a thorough drink. Wet and heavy, with yellowing lower leaves, points to overwatering.

**4. Dry air.** The RHS lists dry air as another cause. A croton beside a radiator in a heated winter room is a common case.

**5. Low light.** The RHS notes that low light can cause leaf drop as well as fading. If the plant is several metres from a window, this is likely part of the picture.

To tell them apart: a move or cold snap in the past month points to causes 1 or 2. The state of the soil confirms or rules out cause 3. Crisp brown edges alongside the drop point to dry air. Leaf drop plus dull, greener leaves points to low light.

## Why is my croton losing its colour?

Low light. Wisconsin notes that leaves revert to plain green in low light, and the RHS says low light fades the colour. New leaves on a croton in a dim room come out mostly green, and older ones lose their yellow and red.

Move it to the brightest spot you have. An east or west window is usually a good balance. A south window works if the leaves are not pressed against the glass in strong summer sun. The RHS warns that full sun through glass can scorch the leaves, and Wisconsin notes that too much direct sun turns them grey and dull. Colour returns on new leaves within a few weeks of the move, rarely on the old ones. The [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot's light.

## Why does my croton have brown, crispy edges?

**1. Dry air.** Wisconsin recommends moderate to high humidity. In a room heated through winter, the edges and tips dry out first. Group plants together, use a humidifier, or move it away from a radiator. The [humidity guide](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

**2. Soil drying out completely.** If the pot goes bone dry between waterings, edges crisp and lower leaves may fall. Water when the top 1 to 2.5 cm (½ to 1 in) is dry, not when the whole pot is.

**3. Sun scorch.** Pale or brown patches in the middle of the leaf, on the side facing the window, after a sunny spell, are sunburn. Move it back or add a sheer curtain.

**4. Hard tap water and fertiliser salts.** The RHS advises against regular use of hard tap water. A white crust on the soil or pot rim points to salt build-up. Flush the pot with plenty of water and use rainwater or filtered water if your tap water is hard.

Brown edges do not recover. Trim them following the leaf's shape if they bother you.

## Why is my croton drooping?

If the soil is dry, it is thirsty. Water slowly until water runs from the drainage hole. The leaves usually lift within hours.

If the soil is wet and the plant droops, the roots may be rotting. The RHS links root rot to waterlogging, standing water and poor drainage. Do not water. Check the roots, as below.

A croton that droops right after a cold night or a move is in shock. Keep it warm and stable and do not water unless the soil is dry.

## Why are my croton leaves turning yellow?

A few lower leaves yellowing and falling, while new leaves keep coming at the top, is normal turnover. Many yellow leaves at once usually means soggy soil or a cold spell, the same causes as leaf drop. Check the soil and the temperature. Yellow leaves with fine pale dots and webbing underneath point to spider mites.

## Does my croton have root rot?

Signs: drooping with wet soil, yellowing and dropping lower leaves, a soft stem base, and a sour smell. Slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and light-coloured. Rotten roots are dark and mushy.

To treat, cut out the rotten roots with clean scissors, then repot in fresh, free-draining mix. The RHS suggests adding grit to improve drainage. Use a pot with a drainage hole and only slightly larger than the root ball, since Wisconsin advises repotting only 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) larger. Water once and wait until the top of the soil is dry before watering again. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) has the full method.

## What pests attack crotons?

Wisconsin lists mealybugs, spider mites and scale.

- **Spider mites** do well in warm, dry rooms, the same rooms that stress crotons. Look for fine pale speckling on the upper leaf and tiny moving dots or fine webbing underneath. The [spider mite guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) covers treatment.
- **Mealybugs** are white, cottony tufts in leaf joints.
- **Scale** looks like small brown bumps on stems and leaf undersides.

[Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/common-houseplant-insects-related-pests/) notes that spraying with water helps against spider mites, that mealybugs can be dabbed off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, and that scale can be scraped off by hand. Insecticidal soap works on contact only and needs repeat treatments. Isolate the plant while you treat it.

## Handle crotons with gloves

The RHS says the sap irritates skin and eyes and that all parts of the plant are poisonous, so it advises keeping crotons away from children and pets and wearing gloves. Wisconsin also notes that the milky sap can cause dermatitis. Wear gloves when you prune or remove leaves, and wash your hands afterwards.

## Quick answers

**Will my croton grow its leaves back?** Yes, if the cause is fixed and the stems are still firm. New leaves come from the tips and from buds along the stems. Wisconsin suggests a hard prune in early spring for a leggy, bare plant.

**Should I put my croton outside in summer?** Only when nights stay above about 15 °C (60 °F). Bring it in before nights cool down, and expect some leaf drop after each move.
