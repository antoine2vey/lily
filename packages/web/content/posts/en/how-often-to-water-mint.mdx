How often to water mint depends almost entirely on the pot: water when the top 2 cm (1 in) of compost feels dry, which can mean every day for a small pot in summer sun and once a week or less on a winter windowsill. Mint likes compost that stays evenly moist, so it is one of the few herbs that you are more likely to underwater than overwater. The catch is that "moist" never means sitting in a saucer of water.

## How to tell when mint needs water

The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/herbs/mint/grow-your-own) advises adding enough water to keep the compost evenly moist, and checking containers regularly during the growing season. [University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/mint/) notes that peppermint grows best in a rich, moist soil and spearmint in a somewhat moist one. In practice:

- **Touch the compost.** Push a finger 2 cm (1 in) in. If it feels dry, water. If it feels cool and damp, check again tomorrow.
- **Lift the pot.** A pot of mint that needs water is noticeably lighter than one watered that morning. After a week of lifting you will feel the difference without thinking.
- **Watch the tips.** Mint droops quickly when it is dry, starting with the soft young shoots. A plant that droops in the afternoon sun and recovers by evening is on the edge; one that is still limp in the morning needs water now.

Water slowly until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer after about 30 minutes. A thorough soak reaches the whole root ball. A splash on top only wets the surface.

## How often to water mint through the year

Starting points for mint in a pot of multipurpose compost, about 30 cm (12 in) across. Check the compost before each watering; these are not a schedule.

| Situation | Starting interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Outdoors, summer, full sun | Every day to every 2 days | Every day in a heatwave, sometimes twice for small pots |
| Outdoors, spring and autumn | Every 3 to 5 days | Less after rain |
| Outdoors, winter | Rarely | The plant dies back; keep the compost from drying out completely |
| Indoors, sunny windowsill, summer | Every 2 to 4 days | Warm rooms dry pots quickly |
| Indoors, winter | About once a week | Slow growth in weak light |

Mint is a herbaceous perennial. The RHS explains that it dies back over winter and regrows every spring. An outdoor pot with no green growth uses very little water, so a sodden pot through a wet winter does more harm than a dry one. Water only if the compost has dried right through.

## What changes how often you water

- **Pot size.** UMD recommends a container about 30 to 40 cm (12 to 16 in) across to keep mint contained. A big pot holds more water and dries slowly. The small pots that supermarket and garden-centre mint comes in can dry out in a single hot afternoon. Our guide to [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) covers sizes and materials.
- **Pot material.** Terracotta dries much faster than plastic or glazed ceramic, which is a drawback in summer and a help in winter.
- **Sun and heat.** UMD notes that peppermint is probably most productive in full sun, but full sun dries a pot fastest. The RHS suggests moving pots to a shadier spot in hot weather to cut down on watering.
- **Wind.** A balcony or roof terrace dries pots far faster than a sheltered patio.
- **Rain.** Rain often runs off the leaves and misses a pot, especially one under eaves. Check the compost after a shower rather than assuming it is wet.
- **How crowded it is.** The RHS notes that mint in containers needs dividing and repotting every few years. A pot packed with rhizomes holds little compost and dries out within hours of watering. If it needs water twice a day, divide it.

Indoors, mint is usually on a kitchen windowsill. Our [indoor herb garden guide](/en/blog/indoor-herb-garden) covers windows and grow lights for herbs.

## Signs of underwatered mint

- Soft shoot tips droop first, then whole stems flop over the side of the pot.
- Lower leaves turn yellow, then dry and crisp.
- The compost shrinks away from the side of the pot, and water runs straight down the gap and out.
- Growth stalls and leaves stay small.

A thirsty mint usually recovers within hours of a good soak. If the compost has pulled away from the pot, stand the whole pot in a bowl of water for 20 to 30 minutes so the root ball wets right through, then let it drain.

## Signs of overwatered mint

Mint is hard to overwater in a pot with drainage holes. The trouble starts in pots without holes, in cachepots that collect water, and in saucers that are never emptied.

- Lower leaves yellow while the compost is wet.
- Stems blacken or go soft at the base.
- Compost that smells sour and stays soggy for days.
- Wilting on wet compost. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) points out that roots need oxygen, and that rotting roots cause wilting too, so a limp plant in wet compost needs less water, not more.

If the compost is waterlogged, tip out the standing water, move the plant to a pot with drainage holes, and let the surface dry before the next watering. ## Can you grow mint in water?

Yes, for a while. The RHS notes that mint stems root readily in a jar of water and can then be potted up. This is the easiest way to multiply a plant or to rescue stems from a bunch bought for the kitchen.

1. Cut a healthy stem 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) long, just below a leaf joint.
2. Strip the leaves from the lower half, so no leaves sit under water and rot.
3. Stand it in a glass of water on a bright windowsill out of harsh midday sun.
4. Change the water every 2 to 3 days to keep it clear.
5. Once the roots are a few centimetres long, pot it up in multipurpose compost and keep it moist for the first few weeks.

Mint can sit in a glass on the windowsill for weeks, but a stem in plain water has no compost to feed it and gradually weakens. Treat the glass as a nursery, not a permanent home.
