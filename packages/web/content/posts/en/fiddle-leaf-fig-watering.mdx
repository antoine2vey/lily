Water a fiddle leaf fig when the top 5 cm (2 in) of soil has dried, then soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes. In a bright room that usually means every 7 to 10 days in spring and summer and every 2 to 3 weeks in winter. Use those intervals as a starting point only: the soil decides, and a fiddle leaf fig is far more often killed by too much water than too little.

## How to tell when a fiddle leaf fig needs water

[NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ficus-lyrata/) describes *Ficus lyrata* as wanting moist, well-drained soil and being sensitive to overwatering. In practice, that means letting the top few centimetres dry before you water again, while never letting the whole root ball dry out.

Three ways to check, from most to least reliable:

- **Finger or chopstick.** Push it 5 cm (2 in) into the soil. If it comes out dry and clean, water. If soil sticks to it or it feels cool and damp, wait.
- **Moisture meter.** Push the probe halfway down the pot. Water when it reads at the dry end of "moist". Cheap meters drift, so check them against your finger now and then.
- **Pot weight.** Lift or tilt the pot right after watering and again a week later. A big fig in a heavy pot is harder to judge this way, but on smaller plants the difference is obvious.

Leaves are a poor guide. By the time a fiddle leaf fig droops from thirst it is already stressed, and drooping can also come from rotting roots.

## How often to water a fiddle leaf fig in each season

Starting intervals for a plant in a bright room, in a pot with drainage holes and ordinary houseplant compost:

| Season | Starting interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Every 7 to 10 days | Growth starts; check weekly |
| Summer | Every 7 to 10 days, sometimes 5 to 7 in a heatwave | Most light and warmth, fastest drying |
| Autumn | Every 10 to 14 days | Days shorten, growth slows |
| Winter | Every 2 to 3 weeks | Least light; the soil stays wet longest |

Winter is where most fiddle leaf figs go wrong. The plant uses far less water in short, dim days, but the heating dries the air and makes the leaves look like they want more. Keep checking the soil and let it decide. [Lily](/en) schedules watering per plant and, indoors, adjusts it for the season's daylight, so the interval stretches as the days shorten. ## How to water a fiddle leaf fig

1. Water slowly over the whole surface of the soil until it runs out of the drainage holes. A thorough soak wets the whole root ball and flushes out fertiliser salts.
2. Let it drain. Empty the saucer or cachepot after about 30 minutes. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) points out that roots need oxygen and rot when they sit in water.
3. Use water at room temperature. Clemson recommends roughly 17 to 22°C (62 to 72°F). Cold water straight from the tap can shock the roots in winter.
4. Avoid softened water. Clemson warns that the sodium in water from a domestic softener harms plants. Ordinary tap water, rainwater or filtered water are all fine.

Small, frequent sips are the habit to avoid. They keep the top of the soil wet while the bottom of the root ball stays dry, or, in a pot with no drainage, slowly turn the bottom into a swamp.

## What changes the interval

- **Light.** A fig a metre from a bright south or west window drinks much more than one across the room. Low light slows growth, and the soil stays wet longer. NC State recommends bright, indirect light with protection from afternoon sun.
- **Pot size.** A big pot holds more water and dries slowly; a pot much larger than the root ball keeps wet soil around roots that cannot use it. Go up only one size when you repot. Our guide to [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) explains why.
- **Pot material.** Terracotta breathes and dries faster than plastic or glazed ceramic.
- **Soil.** A chunky, free-draining mix with bark or perlite dries in days. Dense compost straight from the bag can stay wet for weeks.
- **Temperature.** Warm rooms dry the soil faster. NC State gives 13°C (55°F) as the minimum; below that, the plant barely uses water.
- **Plant size.** A tall fig with many leaves in a snug pot drinks faster than a young one in the same pot.

## Signs you are overwatering a fiddle leaf fig

- Brown spots in the middle of the leaves or along the edges, often starting on lower leaves, with the soil still damp.
- Lower leaves yellowing and dropping.
- Soil that smells sour or stays wet more than a week after watering.
- Fungus gnats hovering over the soil.
- Drooping on damp soil. Clemson notes that root rot also causes wilting, so a wilted plant on wet soil needs less water, not more.

If you suspect rot, slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are pale and firm; rotten ones are brown, soft and smell bad. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) explains how to trim and repot.

## Signs you are underwatering a fiddle leaf fig

- Leaves droop and feel soft, then perk up within a day after a good soak.
- Leaf edges turn light brown and crisp, starting on the newest leaves.
- The soil shrinks away from the side of the pot, and water runs straight through.
- The pot feels much lighter than usual.

NC State notes that leaf drop can come from too much or too little water, so check the soil before you act. Our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) compares the two side by side.

## Brown spots that are not about water

Not every brown patch is a watering problem. NC State links brown spots to temperature swings near heating and cooling vents, and scorch to direct sun. If the spots are only on the side facing the window or the radiator, move the plant before you change the watering.
