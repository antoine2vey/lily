How to water plants while on vacation depends on how long you are away. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/houseplants/holiday-care) says most houseplants tolerate a few days' absence without harm. For up to about a week, a thorough watering and a cooler, shadier spot are usually enough. For longer trips, give the plants a water reservoir to draw on, and beyond a couple of weeks the RHS suggests asking someone to water for you.

## How long can houseplants go without water?

It depends on the plant, the pot and the season. Cacti and succulents store water in thick leaves or stems and can go much longer than leafy tropical plants. [The University of Saskatchewan's gardening service](https://gardening.usask.ca/articles-and-lists/articles-indoor-growing/watering-houseplants-while-on-vacation.php) points out that clay pots dry out faster than plastic or glazed ones, and small pots faster than large ones. A thirsty plant in a small terracotta pot by a sunny window may need water within three or four days in summer.

A rough guide, by trip length:

| Time away | What to do |
|---|---|
| A long weekend | Water thoroughly before you leave. Nothing else for most plants. |
| Up to a week | Water thoroughly and move plants away from bright windows to a cooler spot. |
| One to two weeks | Add a reservoir: wicks, capillary matting or bottle spikes. Covers can help on short trips. |
| More than two weeks | Ask a neighbour, friend or relative to water, as the RHS suggests. |
| A winter trip of one to two weeks | The RHS says most houseplants can be left with no attention, as long as the room stays warm enough. |

## How to water plants while on vacation: what to do before you leave

1. **Water thoroughly.** USask recommends a thorough watering just before you go, whichever method you choose. Water until it runs out of the drainage hole, then let the pot drain. Do not leave it standing in a full saucer for the whole trip, since roots sitting in water for days can rot.
2. **Move plants out of the sun.** Both the RHS and USask advise moving plants to a cooler room or away from a bright window. Less light and lower temperatures mean the plant uses water more slowly.
3. **Group them together.** Plants grouped on the same mat or in the same room are easier to set up, and the RHS recommends grouping smaller pots for capillary matting.
4. **Mulch the surface.** USask suggests mulching the soil to slow water loss. A layer of gravel or bark on top of the pot does this.
5. **Test your method first.** USask advises trying it a few days before you leave, to see what works for your plants. This is where you find out that a wick does not draw or a bottle tips over.

Leave cacti and succulents out of all of this. The RHS excludes them, since they handle longer periods without water. Water them normally before you go and leave them in their usual spot.

## Wick watering for large pots

The RHS recommends this method for large single pots.

1. Fill a large container with water and put it next to the plant, or slightly higher.
2. Push one end of a wick into the reservoir and tuck the other end into the soil, a few centimetres down. The RHS uses a strip of capillary matting. USask suggests absorbent cotton string about 6 mm (1/4 in) thick, or a strip of old cotton T-shirt about 2.5 cm (1 in) wide, twisted.
3. Water the plant first so the soil is moist, which helps the wick start drawing.

The plant draws water from the reservoir as the soil dries. Use one wick for each pot, and a reservoir big enough to last the whole trip. The RHS warns that small reservoirs can run dry and wicks can slip out of pots if not tucked in well.

## Capillary matting for groups of small pots

Capillary matting is a felt-like fabric sold at garden centres. It soaks up water and passes it to the bottom of pots sitting on it. The RHS recommends it for collections of smaller pots.

1. Lay a sheet of matting on the draining board by the kitchen sink, or on a surface next to the bath.
2. Drape one end into the sink or bath, and fill it with water.
3. Stand the plants on the wet matting and press them down slightly so the compost touches the mat through the drainage holes.

Clay pots need a thorough watering first before they start drawing from the mat, the RHS notes. It also warns that matting may not work with pots that have broken crocks in the bottom, because the compost does not touch the mat.

## Bottle spikes

USask describes watering spikes that screw onto plastic drink bottles. Fill the bottle, screw on the spike and push it into the soil. Water seeps out slowly. Prop the bottle up if needed so it does not topple. Test one before you leave. The flow varies with the spike and the soil, and a spike that empties in a day does little for a two-week trip.

## Covering plants

The RHS suggests a clear cover, a propagator lid or a reused plastic bag over the plant to hold in moisture, for short absences only. The water that evaporates condenses and runs back into the pot. Keep the plastic off the leaves by supporting it with canes or chopsticks, and secure the bag around the pot with string or an elastic band, as USask describes. Keep covered plants out of direct sun, which can overheat them under plastic.

## What about self-watering pots?

Self-watering pots have a reservoir in the base that wicks water up into the soil. The RHS says they suit plants that like moisture all year, and are best used as a long-term way of growing rather than as a holiday measure. It advises against repotting a plant just for a short trip, unless you already use such pots. If your plants already live in them, fill the reservoirs before you go.

## When you get back

Do not water everything straight away. Check each pot first, because plants on wicks or matting may still be moist. Water only those that are dry, then move plants back to their usual spots. Our guide on [how often to water houseplants](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-houseplants) covers getting back into a routine.

A plant that is limp and dry usually recovers after a thorough watering. A plant that wilts in wet soil after a trip on matting or wicks may have stayed too wet.
