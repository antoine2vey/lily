Here is how to get rid of thrips on a houseplant: isolate it, rinse off as many as you can, cut away the worst leaves, then treat every 5 to 7 days for 3 to 4 weeks. One spray is never enough, because eggs sit inside leaf tissue and pupae hide in the soil, out of reach. In a warm room a new generation can go from egg to adult in about 2 weeks, according to [UC Integrated Pest Management](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/PMG/PESTNOTES/pn7429.html).

## How to recognise thrips

Adult thrips are slender insects 2 mm or smaller, according to [University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/thrips-indoor-plants). Juveniles are white or yellow and have no wings. They move fast and hide along leaf veins and in folded new growth.

The damage is easier to spot than the insects:

- **Silvery or bleached patches** on leaves, where thrips have scraped the surface and sucked out the cells.
- **Fine speckling (stippling)** on the upper side of leaves.
- **Tiny black dots** of excrement scattered on the damaged areas.
- **Twisted or scarred new leaves** and deformed flowers.

To confirm, hold a sheet of white paper under a leaf and tap the leaf sharply. Thrips fall onto the paper and show up as moving dark or pale slivers. UC IPM also recommends yellow or blue sticky traps near the plant to catch adults and tell you whether numbers are falling.

Spider mites cause similar speckling, but they leave fine webbing and no black dots. If you see webs, read the [spider mite treatment guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) instead.

## Why thrips are hard to kill

Three things about their life cycle explain why infestations come back:

- **Eggs are inserted into leaf tissue,** where sprays cannot reach them.
- **Pupae drop into the soil** or leaf litter, so a spray on the leaves misses them too.
- **Females can lay eggs without mating,** from about two dozen to a couple of hundred each, UMD says. A single thrips on a new plant can start an infestation.

Development speeds up with heat. UMD gives about a month from egg to adult at 16°C (60°F) and two weeks or less at 29°C (85°F). Treatments have to keep going long enough to catch every egg as it hatches.

## Which plants get thrips

UMD lists the indoor plants most often hit: African violets, Tradescantia, aroids such as monstera, philodendron and anthurium, orchids, peperomia, ficus, palms and herbs. New plants are the usual source, so keep any new arrival apart from the rest for a few weeks. Our guide on [how to acclimate new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) covers quarantine.

## How to get rid of thrips, step by step

1. **Isolate the plant** from all others, at least in a different room.
2. **Check its neighbours.** Inspect the plants that stood next to it, with the paper test.
3. **Remove the worst damage.** Cut off badly scarred leaves and any flowers. UMD advises discarding a heavily infested plant rather than treating it.
4. **Rinse the plant.** Spray the leaves, especially the undersides and new growth, with a strong stream of water in the shower. This knocks off a large share of adults and juveniles.
5. **Apply a treatment** (see below), covering both sides of every leaf and the stems.
6. **Repeat every 5 to 7 days for 3 to 4 weeks.** This catches each wave of juveniles as they hatch from eggs inside the leaves.
7. **Watch the sticky trap.** Stop when the trap stays empty for two weeks and new leaves come in clean.

Repotting into fresh soil removes pupae in the old soil, but UMD notes it does nothing about the eggs and juveniles on the plant, so it is a complement to spraying, not a substitute.

## Treatments that work

- **Insecticidal soap and horticultural oil** kill thrips they touch and are gentle on most houseplants. UC IPM notes they have no lasting effect, so coverage and repeats matter. Use a product made for plants, not dish soap, which UMD warns can damage leaves.
- **Azadirachtin,** the active compound in neem products, is also on UMD's list. Like soap and oil, it needs repeat applications.
- **Spinosad** is a product derived from a soil bacterium. UC IPM rates it as more effective against thrips than soap or oil.
- **Biological controls.** UMD lists the fungi Beauveria bassiana and Metarhizium, the predatory mite Amblyseius cucumeris on leaves, and the nematode Steinernema feltiae in the soil to target pupae. These suit large collections or greenhouses more than a single plant.

UC IPM calls systemic imidacloprid "generally not recommended" for thrips, and its advice for any infestation is to combine methods rather than rely on one product.

Test any spray on one leaf first and wait 2 days before treating the whole plant. Spray out of direct sun, since oils and soaps can burn leaves in strong light.

## How to keep thrips away

- Inspect new plants at the shop and quarantine them at home.
- Check under leaves and in new growth each time you water.
- Keep a yellow sticky trap near susceptible plants as an early warning.
- Rinse dusty foliage in the shower every month or two.
- UMD notes that high humidity may discourage thrips. The [humidity guide](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) shows how to raise it.

Keeping inspections regular is the hard part. [Lily](/en) reminds you to water each plant, which is a natural moment to look under a few leaves. For the other insects that turn up indoors, see the [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) guide.
