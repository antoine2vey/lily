Basil care is mostly about warmth and sun: give it at least 6 hours of direct sun a day, keep it above 10°C (50°F) at night, and water in the morning once the top 2 cm (1 in) of compost has dried. Pick the shoot tips every week or so and remove flower stems as soon as they appear, and one plant keeps producing leaves all summer. Most basil that dies on a kitchen windowsill was too cold, too dark, or sitting in wet compost overnight.

## Basil care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | 6 or more hours of direct sun. Indoors, the sunniest window you have |
| Water | When the top 2 cm (1 in) is dry, in the morning, at the base of the plant |
| Humidity | Ordinary room air. Keep the leaves dry rather than misting them |
| Temperature | Above 10°C (50°F) at night; it grows best around 20°C (68°F) and warmer |
| Soil | Peat-free multipurpose compost in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Balanced liquid fertiliser at label strength, roughly monthly, for pots only |
| Toxicity | Non-toxic to cats and dogs |

Basil (*Ocimum basilicum*) is a tender plant from tropical Africa and Asia. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/herbs/basil/grow-your-own) explains that it needs so much warmth and sunlight that it is grown as an annual: you sow or buy it in spring, harvest through summer, and start again the next year. There is no winter dormancy to manage, only a plant that slows down and gets weaker as light fades.

## How much sun does basil need?

[NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ocimum-basilicum/) lists full sun, meaning 6 or more hours of direct sunlight a day, with part shade tolerated. Outdoors, choose a warm, sheltered, sunny spot. Indoors, a south- or west-facing windowsill is the best you can do, and even there basil grows slower than outside.

Signs it needs more light:

- Stems stretch long and thin between pairs of leaves.
- New leaves are small and pale.
- The plant leans hard toward the glass.

If your only window is north-facing, a small LED grow light a few centimetres above the plant for 14 to 16 hours a day makes more difference than anything else on this page. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a window.

## How often should you water basil?

Water when the top 2 cm (1 in) of compost feels dry, then water until it runs from the drainage hole and empty the saucer. In summer, a small pot on a sunny sill can need water every day or two; a large pot outdoors, every two or three days. Check with a finger rather than going by the calendar.

Two details matter more than the interval. Water in the morning: the RHS notes that basil hates having wet roots overnight. And water the compost, not the leaves, because wet foliage and cool nights are how grey mould and downy mildew get started. Basil left to dry out completely tends to flower early, which ends the harvest. Our guide on [how often to water basil](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-basil) covers pot size, season and the signs of too much and too little.

## How to keep supermarket basil alive

A supermarket pot is not one plant. It is dozens of seedlings packed together for a few weeks of picking. The RHS warns that these crowded pots tend to rot or flower quickly, but they can survive if you divide and repot them promptly.

1. Slide the root ball out of its pot.
2. Pull or cut it gently into three or four clumps.
3. Pot each clump in its own 12 to 15 cm (5 to 6 in) pot of fresh multipurpose compost.
4. Water once, let it drain, and keep the pots on your sunniest sill.

Each clump now has room and air around its stems, and grows into a bushier plant.

## How to pinch basil so it doesn't flower

Harvesting is pruning. Pinch or snip each stem just above a pair of leaves, taking the top few centimetres. Two new shoots grow from the leaf joints below the cut, so the plant gets bushier each time you pick. Take a few tips from every stem rather than stripping one bare.

Basil usually starts flowering in mid to late summer. As soon as you see a flower spike forming at a stem tip, pinch it out with the pair of leaves below it. The RHS notes that flowering affects the flavour of the leaves; removing the spikes keeps the plant in leaf for longer. Toward the end of the season you can let one plant flower for the bees. The flowers are edible too.

## Does basil need fertiliser?

Basil planted in the ground usually needs no feeding. In a pot, it uses up the nutrients in the compost within a couple of months. Feed potted basil with a balanced liquid fertiliser, at the strength on the label, about once a month from late spring to the end of summer.

Avoid tomato feed and other high-potassium fertilisers. The RHS points out that they encourage flowering, which is the opposite of what you want from basil. More fertiliser is not better either: overfed basil grows soft, sappy leaves and salt builds up in the pot. Our guide to [fertilising houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) explains how to read the numbers on the bottle.

## Can you grow basil indoors in winter?

You can, but it is hard work. Winter days are short and a windowsill gets a fraction of summer light, so basil stretches and fades. Expect slow growth and small harvests unless you use a grow light. Keep it away from cold glass at night and from the dry heat right above a radiator. Water less often, only when the top of the compost is dry, because a slow plant in cool compost rots easily.

Many people find it simpler to keep a few cuttings going through winter, or to sow a fresh pot in early spring. Basil seeds germinate well at about 20°C (68°F). Our [indoor herb garden guide](/en/blog/indoor-herb-garden) covers which herbs cope best on a winter sill.

## How to propagate basil in water

Basil roots easily from cuttings, which is the cheapest way to get more plants.

1. Cut a non-flowering shoot 8 to 10 cm (3 to 4 in) long, just below a leaf joint.
2. Strip the lower leaves so no leaf sits in water, and pinch out the tip.
3. Stand the cutting in a glass of water on a bright windowsill out of harsh midday sun.
4. Change the water every two or three days.
5. When the roots are 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) long, usually after one to two weeks, pot the cutting in multipurpose compost and keep it moist for a week while it settles.

Our guide to [propagating plants from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers the same method for other herbs and houseplants.

## Common basil problems

- **Black or brown patches after a cold night:** cold damage. Bring it inside or move it away from cold glass.
- **Grey fuzz on stems and leaves:** grey mould, usually on crowded, damp plants. Remove affected parts, space plants out, and water at the base in the morning.
- **Yellow patches on top of the leaves with grey-purple fuzz underneath:** basil downy mildew. NC State lists it as a main disease of basil and recommends resistant cultivars such as 'Prospera'. Infected plants rarely recover, so pull them out.
- **Sticky leaves and clusters of small insects on shoot tips:** aphids. Rinse them off and pinch out the worst tips.
- **Wilting on wet compost:** the roots are rotting. Let the pot dry out and check the roots.

Each of these has its causes and fixes in our article on [basil problems](/en/blog/basil-problems).

## Is basil safe for cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/basil) lists basil as non-toxic to cats and dogs. A pet that chews it may still vomit a little, as with any plant material, but basil is not poisonous to them.
