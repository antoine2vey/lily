Most basil problems come from one of four things: soil kept too wet, temperatures below 10°C (50°F), too little sun, or basil downy mildew. Yellow leaves usually point to overwatering or downy mildew, drooping to thirst or rotting roots, and blackened leaves to cold. Basil needs at least 6 hours of direct sun a day, warm air and soil that stays lightly moist but drains freely.

The sections below take each symptom in turn, with the likely causes listed from most to least common.

## Why are my basil leaves turning yellow?

**1. Too much water or poor drainage.** The most common cause indoors. The lower leaves yellow first and go soft, the soil stays wet for days and the stems may darken at the base. Check that the pot has a drainage hole and that it is not sitting in a full saucer or a supermarket sleeve. Let the top 2 cm (1 in) dry before watering again.

**2. Basil downy mildew.** If the yellowing starts on the lower leaves, spreads between the veins and you see grey-purple fuzz on the undersides, it is downy mildew. See the section below.

**3. Not enough light.** Pale, yellow-green leaves on long, thin stems mean the plant is starving for light. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/growing-herbs-indoors) says herbs need at least 6 hours of direct sun indoors. A south-facing window is best, or fluorescent or LED grow lights 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in) above the plants for 14 to 16 hours a day.

**4. Hungry plant.** Basil in a small pot that has been harvested for weeks can run out of nutrients. The older leaves turn evenly pale yellow. Penn State recommends a low dose of fertiliser every 2 weeks for indoor herbs.

**5. Natural ageing.** A few of the lowest leaves yellowing on an otherwise healthy, growing plant is normal. Pick them off.

## Why are the undersides of my basil leaves grey or fuzzy?

That is almost certainly basil downy mildew, a disease caused by Peronospora belbahrii. According to [University of Wisconsin Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/basil-downy-mildew/), the first sign is yellowing of the lower leaves, often limited by the veins, followed by browning, curling and wilting. On the underside of the leaves you see a fuzzy grey to purple growth, which is the spores.

How to tell it apart from overwatering: turn a yellow leaf over. Fuzz on the underside means downy mildew. A clean underside with wet, sour-smelling soil means a watering problem.

**What to do.** Wisconsin is clear that there is no cure. Harvest any leaves that still look clean and use them right away, then bag and bin the infected plants. Do not compost them. The disease spreads by wind and on infected seed and transplants, and it favours warm, humid conditions with wet leaves.

**Preventing it next time:**

- Water the soil, not the leaves, and water in the morning.
- Space plants so air moves between them.
- Grow resistant varieties. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ocimum-basilicum/) lists 'Prospera' and 'Rutgers Obsession DMR'. Wisconsin notes that purple, Thai, lemon and spice basils are less susceptible, and suggests trying 'Eleonora'.

## Why is my basil drooping or wilting?

**1. Thirst.** Basil wilts quickly when the soil dries out, especially in a small pot in a sunny window. If the soil is dry and the plant perks up within an hour of watering, that was it. Penn State advises never letting the mix dry out completely. A pot this size may need water every day or two in summer. Our article on [how often to water basil](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-basil) sets out intervals by season and pot size.

**2. Root rot.** If the soil is wet and the plant still droops, the roots are probably rotting and cannot take up water. See the root rot section below.

**3. Heat or transplant shock.** A plant that droops in the afternoon sun and recovers by evening is losing water faster than its roots can supply. Move it out of the hottest midday sun or into a bigger pot. Freshly repotted basil can also sulk for a few days.

**4. Fusarium wilt.** NC State lists Fusarium wilt as one of basil's two main diseases. The plant wilts even in moist soil, often on one side first, and the inside of the stem often shows brown streaks. There is no treatment. Bin the plant and its soil, and choose a resistant variety such as 'Newton' next time.

## Why is my basil turning black?

**1. Cold.** Basil is very sensitive to cold. University of Illinois Extension notes that cold air and soil can blacken basil leaves at 10°C (50°F), and Penn State says basil cannot survive temperatures lower than that. Leaves touching a cold window pane in winter, a draughty spot, or a night outside in spring are typical causes. The black patches will not recover. Pinch them off and keep the plant above 15°C (59°F).

**2. Downy mildew.** In later stages the yellowed leaves turn brown to black. Check the undersides for fuzz.

**3. Bacterial or fungal leaf spots.** Dark spots with a water-soaked look usually follow overhead watering and humid air. Remove affected leaves and water the soil only.

## How do you tell if basil has root rot?

The signs are drooping in wet soil, yellowing lower leaves, a dark, soft base to the stems and a sour smell from the pot. Healthy basil roots are white or cream and firm. Rotten roots are brown or black and mushy, and the outer skin slips off when you pull them.

Root rot comes from soil that stays wet. Penn State notes that excess moisture leads to root rot in indoor herbs. Supermarket basil is especially prone, because it is sold as a dense clump of seedlings in a tiny pot with no room for air.

**To save it:**

1. Take the plant out of its pot and rinse the roots.
2. Cut away every brown, soft root with clean scissors.
3. If the clump is crowded, split it into 3 or 4 smaller clumps.
4. Repot in fresh, free-draining herb or potting mix in pots with drainage holes.
5. Water once, then wait until the top 2 cm (1 in) is dry before watering again.

If the stems are soft right up from the base, take cuttings instead. Basil roots easily from 10 cm (4 in) tip cuttings in a glass of water. Our guide to [root rot recovery](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) has more detail.

## Why is my basil leggy and flowering?

**1. Too little light.** Long gaps between leaf pairs and thin, floppy stems mean the plant is stretching toward the light. Give it more sun or a grow light, as above.

**2. It is not being pinched.** NC State and Illinois both recommend pinching off the growing tips and the flower buds as soon as they appear. Once basil flowers, it puts its energy into seed, and the leaves become smaller and less flavourful.

**How to pinch:** cut each stem just above a pair of leaves, leaving at least two pairs below. Two new shoots grow from that point, making the plant bushier. Harvest this way every week or two in summer. Our [basil care guide](/en/blog/basil-care-guide) explains pruning in more detail.

## Basil problems caused by pests

- **Aphids.** NC State lists them as an occasional pest. Small green or black insects cluster on the soft tips and the undersides of leaves, leaving sticky residue. Rinse them off with a strong spray of water, or wipe them off by hand.
- **Fungus gnats.** Tiny black flies around the soil mean the soil is staying too wet. Their larvae live in the top layer of damp soil. Penn State recommends yellow sticky traps, and letting the surface dry out between waterings breaks their cycle.
- **Spider mites.** Fine speckling on the leaves and webbing at the tips, especially in warm, dry indoor air. Rinse the plant and raise the humidity around it.
- **Whiteflies.** Tiny white insects that fly up when you touch the plant. Yellow sticky traps help.
- **Slugs and Japanese beetles** outdoors. NC State mentions Japanese beetles in parts of the US.

Since you eat the leaves, avoid any spray not labelled for edible herbs, and rinse leaves well before using them. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers identification.
