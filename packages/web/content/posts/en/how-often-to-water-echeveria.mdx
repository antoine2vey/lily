How often to water echeveria depends on how fast its soil dries, but a good starting point indoors is a thorough soak every 7 to 14 days in spring and summer and every 3 to 4 weeks, or even less, in winter. Water only when the soil is dry all the way through, then let the excess drain away. More echeverias die from too much water than from too little.

## How to tell when an echeveria needs water

Echeverias store water in their thick leaves, so the soil should dry out completely between waterings. Check it before you water, not by the calendar.

- **Finger or stick test.** Push a finger or a wooden skewer to the bottom of the pot. If it comes out with damp soil stuck to it, wait.
- **Weight.** Lift the pot right after watering and again a few days later. A dry pot of gritty mix is noticeably lighter.
- **The leaves.** A thirsty echeveria's lower leaves go slightly soft and thin, and may wrinkle a little. They plump back up within a day or two of watering. Wait for that slight softening if you are unsure.

The [RHS guide to growing cacti and succulents indoors](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/cacti-succulents/houseplants/growing-guide) describes the same rhythm: water thoroughly once the compost surface has dried out in spring and summer, let it drain, and never leave the pot standing in water.

## How often to water echeveria in each season

These are starting intervals for an echeveria in a gritty mix, in a pot with a drainage hole, on a bright windowsill. Adjust them once you have seen how fast your pot dries.

| Season | Starting interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Every 10 to 14 days | Growth restarts as days lengthen |
| Summer | Every 7 to 14 days | Faster in a hot, sunny window or outdoors |
| Autumn | Every 2 to 3 weeks | Stretch the gap as light fades |
| Winter | Every 3 to 4 weeks, or less | Only enough to stop the leaves shrivelling |

Winter is where most mistakes happen. The RHS recommends a cool winter rest at around 8 to 10°C (46 to 50°F) at night, with watering only to prevent shrivelling. In a cool room, that can mean one small drink a month. In a warm, heated room the plant keeps growing slowly and dries a little faster, but it still needs much less than in summer.

## What changes how often you water

**Light.** An echeveria in full sun uses water far faster than one in a dim spot. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/echeveria/) notes it grows well in full sun and that its colour is better with more sun. In low light it also stretches, and wet soil stays wet for days.

**Pot.** Terracotta breathes and dries out much faster than plastic or glazed ceramic. A pot without a drainage hole is the single biggest risk, because water pools at the bottom where you cannot see it. Choose a pot only slightly wider than the rosette.

**Soil.** NC State and the RHS both call for very well-drained, gritty soil. Use a cactus and succulent mix, or regular potting compost with about half its volume of grit, pumice or perlite. Plain compost holds water for a week or more. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains the mixes.

**Temperature and season.** Warmth and long days speed up drying, cold and short days slow it. That is why a fixed weekly routine that works in July can rot the plant in December. [Lily](/en) adjusts each plant's watering interval for the season's daylight indoors, and for rain and heat outdoors, so the gap stretches as the days shorten.

**Outdoors.** In summer, outdoor echeverias in pots dry fast in sun and wind but also catch rain. Check after a dry, hot week, and skip watering after heavy rain. NC State notes echeveria is not frost hardy, so bring it in before the first frost.

## How to water an echeveria

1. Check the soil is dry all the way down.
2. Water the soil, not the rosette. Pour slowly around the edge of the pot until water runs out of the drainage hole. Water left sitting in the centre of the rosette can rot the crown.
3. Let it drain for 10 to 15 minutes, then empty the saucer or cachepot.
4. Do not water again until the soil is fully dry.

Bottom watering also works: stand the pot in 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of water for 10 to 20 minutes, then take it out and let it drain.

Never mist an echeveria. It does not need humidity, and water sitting on the leaves and in the rosette encourages rot and leaf spots. Some old care advice recommends misting succulents; it does not help them.

## Signs of overwatering

Overwatering is the most common way echeverias die. The RHS lists rot and oedema as the main problems caused by too much water. Look for:

- Lower leaves that turn yellow, translucent or mushy and fall off at a touch.
- A stem that goes black or soft at the soil line.
- Leaves that swell, split or develop corky, raised bumps (oedema), which happens when roots take up water faster than the leaves can use it.
- Soil that smells sour and stays wet for a week or more.

What to do: stop watering and let the soil dry completely. If the stem is soft or black, unpot the plant, cut away rotten roots and stem, and let it callus for a few days before replanting in dry gritty mix. If the stem is rotted through, remove a few healthy leaves or the top of the rosette and propagate them. Our guide to [root rot recovery](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) walks through the steps.

## Signs of underwatering

Underwatering is rarer and easy to fix. Look for:

- Wrinkled, thin, soft leaves across the whole rosette, not just the oldest ones.
- Leaves that curl inward.
- The very lowest leaves drying to crisp brown and shrivelling.

Losing one or two of the oldest leaves at the base now and then is normal; NC State recommends simply removing withered lower leaves. A thorough soak fixes true thirst, with the leaves plumping up within a day or two. If they stay wrinkled after watering, the roots may be damaged, which points back to rot.
