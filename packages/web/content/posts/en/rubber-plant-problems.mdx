Most rubber plant problems trace back to two things: soil that stays wet too long, and a sudden change of temperature or place. Wet soil yellows the leaves and rots the roots. A cold draught, a drop below 13 °C (55 °F) or a move to a darker room makes the plant shed leaves, sometimes within days. Diseases are rare indoors, so check watering, light and temperature before you look for anything else.

## Rubber plant problems: check these three things first

Before you treat a symptom, look at three things. Push a finger 5 cm (2 in) into the soil. Damp at that depth, a week after watering, means it stays wet too long. Then check the pot has a drainage hole and the saucer is empty. Last, think back over the past month. Did the plant move, did the heating come on, did it sit next to a door or window through a cold night?

[Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/rubber-plant/) puts it bluntly: indoors, too much or too little water plus insects and mites cause most problems, and diseases are rare. The rubber plant, *Ficus elastica*, is also sensitive to being moved. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ficus-elastica/) notes that it dislikes being moved, draughts and cold.

## Why are my rubber plant leaves turning yellow?

**1. The soil stays wet.** This is the most common cause. Clemson notes that leaves turn yellow when the soil stays too wet. The yellowing usually starts on lower and middle leaves, the leaves feel soft rather than crisp, and the soil is still damp days after watering. Let the top 5 cm (2 in) dry before you water again, and water less often in autumn and winter, when the plant grows slowly. If yellowing continues while the soil is wet, check the roots (see the root rot section below).

**2. Old leaves are reaching the end of their life.** NC State notes that a few bottom leaves yellowing and dropping is normal. One lower leaf at a time, with healthy new growth at the top, is not a problem.

**3. Too little light.** In a dim corner the plant cannot support all its leaves, so it yellows and drops the oldest ones. New leaves come out smaller and further apart. Move it within 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft) of a bright window.

**4. It needs feeding.** A plant that has sat in the same mix for years, with pale yellow-green older leaves and slow growth, may be short of nutrients. Clemson suggests feeding every 2 weeks in spring and summer with a houseplant fertiliser, less often in low light. Only feed a plant whose roots are healthy.

Yellow leaves will not turn green again. Cut them off once you have found the cause. The [yellow leaves guide](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) covers the same causes across other houseplants.

## Why is my rubber plant dropping leaves?

Leaf drop on a rubber plant often looks dramatic: green, healthy-looking leaves falling over a week or two. The causes, roughly in order:

**1. A change of place or temperature.** A plant just brought home from the shop, moved to another room, or put next to a door that opens onto a cold street will often drop leaves. NC State lists temperature drops and cold draughts among the main causes. Clemson gives an ideal range of 16 to 18 °C (60 to 65 °F) at night and 24 to 27 °C (75 to 80 °F) by day, and advises keeping it above 13 °C (55 °F). Give it a stable spot and wait. A healthy plant usually stops dropping once it settles. The [guide to acclimating new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) covers the first weeks.

**2. Overwatering.** NC State also names overwatering. Leaves usually yellow first, then drop, and the soil is damp.

**3. Too little light.** In low light, the plant sheds older leaves it can no longer support. The stem slowly becomes bare from the bottom up.

**4. Dry air or dry soil.** Clemson lists dry air as a possible cause of leaf loss, along with low light and cold draughts. If the soil dried out completely, leaves droop first, then the lower ones fall. A radiator right beside the plant combines both problems.

To tell them apart: a recent move or cold snap points to cause 1. Wet soil points to cause 2. A dark spot and leggy growth point to cause 3. Bone-dry soil or a radiator nearby points to cause 4.

## Why does my rubber plant have brown spots or brown edges?

**Brown, crisp edges and tips** usually mean the soil dried out completely, or salts from fertiliser built up in the pot. A white crust on the soil or around the drainage hole points to salts. Flush the pot: water slowly with about twice the pot's volume, let it drain, and feed less afterwards.

**Large brown or bleached patches in the middle of the leaf**, on the side facing the window, are sunburn. NC State recommends bright indirect light with protection from afternoon sun. The patches stay. Move the plant back from the glass or behind a sheer curtain.

**Dark brown, soft patches, often near the leaf base, with wet soil** go with root rot. Check the roots.

**Brown spots after a cold night** on leaves that touched a window are cold damage. Move the plant 20 to 30 cm (8 to 12 in) from the glass in winter.

## Why is my rubber plant drooping?

If the soil is dry, it is thirsty. Water slowly until water runs from the drainage hole, and the leaves should lift within a day.

If the soil is wet and the plant droops anyway, do not water. Roots sitting in wet soil run short of air and rot. Rotten roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts as if it were dry. Clemson's [watering factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) warns about exactly this: wilting can be a sign of root rot, and watering makes it worse.

## How do I know if my rubber plant has root rot?

Clemson links root rot to a slow-draining mix or watering too often. Signs above the soil: yellowing and dropping leaves, drooping with wet soil, and a sour smell from the pot. To confirm, slide the plant out. Healthy roots are firm and light-coloured. Rotten roots are dark, soft and slimy, and their outer layer slides off when you pull gently.

If you find rot:

1. Remove the old mix and cut every soft, dark root back to firm tissue with clean scissors.
2. Repot in fresh, fast-draining mix in a pot with a drainage hole. Use the same pot size or smaller if many roots were removed.
3. Water once, then wait until the top 5 cm (2 in) is dry before watering again.
4. Keep it in bright, indirect light and do not feed it for a month or two.

If most of the roots are gone, take a cutting from the healthy top and root it instead. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) has more detail.

## What pests attack rubber plants?

NC State lists mealybugs, scale, spider mites and thrips.

- **Mealybugs** look like small tufts of white cotton in leaf joints and along the veins.
- **Scale** looks like small brown bumps on the stems and the undersides of leaves. Both leave sticky honeydew on the leaves below.
- **Spider mites** leave fine pale speckling on the leaves and, when numbers are high, fine webbing.
- **Thrips** leave silvery streaks with tiny black dots.

For light infestations, [Clemson's houseplant insects factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/common-houseplant-insects-related-pests/) recommends dabbing mealybugs with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, and scraping scale off by hand. Insecticidal soap kills on contact only, so repeat treatments are needed. Isolate the plant until the pests are gone. The [common houseplant pests guide](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers each pest, and the [spider mite guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) covers mites.

A quick note on handling. Clemson warns that the plant's milky white sap may irritate skin and the stomach. Wear gloves when you prune, and keep it away from pets that chew leaves.

## Why is my rubber plant leggy or leaning?

A tall bare stem with leaves only at the top, wide gaps between leaves, and a lean toward the window all point to too little light. Move it to a brighter spot and turn the pot a quarter turn every week or two.

To make it bushier, cut the stem back in late winter or spring, just above a node. New shoots usually grow from one or more buds below the cut. The cut top can be rooted as a cutting. Clemson notes it can also be propagated by air layering, which works well for tall stems.

## Quick answers

**Will the leaves grow back on a bare stem?** Not where they fell. New growth comes from the top or from buds below a pruning cut.

**Should I wipe the leaves?** Yes. Clemson recommends washing dusty leaves. Dust cuts the light reaching the leaf. Use a damp cloth and skip leaf shine.

**Is a rubber plant toxic to cats and dogs?** Treat it as a risk. Clemson advises keeping it away from pets because the sap can irritate the stomach.
