Jade plant care is simple once you know what it is: a South African succulent that wants 4 or more hours of direct sun a day, a deep watering only when the soil has dried out, and a fast-draining succulent mix. It copes with dry indoor air and a wide range of temperatures. Most jade plants that struggle are kept too dark or watered on a schedule instead of when the soil is dry.

## Jade plant care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | 4 or more hours of direct sun; a south- or west-facing window indoors |
| Water | Water deeply when the soil is dry; much less from autumn to late winter |
| Humidity | Any normal indoor level. No misting needed |
| Temperature | Average to cool room temperatures; keep it frost-free |
| Soil | Cactus and succulent mix, or potting mix with extra perlite or sharp sand |
| Feeding | Every two months in spring and summer, or a dilute feed more often |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats, dogs and horses |

Jade plant, *Crassula ovata*, is native to South Africa. Outdoors in frost-free climates it becomes a shrub of 0.9 to 1.8 m (3 to 6 ft), according to [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/crassula-ovata/). Indoors it grows slowly into a small, thick-trunked tree.

## How much light does a jade plant need?

A lot. The [University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/jade-plant-crassula-ovata/) says jade plants need four or more hours of direct sun, though they survive in bright, indirect light. Indoors, the best spot is right at a south- or west-facing window. NC State adds that some shade from the hottest afternoon sun is welcome, which matters most behind glass in summer.

Signs of too little light: leaves turn a deep, dark green, stems grow long and thin, and branches droop or lean toward the window. A jade in low light also stays wet longer after watering, which makes rot more likely.

Red or purple leaf edges, on the other hand, are a normal reaction to strong sun and not a problem. Bleached or brown patches that appear after a sudden move into full sun are sunburn. If you put a jade outdoors for summer, move it into direct sun gradually over a week or two, and bring it back in before the first frost.

## How often should you water a jade plant?

Water deeply, then let the soil dry out before you water again. Wisconsin's advice is to water more during spring and summer and keep the soil on the dry side in winter. NC State says the same: water in moderation when the soil is dry, and reduce watering from fall to late winter.

To check, push a finger or a wooden skewer a few centimetres into the soil. If it is dry at that depth, pour water slowly until it runs from the drainage hole, then empty the saucer. In a sunny window that might be every 2 to 3 weeks in summer and once a month or less in winter, but the soil decides. Our guide on [how often to water a jade plant](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-jade-plant) covers what changes the interval.

The leaves tell you which way you went wrong:

- **Overwatered:** leaves turn yellow, soft or translucent and drop, and the stem can go soft at the base. Wisconsin lists leaf drop and stem rot as the result of overwatering.
- **Underwatered:** leaves lose their plumpness, wrinkle and may spot or fall. They firm up again within a few days of a thorough watering.

## Does a jade plant need humidity?

No. Wisconsin notes that jade tolerates a wide range of humidity. Normal indoor air, including dry winter air with the heating on, is fine. Misting and pebble trays do nothing useful for a succulent, and water left sitting on the leaves in a cool room can encourage disease. Good airflow matters more than humidity.

## Temperature and winter care

Jade does well at average to cool indoor temperatures. It tolerates a wide range and may survive a light frost, but a hard freeze kills it, so keep it frost-free in winter. A bright room that is a little cooler in winter, with less water, suits it well. Keep it away from radiators, which dry the soil fast and can scorch leaves.

## Best soil and pot for a jade plant

Use a high-porosity mix made for cacti and succulents, or add extra perlite or sharp sand to a standard potting mix, as Wisconsin recommends. About one part perlite or grit to two parts mix is a sensible starting point. The pot needs a drainage hole.

Jade plants grow top-heavy with age. A terracotta pot is heavier than plastic, dries faster, and keeps a tall plant from tipping over. Pick a pot only slightly wider than the root ball. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains why a big pot of wet mix causes root rot.

Repot every two to three years, or when the plant becomes top-heavy, at the start of new growth in spring. If you want to keep the same pot, trim some roots before replanting. Water sparingly for the first couple of weeks while the roots settle.

## Feeding a jade plant

Feed only during active growth in spring and summer. Wisconsin suggests feeding every two months, or using a dilute fertiliser more often. A balanced houseplant or succulent fertiliser works. Give nothing in autumn and winter. Overfeeding produces soft, stretched growth and salt build-up in the soil.

## How to prune a jade plant

Prune in spring. Cut a stem back to just above a side branch or a pair of leaves. Wisconsin notes that the cuts heal within a few days and new growth appears within a few weeks, usually as two new shoots from the cut. That is how you turn a lanky plant into a dense, branching one. Remove any branches that cross or make the plant lopsided. For general technique, see our guide on [how to prune houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-prune-houseplants). Keep the pieces you cut off: they make cuttings.

## How to propagate a jade plant

Jade roots easily from stem or leaf cuttings, best in late spring and summer.

1. Take a stem cutting 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) long, or twist off a whole, healthy leaf.
2. Leave it in a dry, shaded place for a few days so the cut surface heals over. Wisconsin's advice is a few days; a thicker stem can take a week.
3. Insert the cut end into fairly dry, well-drained succulent mix. A leaf can simply lie on top.
4. Keep it in bright light without harsh sun. Water lightly once it has rooted, which takes a few weeks. You can tell it has roots when it resists a gentle tug.

Stem cuttings make a bigger plant faster than leaves. Rooting in water also works but tends to make fragile roots that struggle when moved to soil. Our [plant propagation guide](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers cuttings in more detail.

## How to make a jade plant flower

Jade can produce small white to pink star-shaped flowers, usually in winter, but only a mature plant that gets the right signals. Wisconsin describes the method: in autumn, cut back on water, keep the plant around 13°C (55°F) at night, and give it long nights with no artificial light in the evening. After several weeks of this cool, dry, dark treatment, return to normal watering and flowers should follow. A plant kept warm and lit in a living room all winter rarely blooms, which is why NC State notes it does not usually bloom in homes.

## Common jade plant problems

- **Leaves dropping:** overwatering is the most common cause; drought and a sudden change of place can also do it.
- **Wrinkled leaves:** usually thirst, sometimes rotten roots that can no longer take up water. Check the roots if watering does not help.
- **White, cottony spots in leaf joints:** mealybugs, the most common jade pest. Wipe them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol and repeat every few days for several weeks.
- **Fine webbing and stippled leaves:** spider mites.
- **Leggy, floppy growth:** not enough light.

Wisconsin warns that many insecticides damage jade leaves, so test any product on one leaf first. Our article on [jade plant problems](/en/blog/jade-plant-problems) goes through each symptom. ## Is a jade plant toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/jade-plant) lists jade plant as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. Signs include vomiting, depression and incoordination. The toxic compound is not known. Keep it out of reach of pets that chew plants, and see our list of [plants safe for cats](/en/blog/plants-safe-for-cats) for alternatives.
