How to choose a pot for your plant comes down to three things: a drainage hole, a size only 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider than the current root ball, and a material that matches how fast the plant likes to dry out. Clay dries quickly and suits succulents and cacti. Plastic and glazed ceramic hold moisture longer and suit plants that like to stay damp. Skip the layer of gravel at the bottom, which makes drainage worse, not better.

## Start with a drainage hole

Every pot that holds soil needs a hole in the bottom. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/repotting-houseplants) calls drainage holes necessary and suggests drilling one if a pot you love has none. Without a hole, water collects at the bottom, the roots sit in it, and they rot.

If you want to use a decorative pot without a hole, use it as a cachepot. Plant into a plain pot with drainage, then set that inside the decorative one. Penn State suggests leaving some space below the inner pot and emptying any water that collects there after watering.

## Choose the right size

Go up only a little. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-transplanting-repotting/) recommends a new pot 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) larger in diameter than the old one, and Penn State also says the new pot should be only slightly larger.

A pot that is too big causes problems. The extra soil holds water that the roots cannot reach, so the mix stays wet for days around a small root ball. That is a common path to root rot. A plant that looks small in its new pot is fine. It will fill the space.

A pot that is too small shows itself too. Clemson lists the signs that a plant needs a bigger pot:

- Roots form a solid mass or grow out of the drainage hole.
- The plant needs water much more often than it used to.
- It wilts soon after watering.

Penn State adds roots circling the inside of the pot and a dense mat of roots in the bottom third. Clemson notes that fast-growing plants usually need repotting every year and slow growers every 2 to 3 years. The [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) covers the steps.

## Choose the material

The material changes how fast the soil dries, which changes how often you water.

| Material | Dries | Weight | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unglazed clay (terracotta) | Fast | Heavy | Succulents, cacti, plants that rot easily |
| Plastic | Slow | Light | Ferns, calatheas, plants that like steady moisture |
| Glazed ceramic | Slow | Heavy | Same as plastic, with more weight for tall plants |
| Fibreglass | Slow | Light | Large plants you need to move |
| Stone or concrete | Varies | Very heavy | Tall, top-heavy plants and outdoor pots |

**Clay.** Penn State explains that clay is porous and pulls water and salts out of the mix, so plants in clay need water more often. That makes terracotta a good fit for succulents and cacti. The [cactus care guide](/en/blog/cactus-care-guide) covers those. Clemson's [watering factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) makes the same point: water evaporates from the sides of a porous clay pot, so it needs watering more often than a glazed or plastic one.

**Plastic.** Light, cheap and durable. Penn State says plastic suits plants that like to stay moist. Since it holds water longer, take extra care not to overwater.

**Heavy materials.** Penn State notes that stone and concrete pots are heavy and less likely to tip over, which helps with a tall plant in a light mix. Plastic and fibreglass are easier to move.

Whatever you choose, check the soil before watering. A plant moved from plastic to clay will need water sooner, and one moved from clay to plastic later. The [watering guide](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-houseplants) explains how to check.

## Skip the gravel layer

Many guides still say to put gravel, broken pots or stones in the bottom of a pot "for drainage". It does the opposite. Clemson advises against adding a layer of pebbles or clay pieces because it slows water movement. The [University of Missouri IPM program](https://ipm.missouri.edu/meg/2023/1/gardeningMyths-DT) explains why. Every pot holds a saturated zone at the bottom, called the perched water table. A layer of gravel shortens the column of soil above it, which moves that saturated zone up into the mix, where the roots are.

What actually helps drainage is a hole in the bottom and a free-draining mix. A single piece of broken pot over the hole is fine to keep soil from washing out, as Clemson notes. The Missouri article also points out that, with the same mix, taller containers drain better than shorter ones. The [soil guide](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) covers mixes.

## Match the pot to the plant

- **Succulents and cacti.** Clay, with a gritty mix. Not oversized.
- **Ferns, calatheas, peace lilies.** Plastic or glazed, with a mix that holds moisture but still drains.
- **Tall or top-heavy plants.** Something heavy enough not to tip, such as glazed ceramic or a plastic nursery pot inside a heavy cachepot.
- **Plants prone to root rot.** A pot only just large enough, with a good hole. Clay gives you more margin. The [root rot guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers what to do if it happens.

## Potting tips

When you repot, Clemson recommends leaving the top of the root ball about 2.5 cm (1 in) below the rim, so there is room to water without spilling. It also advises repotting in spring or summer, and not repotting plants that are ailing, dormant or in flower. For a large plant you cannot easily repot, Clemson suggests topdressing instead, replacing the top 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 in) of soil with fresh mix.

[Lily](/en) schedules watering per plant, so after a move to a bigger pot or a different material you can adjust that plant's interval without changing the rest.

## Quick answers

**Can I put a plant straight into a pot with no hole?** It is risky. Use the decorative pot as a cachepot around a pot that drains, or drill a hole.

**Should a pot be wider or deeper?** Match the root ball and go a little larger in diameter. With the same mix, taller pots drain better than shallow ones.
