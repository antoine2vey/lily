Overwatering vs underwatering is confusing because both make a houseplant wilt, yellow and drop leaves. The quickest way to tell them apart is the soil: push a finger 2 to 3 cm (1 in) down and lift the pot. A wilting plant in wet, heavy soil has been overwatered, and its roots are probably damaged; a wilting plant in dry, light soil is thirsty and usually recovers within hours of a good watering.

## Overwatering vs underwatering: why they look the same

[Clemson's watering factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) says it plainly: the symptoms of overwatering and underwatering are similar, and both lead to poor root health. The reason is that in both cases the roots stop supplying water to the leaves.

- **Underwatered:** there is no water in the soil for the roots to take up.
- **Overwatered:** roots need oxygen as well as water. In soil that stays soaked, the air spaces fill with water, the roots suffocate and start to rot, and rotted roots cannot take up water either. The plant wilts in wet soil.

Clemson calls overwatering the main cause of death of potted plants, and warns that watering a wilting plant whose roots are rotten makes things worse. So before you reach for the watering can, check the soil.

## Overwatering vs underwatering signs, side by side

| What you see | Overwatered | Underwatered |
|---|---|---|
| Soil | Wet or damp days after watering, may smell sour | Dry, light, pulling away from the pot sides |
| Pot weight | Heavy | Noticeably light |
| Leaves | Limp and soft, yellow, sometimes with brown patches | Limp then crisp, dry, curled |
| Which leaves first | Often lower and older leaves turn yellow | Edges and tips brown and dry |
| Stem base | Soft, dark or mushy in bad cases | Firm |
| Roots | Brown or black, soft, slip their outer skin | Pale and firm, but dry |
| After watering | No improvement, or worse | Perks up within hours to a day |
| Other clues | Fungus gnats, mould on the soil surface | Water runs straight through and out |

## Signs of an overwatered plant

- **Yellow leaves that feel soft**, often starting low on the plant. Clemson's [houseplant disorders factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/houseplant-diseases-disorders/) notes that yellowing of the whole plant is most often a symptom of overwatering.
- **Wilting even though the soil is wet.** This is the strongest single sign of root rot.
- **Brown, soft spots** on leaves or stems, and a mushy base on succulents and plants with fleshy stems.
- **Blisters or corky bumps** on the undersides of leaves. Clemson describes this as oedema, which wet soil, poor light and low temperatures bring on, most often on succulents in winter.
- **Fungus gnats** flying up from the pot, and green or white growth on the surface. Both need constantly moist soil.
- **Roots** that are brown or black and soft when you tip the plant out, with the outer layer sliding off like a sleeve. Healthy roots are white or tan and firm.

## Signs of an underwatered plant

- **Wilting with dry soil** that is light when you lift the pot.
- **Crisp, brown leaf edges and tips**, and leaves that curl or roll. Clemson notes that chronic underwatering leads to wilting, leaf curling and death.
- **Soil shrinking away from the pot**, so that water runs down the gap and out of the hole without wetting the root ball.
- **Lower leaves yellowing and dropping** after a long dry spell, as the plant sheds what it cannot support.
- **Flower buds dropping** before they open.

## It might not be water at all

Wilting and yellowing have other causes too, which is why the soil check matters. Clemson's disorders factsheet lists:

- **Too little light.** Pale, stretched growth and lower leaves yellowing slowly over weeks.
- **Cold or drafts.** Leaves dropping or browning after a cold night by a window or a door.
- **Salt buildup** from fertiliser or hard water. Brown tips and edges, with a white crust on the soil surface or the pot rim.
- **A pot-bound plant.** So many roots and so little soil that it wilts between waterings. The cure is a bigger pot, not more frequent water.
- **Pests.** Spider mites and mealybugs cause yellowing and leaf drop. Check the undersides of the leaves.
- **Moving the plant.** Some leaf drop after a change of spot is normal and should settle within about three weeks, according to Clemson.

## How to fix an overwatered plant

1. **Stop watering** and empty any water in the saucer or decorative pot.
2. **Move it somewhere bright** but out of hot direct sun, so the soil dries faster.
3. **If the plant is wilting, unpot it and look at the roots.** If most are white and firm, let the soil dry out more than usual before the next watering.
4. **If roots are brown and soft,** cut them off with clean scissors back to healthy tissue, remove the wet soil, and repot in fresh, free-draining mix in a pot with a drainage hole, only slightly bigger than the remaining roots. Clemson's advice is blunt: if most roots are dark, soft and dead, the plant is better discarded. Take a cutting first if any stem is still healthy.
5. **Water lightly** afterwards, and wait until the top of the soil is dry before watering again.

The full process, including when to take cuttings, is in our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery).

## How to fix an underwatered plant

1. **Water thoroughly** until water runs out of the bottom of the pot.
2. **If the water runs straight through,** the soil has shrunk and repels water. Clemson suggests double watering: water once, then again half an hour later. Or stand the pot in a sink or bucket of water until the surface of the soil is moist, then let it drain completely.
3. **Empty the saucer** once it has drained.
4. **Trim crisp leaves** if you want. They will not turn green again, but new growth will be normal.

Most underwatered plants recover the same day. If a plant stays limp a day after a thorough soak, check the roots: they may have died back in the dry spell or from salt buildup.

## How to read soil moisture

Watering by the calendar is the main reason plants end up overwatered. Clemson says there is no pat answer to how often you should water, because it depends on the species, its growth stage, its location, the pot, the soil mix and the weather. Check the soil instead.

- **Touch.** The most accurate gauge, according to Clemson: push a finger to the first joint. If it feels dry at the fingertip, most plants need water. Succulents and cacti should dry further; ferns and peace lilies less.
- **Weight.** A dry pot is clearly lighter. Lift it after watering so you know what "wet" feels like.
- **Colour.** Most potting mixes turn lighter as they dry.
- **Sound.** Tap a clay pot with a knuckle: a hollow sound means dry, a dull one means moist.
- **Meters.** Clemson notes that moisture meters vary widely in accuracy and that fertiliser and soil type affect the reading. Use one as a guide, not a verdict.

What changes how fast a pot dries: clay pots dry faster than plastic, a large plant in a small pot needs water more often, peat-heavy mixes hold more water than mixes with bark and perlite, and warm, bright rooms dry soil faster than cool, dim ones. Plants also use much less water in winter, when growth slows and days are short. ## How to avoid both

- Use pots with drainage holes, and empty the saucer after watering.
- Choose a pot only slightly bigger than the root ball; a much larger pot stays wet. Our [pot guide](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) has more.
- Match the mix to the plant: gritty for succulents, airy with bark for aroids. See [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants).
- Water thoroughly when the plant needs it, rather than little and often.
- Water less in winter.

If you keep a lot of plants, [Lily](/en) schedules watering per plant and lengthens the intervals indoors as daylight shortens, so you check the soil on the right days instead of guessing.
