Most mint problems come from the pot rather than from disease: soil that dries out between waterings, soil that stays waterlogged, too little light or roots that have filled the container. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/herbs/mint/grow-your-own) calls mint vigorous, healthy and trouble free, asks for evenly moist compost, and suggests repotting or dividing every few years. Below, each symptom has its likely causes in order of frequency and what to do.

## Mint problems start with water, light and pot size

Mint is a plant of damp ground. NC State's spearmint profile describes it growing wild in moist fields and at pond edges, and notes that it does not do well in dry soil. In a pot it has three common weak points:

- **Water.** A small pot of mint in sun can dry out within a day in summer. The RHS notes that containers dry faster than beds and should be checked often in hot weather.
- **Light.** [NC State's peppermint profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/mentha-x-piperita/) says it copes with shade surprisingly well but needs at least three hours of sun a day. A dim kitchen counter gives far less.
- **Space.** Mint spreads by underground stems called rhizomes and fills a pot fast. NC State suggests a pot 30 to 40 cm (12 to 16 in) wide.

Most of the fixes below come back to one of these three.

## Why are my mint leaves turning yellow?

**1. Waterlogged roots.** The most common cause indoors. Mint likes moist soil, not standing water. A pot without drainage holes, or one left sitting in a full saucer, starves the roots of air. Leaves yellow from the bottom up and the stems may go soft at the base. Move the plant into a pot with holes, empty the saucer after watering, and let the surface dry slightly before watering again. If the roots are brown and mushy, follow our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery), or start again from a healthy cutting, which roots easily.

**2. A pot packed with roots.** After a year or two, rhizomes fill the container, the compost is used up and the plant dries out within hours of watering. Lower leaves yellow and new growth comes in small. Tip it out: a solid mat of roots means it is time. The RHS recommends repotting every few years, either into a larger pot or by cutting the clump into sections and replanting one in fresh compost. Spring or autumn is best for dividing.

**3. Repeated drying out.** A pot that wilts hard and recovers again and again loses its lower leaves, which yellow and then brown. Water more often in summer, or move the pot out of the hottest afternoon sun; the RHS notes that a shadier spot cuts watering in hot weather.

**4. Mint rust.** If the yellow comes with orange, then yellow, then black spots, it is a disease, not a watering problem. See the next section.

**5. The end of the season.** Mint is a herbaceous perennial. The RHS explains that it dies back to the ground over winter and regrows in spring. Yellowing stems in late autumn are normal.

## What are the orange spots on my mint?

Almost always mint rust, a fungus that affects garden mints along with marjoram and savory. According to the [RHS page on mint rust](https://www.rhs.org.uk/disease/mint-rust), the signs follow a sequence:

- **Spring.** Shoots come up pale and distorted.
- **Spring to early summer.** Orange pustules appear on stems and leaves.
- **Later in the season.** Yellow and then black pustules follow, large patches of leaf can die and the plant may drop leaves.

The black spores survive the winter in the soil and, it is thought, inside the rhizomes, so rust returns to the same plant each spring. The RHS advice:

1. Remove affected plants promptly, before the black resting spores form, along with their rhizomes.
2. If only part of a bed is infected, take healthy stems from the clean part and replant them in a new spot or a fresh pot.
3. Nurseries use heat treatment: washed rhizomes go into water at 44°C (111°F) for 10 minutes, then into cold water before planting. The RHS warns that this is very close to the temperature that kills the plant, so you need an accurate thermometer.

The RHS also states that no fungicides active against mint rust are available for culinary herbs. Starting again with a clean plant is usually simpler. NC State lists rust and leaf spot as the two main diseases of spearmint. Our guide to [houseplant fungal diseases](/en/blog/houseplant-fungal-diseases) covers leaf spot.

## Why is my mint drooping?

**1. Dry soil.** By far the most common cause. Push a finger into the compost. If it is dry, water thoroughly until it drains, and the stems usually stand up again within an hour or two. A pot that droops every afternoon in summer is too small or in too much sun. Our guide on [how often to water mint](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-mint) gives starting intervals by season.

**2. Heat.** On very hot afternoons, mint can sag even in damp soil and recover in the evening. That is the plant losing water faster than its roots can draw it up. Shade from the afternoon sun helps more than extra water.

**3. Rotting roots.** If the soil is wet and the stems still droop, the roots may be damaged. Check for a sour smell and soft, dark roots, and treat as waterlogging above.

## Why is my mint leggy and sparse?

Long, thin stems with small, widely spaced leaves mean too little light, a plant that has run to flower, or both.

- **Indoors**, put it on the sunniest windowsill you have. The RHS suggests a sunny windowsill for potted mint brought in for winter.
- **Pinch the tips** of each stem regularly. NC State notes that this promotes bushier, leafier growth.
- **After flowering**, cut the whole plant down to about 5 cm (2 in) from the base. The RHS says fresh leaves follow within a few weeks.

## What pests attack mint?

- **Aphids.** Clusters of small green or black insects on soft shoot tips. The RHS notes that heavy feeding weakens the plant and distorts growth. Rinse them off with a jet of water, or pinch off the worst tips and use them.
- **Spider mites.** Fine pale speckling on the leaves and fine webbing, most often indoors in warm, dry air. Rinse the plant under the shower and repeat every few days. Our [spider mite treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) guide has the full routine.
- **Mint moth caterpillars.** Small caterpillars feeding on mint-family plants outdoors. The RHS says they can usually be tolerated.
- **Leafhoppers.** Coarse, pale mottling on the leaves outdoors in summer. Usually cosmetic.
- **Blue mint beetle** (UK). A metallic blue beetle first found breeding in Britain in 2011, now recorded in parts of England and in Edinburgh. Pick them off by hand.

Since you eat the leaves, avoid spraying anything on mint that is not labelled for edible herbs. Picking off pests and cutting back infested stems usually does enough, because mint regrows fast.

## Why is my mint taking over?

Because that is what it does. Its rhizomes spread underground, and NC State notes that stems also root wherever they touch the soil. In a border, the RHS recommends sinking a bottomless container or a pot with large drainage holes into the ground, with the rim slightly above the surface so shoots cannot root over the edge. Dig out stray shoots as soon as they appear, taking as much root as you can.

## Quick answers

**Is mint toxic to cats and dogs?** The ASPCA lists mint as toxic to dogs, cats and horses because of its essential oils, with vomiting and diarrhoea after large amounts. Keep pots out of reach of pets that chew plants.

**Is my mint dead in winter?** Probably not. Mint dies back to the ground and regrows from the rhizomes in spring. Keep the compost barely moist and cut old stems back before new shoots appear.
