Croton care comes down to three things: bright, indirect light, steady warmth that never drops below 15°C (59°F), and humid air. Water when the top 1 to 2.5 cm (½ to 1 in) of compost is dry, less in winter, and feed only once or twice in the growing season. Most crotons that struggle indoors are reacting to a change, such as a move, a cold draught or a dry spell, by dropping leaves.

## Croton care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light. No full summer sun through glass. |
| Water | When the top 1 to 2.5 cm (½ to 1 in) is dry. Less in winter. Never leave it standing in water. |
| Humidity | Moderate to high. A pebble tray or a humidifier helps in heated rooms. |
| Temperature | 18 to 29°C (65 to 85°F) is comfortable. Keep it above 15°C (59°F). |
| Soil | Light, fast-draining, slightly acidic mix, such as 3 parts peat-free ericaceous compost to 1 part grit. |
| Feeding | Once or twice during the growing season. |
| Pets and children | Poisonous if eaten, and the milky sap irritates skin and eyes. Keep it out of reach. |

The croton, *Codiaeum variegatum*, is a tropical shrub from tropical Asia and the western Pacific, grown for its thick, leathery leaves splashed with yellow, orange, red, pink, purple or white. Indoors it usually stays between 30 and 90 cm (1 and 3 ft) tall, depending on the cultivar and its age.

## Light: bright but not scorching

Light controls the leaf colour. [Wisconsin Horticulture](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/croton-codiaeum-variegatum/) explains that higher light gives brighter colours and a more compact plant, while leaves can revert to shades of green in too little light. Too much direct sun makes the leaves look grey and dull.

The [RHS croton growing guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/codiaeum/growing-guide) gives the same balance from the other side. In poor light the leaves may lose their colour or drop, and in full summer sun through glass they can overheat and scorch.

In practice, place it close to a bright window but out of the hottest midday sun. An east-facing window, or a spot set back from a south- or west-facing one, suits most homes. Growth slows in winter as the light fades, which is normal. If new leaves come out mostly green, move the plant somewhere brighter.

## How to water a croton

Wisconsin recommends watering only when the top 1.3 to 2.5 cm (½ to 1 in) of soil has dried, and reducing watering in winter. The RHS says the same in different words: water once the top layer of compost has dried, hold off if it still feels damp, and tip away any water left in the saucer.

Both sources warn about the two extremes. Wisconsin notes that crotons drop leaves if they stay too wet or too dry for long periods. The RHS lists dry compost as a cause of leaf drop and waterlogged compost as the cause of root rot.

Water type matters more than for most houseplants. Crotons like slightly acidic conditions, and the RHS advises against using hard tap water regularly because it makes the compost more alkaline over time. Use collected rainwater or softened water when you can, at room temperature.

Check with a finger rather than watering on a fixed day. A croton in a warm, bright room in summer dries out much faster than the same plant in a cooler room in January.

## Humidity

The RHS calls warmth and high humidity essential for crotons, and Wisconsin recommends moderate to high humidity. Central heating dries the air a lot in winter, which is when crotons tend to lose lower leaves.

To raise the humidity, stand the pot on a tray of damp pebbles or clay granules, as the RHS suggests, so the evaporating water moistens the air around the leaves. Keep the bottom of the pot above the waterline. Grouping plants together or running a humidifier nearby also helps. Wiping the leaves with a damp cloth now and then removes dust, which the RHS says helps the leaves use the light. ## Temperature and draughts

The RHS gives a comfortable range of 18 to 35°C (65 to 95°F) and a firm minimum of 15°C (59°F). Wisconsin puts the ideal at 16 to 29°C (60 to 85°F) and warns that leaves often drop if the temperature stays below 10°C (50°F). The safest target for a houseplant is the RHS one: keep it above 15°C all year.

Steady matters as much as warm. Both sources name cold draughts and sudden temperature swings as causes of leaf drop. Keep crotons away from doors that open onto cold air, single-glazed windows on winter nights and the direct blast of a radiator or air conditioner.

You can move a croton outside for summer. Wisconsin says to wait until temperatures stay above 10°C (50°F), get it used to the brighter light gradually, and bring it back in autumn before nights fall below that. Illinois Extension also notes that crotons resent root disturbance and may drop leaves when transplanted. Our guide on [how to acclimate new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) covers the gradual move.

## Soil and repotting

Crotons need a light, fast-draining mix that is slightly acidic. The RHS suggests 3 parts peat-free ericaceous compost to 1 part grit.

Repot only when the roots fill the pot, usually every year or two, and do it in spring. Wisconsin recommends moving up to a pot only 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) larger. The RHS explains why: a pot much bigger than the root ball holds compost that stays wet for too long, which leads to root rot. Our [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) walks through the steps.

## Feeding a croton

The University of Wisconsin advises fertilising once or twice during the growing season, or more often if you want faster growth. Use a general houseplant fertiliser at the strength on the label, in spring and summer only. Do not feed in winter, when growth slows. Pale new growth is more often a sign of low light than of hunger, so check the light before adding fertiliser. Our guide on [how to fertilize houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) explains how to read a label and dilute a feed.

## Pruning a croton

A croton that has grown leggy can be cut back. Wisconsin says plants can be pruned hard in early spring, before new growth starts, to encourage branching. The RHS advises pruning in spring, cutting just above a leaf joint with sharp secateurs.

Wear gloves. The milky sap that bleeds from cuts irritates skin and eyes, and Wisconsin notes it can cause contact dermatitis in sensitive people. Keep it away from your face while you work.

## Propagating a croton

The RHS recommends softwood cuttings in early summer. Wisconsin adds air layering in spring as a second method. Crotons can be grown from seed, but Wisconsin points out the seedlings will not look like the parent, so cuttings or air layering are the only ways to keep a particular cultivar.

## Common croton problems

**Leaf drop.** This is the classic croton complaint. The RHS lists compost that is too dry, air that is too dry, cold draughts and temperatures below 15°C (59°F) for a long time. Wisconsin adds compost kept too wet and sudden changes of environment, which is why a new croton often drops leaves in its first weeks at home. Fix the conditions and keep them steady. New leaves follow once the plant settles.

**Green, faded leaves.** Too little light. Move it closer to a window.

**Grey, dull or scorched patches.** Too much direct sun. Move it out of the midday sun.

**Root rot.** Soft stems, wilting with wet compost and a sour smell from the pot. The RHS links it to overwatering, a pot left standing in water or a poorly draining mix.

**Pests.** Wisconsin names mealybugs, spider mites and scale insects. Spider mites like warm, dry air, so a croton in a heated room in winter is a common target. Look for fine webbing and pale speckling under the leaves, and see our [spider mites treatment guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment).

## Is a croton toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes, treat it as poisonous. The RHS says all parts of the plant are poisonous and advises keeping crotons away from children and pets. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/codiaeum-variegatum/) rates the poison severity as low, with nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea if eaten and skin irritation from the sap. The ASPCA plant list has no entry for the croton, so it gives no pet-specific rating.

## Quick answers

**Why are my croton's new leaves all green?** Not enough light. Leaf colour comes from bright, indirect light.

**How often should I repot a croton?** Every year or two, in spring, once the roots fill the pot.

**Can a croton live in a dim room?** It will survive for a while but lose colour and drop leaves. It needs a bright spot.
