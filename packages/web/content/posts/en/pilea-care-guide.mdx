Pilea care is simple once the plant has the right spot: bright, indirect light, a well-drained mix watered when the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) is dry, and a quarter turn every week so it grows straight. The Chinese money plant (*Pilea peperomioides*) stays about 20 to 30 cm (8 to 12 in) tall and wide, makes new plants from its roots every year, and is safe for cats, dogs and children.

## Pilea care at a glance

| Need | What it wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light. Some gentle morning sun is fine; no harsh midday sun |
| Water | When the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of mix is dry. Less in winter |
| Humidity | Average room humidity is fine; it likes moderate to high |
| Temperature | Cool to normal room temperature, away from cold drafts and heaters |
| Soil | Moist, well-drained potting mix, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Toxicity to pets | Safe for pets and children |

These points follow the [NC State Extension page for *Pilea peperomioides*](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/pilea-peperomioides/). It describes the plant as native to Yunnan province in southern China, where it grows in shady, cool mountain areas, and lists its round leaves at about 10 cm (4 in) across, held at the centre by long stalks like lily pads. Houseplants rarely flower.

## How much light does a pilea need?

NC State recommends bright, indirect sunlight. In practice:

- An east window, or a spot 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft) from a south or west window, gives good light.
- A pilea in strong, direct afternoon sun can get pale or scorched patches on the leaves facing the glass.
- In low light, the stems stretch and the plant turns leggy, with long gaps between smaller leaves. NC State names inadequate light as the cause of leggy growth.

A pilea grows toward the light, so its stems lean and the plant turns lopsided within weeks. Give the pot a quarter turn each time you water to keep the crown even. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a window.

## How often should you water a pilea?

NC State recommends a moist, well-drained mix and warns that pilea is susceptible to root rot if overwatered. The practical test: push a finger into the mix and water when the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) is dry. In bright light in summer that is often once a week; in winter, every two to three weeks.

Water until it runs from the drainage hole, then empty the saucer. Pour onto the mix rather than over the leaves: NC State notes that wetting the foliage can lead to leaf spots.

Signs to read:

- leaves that droop and feel soft while the mix is dry mean the plant is thirsty. They perk up within hours of watering;
- yellowing lower leaves, drooping stems and mix that stays wet for a week point to overwatering;
- leaves that droop while the mix is wet are the warning sign of root rot. Check the roots.

Our guide on [how often to water a pilea](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-pilea) covers the seasonal schedule in more detail.

## What humidity and temperature does a pilea like?

NC State says pilea does best in moderate to high humidity but tolerates the humidity of an average home. You do not need a humidifier for it, though a bathroom or kitchen with a bright window suits it well.

It also warns against cold drafts and sources of heat. Keep it away from doors that open onto winter air, single-glazed windows on frosty nights, radiators and heat vents. The University of Arkansas Extension [table of houseplant temperatures](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/yard-garden/home-landscape/docs/Temperature%20Requirements%20of%20Selected%20House%20Plants.pdf) lists pilea among medium-temperature plants, which grow best at about 16 to 18°C (60 to 65°F) by day and 13 to 16°C (55 to 60°F) at night. A normal heated living room is warmer than that and still fine; what pilea dislikes is sudden swings.

## What soil and pot does a pilea need?

Use a moist but well-drained potting mix, as NC State recommends. A houseplant compost with about a quarter of perlite drains well and still holds enough water. Always use a pot with a drainage hole. Terracotta dries faster than plastic, which suits a plant prone to root rot.

Repot in spring when roots fill the pot or come out of the drainage hole, moving up only one pot size, about 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider. A large pot of wet mix around a small root system is the usual start of rot. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains the mixes.

## Should you fertilise a pilea?

Lightly. From spring to late summer, a balanced liquid houseplant fertiliser at half the label strength once a month is enough. Stop in autumn and winter, when growth slows. Pale, small new leaves in summer can be a sign the plant needs feeding, but check light first, since low light causes the same.

## How do you propagate a pilea?

NC State lists offsets, or pups, and stem cuttings, and says spring or early summer, when the plant is growing, is the best time.

**Pups from the roots.** A healthy pilea sends up small plants from its roots around the base of the stem.

1. Wait until a pup has four or five leaves and is about 5 cm (2 in) tall.
2. Scrape away the top of the mix to find where the pup joins the root.
3. Cut it free with a clean knife, keeping some root attached if you can.
4. Pot it in a small pot of moist mix, or root it in a glass of water and pot it once the roots are 2 to 3 cm (1 in) long.

**Pups on the stem.** Small plantlets also appear on the main stem. Cut them off with a short piece of stem and root them in water.

**Stem cuttings.** An old, leggy pilea can be cut back. Root the top in water and the old stump often sends out new shoots.

Our guide to [propagating houseplants from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers rooting in water and in mix.

## Why are my pilea's leaves curling or dropping?

A few lower leaves yellowing and dropping as the stem grows taller is normal. More than that usually points to watering or light:

- **Yellow, falling lower leaves with wet mix.** Overwatering. Let the mix dry more between waterings and check the roots for rot.
- **Drooping, soft leaves with dry mix.** Underwatering. Water thoroughly.
- **Leggy stems, small leaves, strong lean.** Too little light. Move it closer to a window and turn it weekly.
- **Brown spots on the leaves.** NC State links leaf spots to wet foliage. Water the mix, not the leaves.

## Pests and pets

NC State lists spider mites, mealybugs, fungus gnats and scale insects. Fungus gnats, small dark flies around the pot, are a sign that the mix stays wet too long. Check the undersides of leaves and the leaf stalks when you water. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers how to identify and treat each one.

NC State describes the Chinese money plant as safe for pets and children, which makes it an easy choice for homes with cats. Our list of [plants safe for cats](/en/blog/plants-safe-for-cats) has more.
