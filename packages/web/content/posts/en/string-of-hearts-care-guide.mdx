String of hearts care comes down to strong light and very little water. Give the plant a bright window, ideally one that gets some direct sun, let the potting mix dry out completely before you water, and cut back further in winter. *Ceropegia woodii* is a trailing succulent from southern Africa. It stores water in its thick, heart-shaped leaves and in a tuber under the soil, so it copes with dry spells far better than with wet soil. Overwatering kills more string of hearts than anything else.

## String of hearts care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright light; a west or south window with some direct sun |
| Water | Only when the mix is completely dry; less in winter |
| Humidity | Normal indoor air; no misting |
| Temperature | Normal room temperature; above 16°C (60°F) in winter |
| Soil | Cactus mix, or potting mix with added perlite |
| Feeding | At most monthly at half strength, spring to late summer; none in winter |
| Repotting | Only when needed, in spring; it likes a snug pot |
| Toxicity | Not on the ASPCA list; see below |

## How much light does string of hearts need?

More than most trailing houseplants. The [University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/string-of-hearts-ceropegia-woodii/) recommends a west- or south-facing window. Light also changes how the plant looks. In strong light the leaves are darker, with clear silver marbling and purple undersides. In weak light they turn pale and the gaps between leaves grow longer.

Watch for these signs of too little light:

- Long, bare stretches of stem between pairs of leaves.
- Small, pale leaves that lose their marbling.
- Slow growth and no flowers.

Move the plant closer to the glass, or to a brighter window. Hanging it high in a dim corner is a common mistake. It looks good there, but the light is a fraction of what it gets on a sill. If no window is bright enough, a grow light a short distance above the pot works well. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a window.

## How often should you water string of hearts?

Only when the potting mix is completely dry. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ceropegia-woodii/) advises letting the soil dry out completely between waterings, and Wisconsin notes that the plant is easily killed by overwatering.

How to check:

1. Push a finger or a wooden skewer to the bottom of the pot. If you feel any moisture, wait.
2. Lift the pot. A dry pot of cactus mix is noticeably light.
3. Squeeze a leaf gently. Firm leaves mean the plant still has water stored. Thin, soft or slightly wrinkled leaves mean it is ready for a drink.

When you water, water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. In a warm, bright spell this may be every week or two. In a cool room in winter it may be every three to four weeks or less. These are starting points, not a schedule. Our guide on [how often to water string of hearts](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-string-of-hearts) goes through each season.

## What soil and pot does string of hearts need?

Fast-draining soil. Wisconsin suggests a cactus mix, or a regular potting mix with extra perlite. Water should run straight through the pot, and the mix should dry within days, not weeks. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) gives a gritty succulent recipe.

Choose a pot with a drainage hole. Terracotta suits string of hearts because it dries faster than plastic. Keep the pot small. Wisconsin notes that the plant likes being crowded, so repot only when roots fill the pot, and do it in spring. Move up just one size, a pot a few centimetres wider than the old one. A big pot holds water the roots cannot use, and the tuber rots.

## Humidity and temperature

Normal indoor air is fine. String of hearts is a succulent and does not need high humidity, so skip the misting. Wet leaves in cool, still air do it more harm than good.

Normal room temperature suits it for most of the year. Keep it away from cold draughts and from the hot, dry air directly above a radiator.

## How to care for string of hearts in winter

String of hearts takes a rest in winter. Growth slows or stops, and the plant uses far less water. Wisconsin's advice for this season:

- **Keep it above 16°C (60°F).** A slightly cooler room is fine, as long as it stays above that.
- **Water less.** The mix dries much more slowly in short, cool days. Wait until it is completely dry, then a little longer.
- **Stop feeding** until spring.
- **Keep the light strong.** Winter light is weak, so give the plant your brightest window, close to the glass.

Some leaf loss in winter is normal, especially at the top of the pot. Yellow leaves that fall while the mix is still damp are not. They point to too much water for the season.

## Should you fertilise string of hearts?

Lightly. Wisconsin recommends feeding no more than once a month during the growing season, at half strength. Do not feed in winter, or a plant that has just been repotted. Too much fertiliser gives weak, soft growth and salt build-up in the pot.

## Flowers and aerial tubers

A string of hearts in good light flowers in summer and autumn. The flowers are small and tubular, and easy to miss among the leaves.

Older stems also form small round tubers along the vines, at the leaf joints. Wisconsin calls these aerial tubers. They are a normal part of the plant, not a disease, and they are the easiest way to grow a new plant.

## How to propagate string of hearts

Wisconsin lists three methods. Tubers and cuttings are the easiest.

1. **Aerial tubers.** Press a tuber, still attached to its vine, onto the surface of a pot of moist cactus mix. Pin it down with a bent paper clip if needed. Once it has rooted and starts to grow, cut the vine from the parent plant.
2. **Stem cuttings.** Cut a length of vine with several leaf joints. Lay it on moist cactus mix, or coil it on the surface, with the joints touching the mix. Wisconsin notes that bottom heat helps them root. Keep the mix barely moist until new growth appears.
3. **Seed.** Possible, but rarely used at home.

Our [plant propagation guide](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers cuttings in more detail.

## How to keep string of hearts full

A string of hearts often grows long, thin vines and a sparse top. Three things help:

- **More light**, so the leaves grow closer together.
- **Trimming** long vines in spring or summer. Cut just above a leaf joint and root the cuttings back in the same pot.
- **Coiling** vines onto the soil surface, so they root and fill the top of the pot.

## Common string of hearts problems

Wisconsin names overwatering and too little light as the main problems, and mealybugs as the pest to watch.

- **Yellow leaves that fall, soft stems near the soil:** too much water. Let the mix dry completely and check the tuber for rot.
- **Thin, wrinkled, deflated leaves:** too little water, as long as the stems and tuber are firm.
- **Long bare stems, pale leaves:** too little light.
- **White, cottony tufts in the leaf joints:** mealybugs. Wipe them off with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol and isolate the plant.

## Is string of hearts toxic to cats?

String of hearts is not on the [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/cats-plant-list) list of toxic or non-toxic plants. It is widely described as non-toxic to pets, but no authority we checked confirms this. To be safe, hang it out of reach of cats that chew trailing stems.
