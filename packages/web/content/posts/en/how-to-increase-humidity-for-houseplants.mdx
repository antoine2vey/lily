To increase humidity for houseplants, run a humidifier in the room. It is the only method that reliably raises the humidity of the air. Grouping plants and standing them on trays of wet pebbles add a little moisture right around the leaves, while misting does almost nothing. Most houseplants are comfortable at 40 to 50% relative humidity, and a heated home in winter can drop to 10 to 20%, so winter is when this matters.

## How humid should it be for houseplants?

[Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/air-our-home-extremely-dry-winter-should-i-mist-houseplants) puts the comfortable range for many houseplants at 40 to 50% relative humidity, and notes that the air in a heated home in winter can fall to 10 to 20%. Summer air indoors is usually much closer to the target, unless air conditioning runs all day.

Not every plant needs help. Most common houseplants, such as pothos, snake plants, ZZ plants, spider plants and most philodendrons, tolerate ordinary room air. Cacti and succulents prefer it dry. The plants that really suffer in dry air are the thin-leaved ones from humid forest floors: ferns like the Boston fern and maidenhair fern, calatheas, and some orchids.

Before you buy anything, measure. A digital hygrometer costs little and tells you whether your room sits at 25% or 45%. Put it near the plants, away from the humidifier if you have one.

## How to tell your plants need more humidity

- Leaf tips and edges turn brown and crisp, starting on the newest leaves.
- Leaves of calatheas and ferns curl inward.
- Flower buds dry up and drop before opening.
- Spider mites appear. They do best in warm, dry air. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/nephrolepis-exaltata/) notes that Boston ferns get spider mites and scale when the air is too dry.

Brown tips can also come from letting the compost dry out, from too much fertiliser or from cold. If the compost is fine and the tips brown mostly in winter, dry air is the likely cause.

## Increase humidity for houseplants with a humidifier

A room humidifier is the one method that changes the humidity of the whole room by a useful amount. Iowa State describes humidifiers as excellent for this. For a few plants, a small unit placed about a metre from the plants and set to hold 45 to 50% is enough.

Practical points:

- Point the mist into the room, not straight at the leaves, so they do not stay wet.
- Clean and refill it as the manufacturer says. Standing water grows mould and bacteria.
- Ultrasonic models fed with hard tap water can leave a fine white dust on furniture. Filtered or distilled water probably avoids it.
- Close the door if you can. A humidifier fights a losing battle in a large open-plan room with the heating on.

## Group your plants together

Plants lose water vapour through their leaves. Iowa State notes that grouping plants raises the humidity around them through transpiration and evaporation from the compost. A tight cluster of pots creates a slightly more humid pocket than the same plants spread around the room. Put the most demanding plants in the middle.

The effect is small, but it costs nothing and adds to whatever else you do. Leave enough space for air to move between leaves, because crowded, damp foliage invites fungal spots. Our guide to [houseplant fungal diseases](/en/blog/houseplant-fungal-diseases) explains why.

## Use a pebble tray

A pebble tray is a shallow tray filled with gravel or clay pebbles, with water poured in to just below the top of the stones. The pot sits on the pebbles. As the water evaporates, it slightly raises the humidity around the plant.

Iowa State's condition is the one most people miss: the water must not reach the bottom of the pot. A pot standing in water soaks it up through the drainage hole, and the compost stays waterlogged, which leads to root rot. Top the tray up as it dries.

Like grouping, a pebble tray helps a little, right around the plant. It will not change the room.

## Move plants to more humid rooms

Bathrooms and kitchens are usually the most humid rooms in a home. NC State suggests both for Boston ferns. A bathroom works only if it has a window with real daylight; a dark bathroom trades a humidity problem for a light problem.

Also move plants away from what dries the air near them: radiators, heating vents, wood stoves, and the airflow from air conditioners. A plant a metre from a radiator lives in far drier air than the hygrometer on the other side of the room shows.

## Use a terrarium or plant cabinet

For the fussiest plants, such as maidenhair ferns, some calatheas and small jewel orchids, a closed or partly closed glass container keeps humidity high without humidifying the whole room. A glass cabinet with a small fan and a grow light is the larger version. Watch for condensation streaming down the glass and open it for a while each day if it does.

## Why misting doesn't work

Misting is the most common humidity advice and the least effective. Iowa State explains that the water on the leaves evaporates within minutes, so the humidity returns to where it was almost at once. You would need to mist several times a day to make any difference. On plants with hairy leaves, such as African violets, the water can leave marks and encourage rot, and wet leaves at night help fungal spots spread.

If you enjoy misting, it does no harm on most plants in the morning. Just do not count on it to fix brown tips.

## Can there be too much humidity?

Yes, in a closed room. Signs include condensation running down the windows, damp patches or mould on walls and window frames, and grey mould on plants. If you see these, turn the humidifier down, open a window for a few minutes a day, and give plants more space for air to move.
