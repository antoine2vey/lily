The short version of this spring repotting guide: repot from March to May in the northern hemisphere (September to November in the southern), once the plant starts making new leaves. Move up to a pot only 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider than the old one, use fresh potting mix, and leave out the gravel layer at the bottom. Below you will find how to tell which plants need it, the ten steps, and how to care for them after.

## Why repot in spring?

A plant that is actively growing replaces lost root tips within days and fills new soil quickly. In autumn and winter, the same plant sits in a larger volume of wet mix it cannot use, which is a common route to root rot. [NC State Extension](https://wayne.ces.ncsu.edu/news/repotting-houseplants) recommends spring for this reason, so the plant can establish during the growing season.

The best signal is the plant itself. Wait until you see new leaves or shoots, which may be early March in a bright room or late April in a dim one.

## Does your plant need repotting?

Signs a plant has outgrown its pot:

- Roots grow out of the drainage holes or circle on the soil surface.
- Water runs straight through, or the soil dries out within a day or two.
- Growth has slowed even with fertiliser in spring and summer, and the lower leaves yellow.
- The plant is top-heavy and tips over.
- There is a white crust of salts on the soil, or the mix has broken down into a dense, sour-smelling mud.

If none of these apply, leave the plant alone. A spring top-up is enough: scrape off the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of old mix and replace it with fresh.

### How often to repot

- **Fast growers** (pothos, monstera, spider plant, most herbs): every year.
- **Most foliage plants:** every 2 years.
- **Slow growers** (snake plant, ZZ plant, aglaonema): every 2 to 3 years.
- **Cacti and succulents:** every 3 to 5 years.
- **Plants that like a snug pot** (orchids, hoya, peace lily) often flower better without a big jump in size. Repot them when the mix breaks down, not just because roots show.

## Choosing the right pot

Choose a pot no more than 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider than the current one. NC State Extension puts the limit at 2 inches larger in diameter. University of Illinois Extension warns that an oversized pot holds more soil than the plant can use, which can lead to root rot.

The pot must have a drainage hole. Material changes how fast the mix dries:

- **Terracotta** is porous and dries quickly. It suits cacti, succulents and anyone who tends to overwater.
- **Plastic and glazed ceramic** hold moisture longer. They suit ferns, calatheas and plants that like even moisture.

Our guide on [how to choose a pot for your plant](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) goes deeper on sizes and materials.

## Skip the gravel layer

Putting gravel or pot shards in the bottom of the pot for drainage is a myth. [University of Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2018-03-19-tips-repotting-houseplants) says it does not improve drainage and can make it worse. NC State explains why: water does not move from the fine mix into the coarse gravel until the soil above is saturated, so the wet zone simply sits higher in the pot, closer to the roots. To keep the mix from falling out of the drainage hole, cover it with a piece of mesh, a coffee filter or a paper towel instead.

## Choosing the potting mix

Use fresh, bagged indoor potting mix, never garden soil, which drains poorly and may carry pests. Then adjust it to the plant:

- **Aroids** (monstera, philodendron, alocasia, anthurium): add orchid bark and perlite for a chunkier, airier mix.
- **Cacti and succulents:** a cactus mix, or about half potting mix and half grit or pumice.
- **Orchids:** bark-based orchid mix only. Regular soil suffocates their roots.
- **Ferns and calatheas:** standard mix with a little perlite, which holds moisture but drains.

## Spring repotting guide, step by step

1. **Water the day before** so the root ball holds together and the plant is hydrated.
2. **Prepare the new pot.** If you reuse a pot, scrub it and soak it in a solution of one part household bleach to nine parts water, as NC State recommends, then rinse well.
3. **Remove the plant.** Turn the pot on its side or upside down with your hand over the soil, and tap the rim on a table edge. Don't pull it out by the stem. For a stuck plastic pot, squeeze the sides.
4. **Inspect the roots.** Healthy roots are firm and pale. Trim any that are dark and mushy with clean scissors. See [root rot recovery](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) if many are affected.
5. **Loosen circling roots.** Gently tease them apart, or make three or four shallow vertical cuts on a tightly bound root ball.
6. **Add mix to the bottom of the new pot,** enough that the top of the root ball sits about 2.5 cm (1 in) below the rim.
7. **Set the plant at the same depth** it grew before. Burying the stem deeper can lead to rot.
8. **Fill around the sides** with mix and firm it gently with your fingers. Don't pack it down hard, which removes air.
9. **Water thoroughly** until it runs out of the bottom, then empty the saucer after 20 minutes.
10. **Top up** if the mix settles after watering.

Spring repotting is also the right time to divide plants that form clumps, such as peace lily, snake plant, calathea and aglaonema, and to trim leggy growth. ## Aftercare: the first four weeks

- **Light:** bright, indirect light, no direct sun for about a week.
- **Water:** check before watering again. Fresh mix in a larger pot stays wet longer than the old one did.
- **Fertiliser:** wait 4 to 6 weeks. Most fresh potting mixes contain some fertiliser, and new roots burn easily.
- **Drooping:** a little wilting in the first few days is normal transplant shock. Illinois Extension notes that it usually resolves with time, water and proper light.

## Other spring jobs for indoor plants

While you are repotting, give your plants a spring reset:

- Move them back from windows that will get strong summer sun.
- Wipe dust off leaves so they use the longer days.
- Start feeding again once new growth appears.
- Check undersides of leaves for pests woken by warmer rooms.
