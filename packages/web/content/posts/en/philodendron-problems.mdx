Most philodendron problems come from water and light. Yellow lower leaves usually mean the soil stays wet too long or the plant sits too far from a window, a philodendron that droops on dry soil is thirsty, and one that droops on wet soil may have root rot. Pests are less common, but mealybugs, spider mites, aphids and scale do show up.

## Philodendron problems: check soil, light and temperature first

Three checks narrow down almost every case. Push a finger 5 cm (2 in) into the soil and note whether it is wet, damp or dry. Look at how far the plant is from the nearest window. And feel for cold: [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/philodendron/) gives an ideal range of 18 to 29°C (65 to 85°F) and says philodendrons are very sensitive to cold and draughts.

Most of what follows applies to both kinds sold as houseplants: vining ones like the heartleaf philodendron (Philodendron hederaceum) and upright, self-heading ones.

## Why are my philodendron leaves turning yellow?

**1. The soil stays too wet.** This is the most common cause. Several lower leaves turn yellow at once, the soil is still damp a week after watering, and the pot may feel heavy. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/philodendron/) names overwatering as one of the two usual causes of yellow lower leaves and dying growing tips, and says to keep the soil evenly moist but never soggy, and never to let the plant stand in water. Empty the saucer, check that the pot has a drainage hole, and wait until the top 2 to 3 cm (about 1 in) is dry before watering again. Our guide on [how often to water a philodendron](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-philodendron) gives starting intervals by season.

**2. Too little light.** Clemson's other cause of yellow lower leaves is a spot that is too dark. Philodendrons tolerate low light, which is why they end up in corners, but in a dim spot they shed old leaves faster than they make new ones. Other signs point the same way: long bare stretches of stem between leaves, and new leaves smaller than the old ones. Move the plant to bright, indirect light, close to a window but out of direct midday sun.

**3. One old leaf at a time.** A single leaf at the base of a vine turning yellow every few weeks, while new leaves keep coming, is normal ageing. Cut it off.

**4. The soil dried out completely.** If the plant wilted before the yellowing started, and the soil pulls away from the sides of the pot, the leaves yellowed from drought. Water thoroughly, then water a little sooner next time.

**5. Cold.** Leaves that yellow after a cold night, or on a plant near a door or a window in winter, point to cold damage. Keep it above 18°C (65°F) and away from draughts.

**6. Hunger.** Pale new leaves on a plant that has not been fed or repotted for a year or more suggest the soil has run out of nutrients. Clemson recommends feeding regularly with a dilute water-soluble houseplant fertiliser. Do that in spring and summer, at half the label strength.

**7. Pests.** Fine yellow or pale speckling on the upper side of the leaves, rather than whole yellow leaves, comes from spider mites or thrips feeding. See the pest section below.

## Why is my philodendron drooping?

**1. The soil is dry.** Leaves hang limp, the pot feels light, and the soil is dry at 5 cm (2 in). Water slowly until it runs out of the drainage hole. The leaves should firm up within a day.

**2. The roots are rotting.** If the plant droops while the soil is wet, do not water it. Rotten roots cannot take up water, so a plant in soggy soil wilts as if it were dry. Check the roots as described below.

**3. Cold or a sudden move.** A plant carried home in winter, set next to a cold window or under an air-conditioning vent can droop with perfectly good soil. Move it somewhere warm and stable and give it a week.

**4. It has outgrown its pot.** A philodendron that droops every three or four days, with roots coming out of the drainage hole, has a root ball too packed to hold water for long. Move it up one pot size in spring.

## Why is my philodendron dropping leaves?

Leaf drop is usually a reaction to change. The common triggers, from most to least frequent:

- **A watering problem that has gone on for weeks.** Either extreme ends with the plant shedding leaves, usually yellow ones first. Read the soil and apply the fixes above.
- **A sudden change of place.** Moving from a bright shop to a dim room, or from a warm spot to a cold one, makes a philodendron drop a few older leaves while it adjusts. If new growth appears, it has settled.
- **Cold and draughts.** NC State's warning about cold sensitivity applies here. Leaves that fall in winter from a plant near a door or an old window usually fell from cold.
- **Pests.** A heavy infestation of mealybugs, scale or spider mites can make leaves yellow and fall. Look at the undersides and the leaf joints.

A vining philodendron also loses its oldest leaves along the stem as it grows. If those are the only ones falling and the tips keep growing, nothing is wrong.

## Why are my philodendron leaf tips brown or curling?

**1. Too much fertiliser.** Clemson notes that over-fertilising makes leaf tips curl and turn brown. A white crust on the soil or the pot rim confirms salt build-up. Take the plant to a sink and run plain water through the pot for a minute or two, let it drain, and skip feeding for a couple of months.

**2. The soil dried out too far.** Crisp brown edges often appear a week or two after a dry spell.

**3. Very dry air.** NC State says philodendrons prefer high humidity but usually adapt to an average home. Brown tips in winter next to a radiator are the exception. A humidifier helps there; misting does not raise humidity for more than a few minutes. Our guide on [raising humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

Brown tissue will not turn green again. Trim the brown part with clean scissors, following the leaf's shape.

## What causes brown or black spots on philodendron leaves?

**1. Leaf spot.** Brown spots, sometimes with a yellow halo, can come from a leaf spot disease. NC State says it can be prevented with better air circulation and by keeping the leaves dry. Remove spotted leaves, water the soil rather than the foliage, and give the plant a little space from its neighbours.

**2. Sunburn.** NC State warns that direct sun scorches the leaves. Scorch shows as dry, pale or brown patches on the leaves facing the window. Move the plant back or filter the light with a sheer curtain.

**3. Cold damage.** Dark, water-soaked patches that appear a few days after a cold night or contact with a cold window pane are cold damage. Cut the damaged leaves off once they dry.

## How do I know if my philodendron has root rot?

Above the soil, root rot looks like drooping on wet soil, several yellow leaves at once and soft, dark stems at the base. Clemson says it usually results from a mix that does not drain quickly or from watering too often. To check, slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and white to light tan. Rotten roots are brown or black, mushy, and smell sour.

To treat it:

1. Remove the plant from its pot and shake or rinse off the old soil.
2. Cut every soft, dark root back to firm tissue with clean scissors.
3. Repot in fresh, fast-draining mix, for example houseplant compost with a third of perlite or orchid bark, in a clean pot with a drainage hole and only slightly larger than the remaining roots.
4. Water once, let it drain, and wait until the top of the soil is dry before watering again.

If almost no healthy roots are left, take stem cuttings with at least one node each and root them in water. Clemson notes that philodendrons propagate from stem cuttings at any time of year. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers severe cases.

## What pests attack philodendrons?

NC State lists mealybugs, aphids, scale, banded greenhouse thrips and spider mites. Clemson calls philodendrons generally pest-free, so when one appears it has probably come in on another plant.

- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony clumps in the leaf joints and along the undersides. Dab each one with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then spray the whole plant with insecticidal soap. Repeat every 7 to 10 days until none come back.
- **Spider mites.** Fine pale speckling on the leaves and thin webbing underneath, mostly in warm, dry rooms. Rinse the plant in the shower, then treat with insecticidal soap, covering the undersides. Our guide to [spider mite treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) has the full routine.
- **Aphids.** Small green or black insects clustered on new growth, with sticky honeydew below them. A strong spray of water knocks most off; insecticidal soap deals with the rest.
- **Scale.** Small brown bumps stuck to stems and leaf undersides that do not move. Scrape them off with a fingernail or a soft toothbrush, then treat as for mealybugs.
- **Thrips.** Silvery streaks on the leaves and tiny black dots of droppings. Rinse the leaves and treat with insecticidal soap on a weekly cycle, since eggs inside the leaf survive a single spray.

Keep an infested plant away from your others until it has been clean for a few weeks. Wiping the leaves with a damp cloth every month removes dust and lets you spot pests early. Our article on [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers each one in more depth.

## Quick answers

**Is a philodendron toxic to cats and dogs?** Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/heartleaf-philodendron) lists heartleaf philodendron as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. It contains insoluble calcium oxalates, which cause mouth pain and swelling, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. The sap can also irritate skin.

**Should I cut off yellow leaves?** Yes. A fully yellow leaf will not turn green again. Cut it at the base of its stalk.

**How do I keep these problems from coming back?** Water by the soil rather than the calendar, give it bright indirect light, keep it above 18°C (65°F), and feed lightly in spring and summer. The full routine is in our [philodendron care guide](/en/blog/philodendron-care-guide).
