Most Chinese evergreen problems come from soil that stays wet and from cold. Water only when the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of soil is dry, and keep the plant above 13 °C (55 °F), away from cold glass and draughts. Yellow leaves, drooping with wet soil and dark, soft stems usually mean overwatering. Brown tips and edges usually mean dry soil or fertiliser salts. Pests are possible but less common.

## Chinese evergreen problems: start with the soil and the room

*Aglaonema*, the Chinese evergreen, is one of the more forgiving houseplants. It handles low light and dry indoor air. That is why, when it struggles, the cause is usually something it cannot tolerate: soggy soil, cold, or direct sun.

So check three things before anything else. Push a finger 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) into the soil and note whether it is damp. Check the pot has a drainage hole and the saucer is empty. Then check the room temperature near the plant, at night as well as by day. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/chinese-evergreen/) gives a best range of 20 to 27 °C (68 to 80 °F), says to avoid temperatures below 13 °C (55 °F), and notes chilling injury below 10 °C (50 °F).

## Why are my Chinese evergreen leaves turning yellow?

**1. Overwatering.** This is the most frequent cause. Clemson's advice is to water when the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) is dry, keeping the soil moist but not too wet. When the soil stays wet for a week or more, roots run short of air, and the plant yellows several leaves at once, often starting low. The soil is damp and may smell sour. Let it dry to that depth before watering again, and water less in winter. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/aglaonema/) also advises reducing water in winter.

**2. Old leaves dying off.** One lower leaf yellowing at a time, while new leaves keep unfurling from the centre, is normal ageing. Cut it at the base.

**3. Cold.** Leaves that yellow after a cold night, or on a plant beside a draughty window or door, point to cold damage. Move it to a stable, warm spot.

**4. Too little light for its type.** Clemson notes that solid green types tolerate low light, while variegated types need low to moderate light. A pink, red or heavily silver variety in a dark corner may yellow and drop older leaves, and lose its colour. Move it closer to a window, out of direct sun.

**5. Lack of nutrients.** A plant that has gone years without fresh mix or feeding may yellow evenly, oldest leaves first. Feed lightly in spring and summer, never more than the label says.

Yellow leaves do not turn green again. Remove them once you know the cause. The [yellow leaves guide](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) covers the same patterns on other plants.

## Why does my Chinese evergreen have brown tips or edges?

**1. Too much fertiliser.** Clemson lists excess fertiliser as a cause of burned leaf edges. A white crust on the soil or around the drainage hole confirms salt build-up. Flush the pot by running about twice its volume of water through it, let it drain, and feed half as often afterwards.

**2. Soil drying out completely.** If the soil regularly goes bone dry between waterings, leaf tips brown and crisp. Water as soon as the top few centimetres are dry rather than waiting for the whole pot.

**3. Cold water or cold air.** Clemson recommends room-temperature water. NC State advises against cold water. Cold glass in winter can brown the leaf parts that touch it.

**4. Direct sun.** Clemson says to keep the plant out of direct sun. Sunburn shows as dry, pale or brown patches in the middle of the leaf, on the side facing the window. Move it back.

Brown tissue stays brown. Trim the brown edge with clean scissors, following the leaf's shape, if it bothers you.

## Why is my Chinese evergreen drooping?

If the soil is dry, the plant is thirsty. Water slowly until water runs from the drainage hole. It should stand up within a day.

If the soil is wet and the plant still droops, do not water. Roots that sit in wet soil rot, and rotten roots cannot take up water, so the leaves wilt as if the plant were dry. Clemson's [watering factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) warns that adding water to a plant wilting from root rot makes it worse. Check the roots (next section).

A plant that droops after a cold night, and shows damaged patches on the leaves over the following days, probably has chilling injury. Move it somewhere warmer and wait for new growth before judging.

## Does my Chinese evergreen have root rot?

Clemson and NC State both name root rot as the main disease, caused by poor drainage or overwatering. The signs: yellowing leaves, drooping with wet soil, a soft, dark stem base, and a sour smell.

To check, slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and pale. Rotten roots are brown or black and mushy, and the outer layer slips off when pulled.

To treat:

1. Remove the old soil and cut off every soft root and any mushy stem tissue with clean scissors.
2. Repot in fresh, well-drained mix in a pot with a drainage hole, no bigger than the remaining roots need.
3. Water once and wait until the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) is dry before watering again.
4. Keep it warm, in bright indirect light, and skip fertiliser for a month or two.

If the stem base has rotted, cut off a healthy top section and root it. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers the method step by step.

## What pests attack Chinese evergreens?

Clemson lists mealybugs, aphids, scale and mites. NC State names the same group, with spider mites among them.

- **Mealybugs** look like white cotton tufts, often hidden where the leaf stalk meets the stem and in the tight centre of the plant.
- **Scale** looks like small brown or tan bumps on stems and leaf undersides.
- **Aphids** cluster on new growth.
- **Spider mites** leave fine pale speckling and, in heavy cases, webbing under the leaves.

Mealybugs, scale and aphids all leave sticky honeydew. For small infestations, [Clemson's houseplant insects factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/common-houseplant-insects-related-pests/) recommends a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol for mealybugs and aphids, and scraping scale off with a fingernail. Insecticidal soap works on contact only, so repeat it as the label directs. Keep the plant apart from others until it is clean. See the [common houseplant pests guide](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) for each pest.

## Why are there white powdery patches on the leaves?

Clemson mentions powdery mildew as a possible problem at high humidity. It looks like a white or grey film that rubs off on your finger. Remove the worst leaves, give the plant more space and air movement, and keep water off the leaves. Mealybugs, by contrast, are raised, cottony and move when poked.

## Why is my Chinese evergreen leggy, or losing its colour?

A long bare stem with a tuft of leaves at the top happens slowly as old leaves drop. Clemson suggests cutting off the top and rooting it, and the old base may produce new shoots.

Faded colour on a pink, red or silver variety usually means too little light. Give it a brighter spot with no direct sun. New leaves should come in with better colour.

## Quick answers

**Is the Chinese evergreen toxic to pets?** Yes. Clemson lists it as toxic to pets, and NC State rates it a low-severity poison. Keep it where cats and dogs cannot chew it.

**Do I need to raise the humidity?** Usually not. Clemson notes it tolerates low humidity.

**Can I cut off brown leaves?** Yes. Cut whole leaves at the base of the stalk, or trim just the brown edge.
