How to prune houseplants comes down to three rules: cut just above a node with clean, sharp shears, do the big cuts in spring as growth restarts, and never remove more than about a third of the plant at once. A node is the bump on the stem where a leaf grows, and the new shoots come from there. Below you will find why that works, what to cut on each kind of plant, and what to leave alone.

## Why pruning makes a plant bushier

The growing tip of each stem produces a hormone called auxin that flows down the stem and holds back the buds below it. Botanists call this apical dominance. It is why an unpruned pothos or philodendron grows into one long vine with leaves only at the end.

When you cut off or pinch the tip, the auxin supply stops and one or more of the dormant buds at the nodes just below the cut start to grow. One stem becomes two or three. Done regularly, this turns a leggy plant into a full one.

Two other hormones matter for the plant's response:

- **Cytokinins**, made in the roots, push those side buds to grow. A plant with a healthy root system responds to pruning faster.
- **Auxin** also triggers root formation on cuttings, which is why rooting powders contain a synthetic auxin. Our guide to [plant propagation from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers how to use the trimmings.

## When to prune houseplants

Spring and early summer are best, when longer days drive new growth and the plant can quickly replace what you remove. That timing also lines up with repotting, so the [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) is a good companion.

Some tasks can happen any time of year:

- removing dead, yellow or diseased leaves;
- trimming brown tips;
- cutting off spent flowers;
- cutting back a stem damaged by rot or pests.

Avoid hard pruning in late autumn and winter. With little light, the plant cannot replace lost leaves and may simply stay bare until spring.

## Tools and preparation

- **Sharp bypass pruners or scissors** for soft and woody stems. Sharp blades make a clean cut that seals quickly. Blunt ones crush the stem.
- **Clean blades.** Wipe them with rubbing alcohol before you start and between plants, so you don't carry disease from one plant to the next.
- **Gloves** for plants with irritating sap, such as ficus, euphorbia, dieffenbachia and other aroids.

Water the plant a day before a big prune so it is not stressed.

## How to prune houseplants step by step

1. **Look at the plant from all sides.** Decide on the shape you want before you cut anything.
2. **Remove dead and damaged material first.** Yellow or brown leaves, mushy stems and spent flowers. Cut them back to healthy tissue or to the base.
3. **Find the node.** Follow a stem down from its tip to the point where a leaf or leaf scar joins it.
4. **Cut about 5 mm (1/4 in) above the node.** Cutting too close can damage the bud. Leaving a long stub invites rot. A slight angle, sloping away from the bud, lets water run off.
5. **Choose the node by direction.** The new shoot will grow the way that node's leaf faces. Cut above an outward-facing node to open up the plant.
6. **Step back and repeat.** Stop at about a third of the total foliage.

## Pinching: the gentlest pruning

Pinching removes just the soft tip of a stem with your thumbnail and forefinger, or with small scissors. The [University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/grooming-indoor-plants) describes it as removing 2.5 cm (1 in) or less of new stem and leaf growth just above a node, which stimulates side branching.

Pinch regularly during the growing season on:

- **Trailing plants:** pothos, philodendron, tradescantia, string of hearts.
- **Herbs:** basil, mint. Pinch above a pair of leaves before the plant flowers.
- **Soft, bushy plants:** coleus, polka dot plant, pilea, peperomia.

## How to prune different kinds of houseplants

- **Vining plants (pothos, heartleaf philodendron, ivy).** Cut long, bare vines back to a node, even by half their length. New shoots will appear along the remaining stem. Root the cut pieces and plant them back in the same pot for a fuller look. Our [pothos care guide](/en/blog/pothos-care-guide) shows this in context.
- **Climbing aroids (monstera, larger philodendrons).** Remove whole stems or leaves at the base where they crowd the plant. Cut tall stems above a node to control height. The [monstera care guide](/en/blog/monstera-care-guide) explains how to train them on a support.
- **Upright woody plants (fiddle-leaf fig, rubber plant, schefflera).** Cut the main stem above a node to encourage branching. These plants usually push one to three new branches just below the cut. Expect milky sap from figs.
- **Cane plants (dracaena, yucca).** Cut the cane at the height you want. New leaves sprout below the cut, and the top can be rooted.
- **Rosette plants (snake plant, ZZ plant, bromeliads, most succulents).** These don't branch from nodes. Prune only by removing whole damaged leaves at the base.
- **Palms.** Never cut the top. A palm grows from a single point, and removing it kills the stem. Remove only fully brown fronds.

## Grooming: brown tips, yellow leaves and spent flowers

Most indoor pruning is grooming. UMD recommends trimming brown tips with sharp scissors and removing yellow leaves, spent flowers and dead branches.

- **Brown tips.** Follow the natural shape of the leaf and leave a thin line of brown so you don't cut into live tissue.
- **Yellow leaves.** Remove them once they are mostly yellow. The plant has already reclaimed what nutrients it could.
- **Spent flowers.** Cut flower stems back to the base. For orchids, cut above a node on the flower spike or at the base, depending on the type.

## Aftercare

After a hard prune, the plant has fewer leaves and uses less water. Water less often until new growth appears, and hold off on fertiliser for 2 to 4 weeks. Keep the plant in bright, indirect light so the side buds have the energy to grow. ## Mistakes to avoid

- Cutting between nodes, which leaves a stub that dries out and produces nothing.
- Removing more than a third of the foliage in one session.
- Pruning hard in winter.
- Using dirty or blunt tools.
- Cutting the growing tip of a palm or the centre of a rosette plant.
