Dieffenbachia care comes down to bright, filtered light, a thorough watering whenever the top 2.5 cm (1 in) of soil has dried, and a room between 16 and 24°C (60 and 75°F) away from cold draughts. It is an easy, fast-growing foliage plant that reaches 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft) indoors. The one thing to take seriously is its sap, which burns the mouth and throat and is toxic to children and pets.

## Dieffenbachia care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, filtered light most of the year; bright light or some direct sun in winter. Tolerates low light but grows slowly |
| Water | Water thoroughly, then let the top 2.5 cm (1 in) dry. Roughly weekly in summer, every 10 to 14 days in winter |
| Humidity | Prefers higher humidity but copes with normal room air |
| Temperature | 16 to 24°C (60 to 75°F). No cold draughts or sudden swings |
| Soil | Loose, rich potting mix that drains well, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Foliage fertiliser at half strength every 2 to 4 weeks, March to September |
| Toxicity | Toxic to people, cats and dogs. Wear gloves when cutting it |

The light, watering, temperature and feeding figures come from [Clemson Cooperative Extension's dieffenbachia factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/dieffenbachia/). The plant, also called dumb cane, comes from Central America, the Caribbean and tropical South America.

## How much light does a dieffenbachia need?

Bright but filtered. Clemson recommends bright filtered light in spring, summer and autumn, and bright light, even direct sun, in winter when the sun is weak. In practice, a spot a metre or two back from a south- or west-facing window, or right beside an east-facing one, works well. Strong summer sun through glass can scorch the leaves, leaving pale or brown, papery patches.

It tolerates lower light, which is why it is often sold as an office plant, but growth slows, the stems stretch and the white or cream patterning can fade. Dieffenbachia also grows toward the window, so turn the pot a quarter turn every week or two to keep it upright. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) helps you judge a spot.

## How often should you water a dieffenbachia?

Water thoroughly, until water runs out of the drainage hole, then wait until the top 2.5 cm (1 in) of soil is dry to the touch before watering again. Both Clemson and [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/dieffenbachia-seguine/) give that rule, and both warn that frequent watering or poor drainage leads to root rot. Empty the saucer after 15 minutes.

As a starting point:

- **Spring and summer:** about once a week in a bright, warm room. A large plant in a small pot may need more.
- **Autumn and winter:** every 10 to 14 days, sometimes less in a cool room. Growth slows and the soil dries more slowly.

Check the soil with a finger each time rather than following the calendar. Signs of too much water are yellowing lower leaves, a stem that turns soft at the base and soil that smells sour. Signs of too little are drooping leaves that perk up within hours of watering, and brown, dry leaf edges.

## What humidity and temperature does a dieffenbachia need?

NC State says it prefers high humidity but also lists it as humidity tolerant. Normal room air is usually fine. If the leaf edges turn brown in winter when the heating is on, move it away from radiators and use a humidifier in the room. Misting wets the leaves for a few minutes without changing the room's humidity, and wet foliage encourages leaf spot. Our guide to [raising humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

Clemson gives 16 to 24°C (60 to 75°F) as the ideal range and says to protect the plant from cold and sudden temperature changes. Keep it away from doors that open onto winter air, cold window panes and air-conditioning vents. Cold damage shows as dark, water-soaked patches on the leaves a day or two later.

In summer, the same rules apply: filtered light, water by the soil, and no spot in front of an air-conditioning vent. A summer outdoors is possible in deep shade once nights stay above about 16°C (60°F), but most plants do fine staying inside.

## What soil and pot does a dieffenbachia need?

NC State recommends a loose, fertile soil rich in organic matter with good drainage. A good houseplant potting mix with a couple of handfuls of perlite per litre works. Always use a pot with a drainage hole.

Repot in spring when roots circle the bottom or grow out of the hole, usually every year or two for a young plant. Move up only 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) in diameter. A tall plant gets top-heavy, so a heavier pot, such as terracotta or glazed ceramic, keeps it from tipping. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) covers mixes in more detail.

## Should you fertilise a dieffenbachia?

Yes, during the growing season. Clemson suggests feeding from March to September with a foliage houseplant fertiliser, either a liquid at half strength every two to four weeks or a slow-release product following the label. Do not feed in winter. Too much fertiliser burns the leaf edges; if you see brown margins and a white crust on the soil, flush the pot with plain water and feed less often.

## Why does a dieffenbachia lose its lower leaves?

Some loss is normal. Clemson notes that lower leaves drop as the plant matures, leaving a bare, cane-like stem with a tuft of leaves at the top. Losing one old leaf every few weeks is part of how the plant grows.

Several yellow leaves at once are a different matter. That usually points to overwatering, cold or a sudden move. Our guide to [dieffenbachia problems](/en/blog/dieffenbachia-problems) goes through each symptom.

## How do you prune a leggy dieffenbachia?

Cut it back. Clemson's advice for a leggy plant is to cut the stem back to about 15 cm (6 in) tall; new shoots grow from below the cut. Do it in spring or early summer so it regrows quickly.

Wear gloves, and wash the knife and your hands afterwards. Keep your hands away from your eyes: NC State warns that the sap irritates skin and can damage the cornea.

## How do you propagate a dieffenbachia?

The pieces you cut off become new plants. Clemson lists cane cuttings, stem tip cuttings and air layering, in spring or early summer.

- **Tip cuttings.** Take the leafy top with 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) of stem, remove the lowest leaf or two, and root it in water or moist potting mix.
- **Cane cuttings.** Cut the bare stem into pieces 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 in) long, each with at least one node (the ring where a leaf was attached). Lay them on their side, half buried in moist potting mix, and keep them warm. New shoots appear from the nodes over several weeks.

Our guide to [propagating plants from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers the general technique.

## Common dieffenbachia pests

NC State lists spider mites, scale, whiteflies, fungus gnats and aphids; Clemson adds mealybugs. Check the undersides of the leaves and the leaf bases every couple of weeks. Wipe off mealybugs and scale with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, spray spider mites and aphids with insecticidal soap, repeating weekly for a few rounds, and let the top of the soil dry more between waterings if fungus gnats appear.

## Quick answers

**Is dieffenbachia poisonous?** Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/dieffenbachia) lists it as toxic to cats and dogs because of insoluble calcium oxalates and a proteolytic enzyme, with signs including intense burning of the mouth, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. Clemson warns that chewing it can make a person's throat swell and advises immediate medical care. Keep it out of reach of children and pets.

**Why is it called dumb cane?** Because chewing the stem makes the mouth and throat swell and can make it hard to speak.

**Does dieffenbachia clean the air?** Not measurably. A potted plant in a normal room removes too little to matter.
