Fiddle leaf fig care comes down to a bright spot near a window, watering only when the top 5 cm (2 in) of soil has dried, and a stable room that never drops below 13°C (55°F). The plant (*Ficus lyrata*) reacts to most stress by dropping leaves, so the other half of the job is changing as little as possible: same spot, same watering habit, no draughts. Prune in spring or early summer if you want branches.

## Fiddle leaf fig care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light. Up to a few hours of morning sun; no hot afternoon sun |
| Water | When the top 5 cm (2 in) is dry, then until water runs out of the drainage hole |
| Humidity | Medium. Normal room air is fine; keep it away from heaters and air vents |
| Temperature | Normal room temperature, steady day to day; never below 13°C (55°F) |
| Soil | Fast-draining houseplant mix with bark or perlite, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Diluted liquid fertiliser about once a month, spring to late summer |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats and dogs if chewed; the milky sap irritates skin |

The light, temperature, soil and toxicity figures come from [NC State Extension's plant profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ficus-lyrata/). The fiddle leaf fig is a tree from the tropical forests of western Africa. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/7207/ficus-lyrata/details) notes that its leaves reach 45 cm (18 in) long and that it is frost tender, which is why it lives indoors in most of Europe and North America.

## How much light does a fiddle leaf fig need?

More than most houseplants. NC State recommends bright indirect light or partial shade, with no more than 2 to 6 hours of direct sun, and warns that strong afternoon sun scorches the leaves. In practice that means right beside an east-facing window, or 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft) back from a south- or west-facing one, or behind a sheer curtain.

A fiddle leaf fig in a dim corner does not die quickly. It stops growing, drops its lower leaves one by one and leans hard toward the window. If yours has bare lower stems and small new leaves, light is the first thing to fix. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot without guessing.

Turn the pot a quarter turn every week or two. The plant grows toward the light, and regular turning keeps the trunk straight and the canopy even.

Dust cuts down the light reaching the leaves. Wipe both sides with a damp cloth every few weeks. It is also the easiest way to spot pests early.

## How often should you water a fiddle leaf fig?

Water when the top 5 cm (2 in) of soil is dry, not on a fixed day. Push a finger into the soil to check. Then water slowly until water runs out of the drainage hole, and empty the saucer after 15 minutes.

In a bright room in summer that usually works out to about once a week. In winter it can stretch to every two or three weeks, because the plant uses much less water in short days. NC State describes the plant as sensitive to overwatering and notes that leaf drop can come from too much water or too little. The common thread is a root ball that swings between soggy and bone dry. Our article on [watering a fiddle leaf fig](/en/blog/fiddle-leaf-fig-watering) goes through the signs of each.

Two habits cause most trouble: a decorative pot with no drainage hole, and small sips of water every few days. The first drowns the bottom of the root ball. The second keeps the top wet and leaves the roots below dry.

## What temperature and humidity does it need?

A normal heated home suits it. NC State asks you to keep it above 13°C (55°F) and notes that fluctuating temperatures from heating or cooling vents cause brown spots on the leaves.

What hurts a fiddle leaf fig is change, not the exact number. A cold draught from a door opened every evening, a radiator under the window or an air-conditioning vent blowing on it are all enough to make it shed leaves. Medium humidity is fine. Very dry winter air near a heater can brown leaf edges; moving the plant away from the heater does more than any spray bottle.

## What soil and pot does a fiddle leaf fig need?

Use a fast-draining houseplant mix. A general potting compost with about a third perlite or fine bark added is a good start. NC State describes the plant's preference as moist, well-drained, slightly acidic soil. Dense, peaty soil that stays wet for a week is the main route to root rot. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) gives mixes by plant type.

The pot must have a drainage hole. Repot in spring only when roots circle the inside of the pot or poke out of the hole, usually every one to two years for a young plant. Go up one size, about 5 cm (2 in) wider. A much bigger pot holds more wet soil than the roots can use, and that soil stays wet. Large, mature plants can stay in the same pot for years if you replace the top few centimetres of soil each spring.

## How and when should you fertilise a fiddle leaf fig?

Feed only while it is growing, from spring to late summer. A balanced liquid houseplant fertiliser, diluted to half the label strength and given about once a month, is a safe starting point. Many brands sell a feed labelled for fiddle leaf figs; it works, but it is not required.

Stop feeding in autumn and winter. With little light the plant cannot use the extra nutrients, and the unused salts build up in the soil and burn root tips. If you see a white crust on the soil or the rim of the pot, run plain water through the pot for a minute to flush it. Our guide on [how to fertilise houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) covers strengths and timing in more detail.

Pale new leaves on a plant that has never been fed, or has sat in the same soil for years, are a sign it needs feeding or fresh soil. Dark, healthy leaves on a plant that is not growing usually mean it needs more light, not more fertiliser.

## How do you prune a fiddle leaf fig?

Prune in spring or early summer, while the plant is growing fast enough to heal and push new shoots. Pruning in winter does not harm it, but the cut often just sits there until spring.

To make it branch, cut the main stem just above a leaf, at the height where you want the branches to start. New shoots usually grow from one or two of the buds just below the cut. To keep it smaller, cut back the tallest stem the same way. To clean it up, remove damaged or crowded leaves at the stem.

A few practical rules:

1. Use clean, sharp secateurs, and wipe the blades with rubbing alcohol first.
2. Wear gloves. The milky sap that runs from the cut irritates skin, and the RHS lists the plant as a skin allergen.
3. Remove no more than about a quarter of the leaves in one session. Leaves feed the plant, and a fig stripped too hard stalls.
4. Hold a paper towel to the cut until the sap stops running. It dries on its own within minutes.

Healthy stem tips you cut off can be rooted in water or moist potting mix.

## Why does my fiddle leaf fig drop leaves?

Leaf drop is the fiddle leaf fig's general stress signal. The usual causes, in rough order of how often they turn up:

- **It was moved.** A plant brought home from the shop or moved to a new room often drops a few leaves over the next few weeks. Leave it in its new spot and keep watering by the soil.
- **The watering swings.** Soggy soil for long stretches, or soil left dry until the plant wilts, both lead to leaves yellowing or browning and then falling. Lower leaves go first.
- **Too little light.** Lower leaves drop one by one, new leaves are smaller, and the plant leans. Move it closer to the window.
- **Cold or a draught.** Several leaves drop within days after a cold night or near an opened door. Move it to a stable spot.

Dark brown patches that spread from the middle of the leaf or the leaf base, together with soil that stays wet, point to root rot. Check the roots: firm and pale is healthy, soft and dark is rot. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers what to do.

Pests are less common but worth checking when leaves show fine speckling, sticky patches or tiny bumps. NC State lists scale, mealybugs, thrips, aphids and spider mites. Our article on [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) shows how to identify and treat each one.

## How should you care for a fiddle leaf fig in autumn and winter?

As the days shorten, the plant slows down, and the routine has to slow down with it:

- **Move it closer to the light.** A spot that was perfect in June may be too dim in December. A window that was too sunny in summer is often right in winter.
- **Water less often.** Keep checking the top 5 cm (2 in). In most homes the gap between waterings doubles.
- **Stop feeding** from early autumn until spring.
- **Keep it away from heat and cold.** Radiators, wood stoves, heating vents and single-glazed windows on frosty nights are the usual culprits for winter leaf drop.

If you keep herbs on the same windowsill, grow them in their own pots. Basil, rosemary and other kitchen herbs want several hours of direct sun and a different watering rhythm, so they are poor pot-mates for a fig.
