Rosemary care is simple once you treat it as the Mediterranean shrub it is: at least 6 hours of direct sun a day, a free-draining gritty compost, and water only when the top of the pot has dried. It hates wet roots far more than dry ones. Established plants in the ground survive down to about -8°C (18°F), but a pot is more exposed, and indoors it wants a cool, very bright spot of 10 to 16°C (50 to 60°F) through winter.

## Rosemary care at a glance

| Need | What it wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Full sun, 6 or more hours of direct sun a day |
| Water | When the top few centimetres are dry; never waterlogged |
| Humidity | Ordinary outdoor or room air with good airflow |
| Temperature | Hardy to about -8°C (18°F) in the ground; 10 to 16°C (50 to 60°F) indoors in winter |
| Soil | Soil-based compost with up to 25% grit, pH 6.5 to 7.0 |
| Pets | Non-toxic to cats and dogs |

Rosemary's botanical name is now *Salvia rosmarinus*. You will still see *Rosmarinus officinalis* on plant labels. They are the same plant.

## How much sun does rosemary need?

As much as you can give it. University of Illinois Extension puts it at more than 6 hours of direct sun a day, and [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/herb-garden-plants-rosemary) lists it as a full sun plant. Outdoors, that means the sunniest bed or a pot against a south-facing wall. Indoors, a south-facing window is the minimum, and a grow light helps a lot in winter.

Rosemary in too little light grows long, soft stems with widely spaced leaves, and the flavour is weaker. It also becomes more prone to the problems below.

## How often should you water rosemary?

Water when the top few centimetres of compost feel dry, then water thoroughly and let the pot drain. The [RHS rosemary guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/herbs/rosemary/grow-your-own) is blunt about the rule that matters: never let it become waterlogged, because cold, wet compost rots the roots.

Rosemary in the ground needs watering in its first summer while it settles in. After that it copes with dry spells on its own.

In pots the picture is different. Containers in full summer sun can dry out within a few days, so check them often. In winter, especially indoors, the plant uses far less water and the risk flips to overwatering. NC State names overwatering as the most common cause of rosemary decline and death. Penn State adds that this is especially true of pots brought inside for winter.

## What soil and pot does rosemary need?

The RHS recommends a pot at least 30 cm (1 ft) wide and deep, filled with a peat-free, soil-based compost mixed with up to 25% grit for drainage. Penn State gives a pH range of 6.5 to 7.0. The pot needs drainage holes, and terracotta helps the compost dry between waterings. Our guide to [the best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains how to mix grit into a bagged compost.

There is no need for a layer of gravel at the bottom of the pot. Mix the grit through the compost instead. A gravel layer does not improve drainage and can leave the compost above it wetter.

## Does rosemary need humidity?

No. Some older guides give rosemary a target humidity range or suggest misting it. NC State says the opposite: rosemary has a low tolerance for wet, humid conditions, and poor air circulation with high humidity encourages powdery mildew. Indoors, keep it away from steamy kitchens and bathrooms and give it a spot with some air movement. Outdoors, avoid crowding it among taller plants.

## How to care for rosemary indoors in winter

Overwintering indoors is where most potted rosemary fails. Penn State suggests bringing pots inside 2 to 3 weeks before your first expected frost, so the plant adjusts before the heating comes on.

The problem is that homes are too warm and too dark in winter. Illinois Extension recommends a spot that is cool, around 10 to 16°C (50 to 60°F), with very bright light. A sunny window in an unheated room, a bright porch or a cool hallway window all work. In a warm living room, add a grow light and water even more carefully.

Water sparingly through winter, but do not let the root ball dry out completely either. If you live somewhere mild enough to keep it outdoors, give the pot shelter from winter rain, since cold and wet together is what kills it.

For more on getting potted plants through the dark months, see [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter).

## Does rosemary need fertiliser?

Very little. The RHS says rosemary in the ground needs no feeding at all. Container plants more than a year old benefit from a sprinkle of general granular fertiliser in late spring or early summer. Heavy feeding produces soft, leggy growth with less flavour.

## How to prune and harvest rosemary

You can harvest rosemary at any time of year. Penn State notes that sprigs for drying are best cut just before the plant flowers, and the RHS says the best flavour comes from the soft new growth of late spring and summer. Cut stem tips with clean scissors, and take only what you need from a small plant in winter, when it barely regrows.

To dry it, the RHS suggests hanging sprigs in a warm, dark, ventilated place, then stripping the leaves once they are brittle.

Prune the whole plant lightly after it flowers to keep it bushy. Do not cut back into old, bare wood, because rosemary rarely regrows from it. The RHS notes that plants get woody and sparse with age and are often best replaced after 7 or 8 years. Our guide on [how to prune houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-prune-houseplants) covers the cuts.

## How to propagate rosemary from cuttings

Cuttings are the easy way to replace an old plant. Penn State's method:

1. Cut 15 cm (6 in) tips from healthy, non-flowering stems.
2. Strip the leaves from the lower half.
3. Dip the cut end in rooting hormone.
4. Insert the cuttings in a sterile, soilless mix and keep it lightly moist.
5. Keep them warm, ideally with bottom heat of 21 to 24°C (70 to 75°F).

Roots usually form in 10 to 20 days. The RHS recommends softwood cuttings in late spring or summer. Layering also works outdoors: pin a low stem to the soil, and once it has rooted, cut it from the parent. Our [guide to propagating from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) has more detail on rooting mixes.

## Common rosemary problems

Root rot from wet compost is the big one. Browning needles and tips can be its first sign, according to Penn State. Indoors, watch for aphids, mealybugs, whiteflies, spider mites and thrips, and for powdery mildew, a white dusting on the leaves in still, humid air. Outdoors in Europe, the rosemary beetle, a small striped metallic beetle, eats the leaves of rosemary, lavender and sage, though the RHS says healthy plants usually tolerate it. ## Is rosemary safe for cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/rosemary) lists rosemary as non-toxic to dogs, cats and horses.
