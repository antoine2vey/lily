Most English ivy problems indoors come from a room that is too warm and dry, too dark, or watered too often. Crisp brown leaf edges usually mean warm, dry air or soil that dried out completely; fine yellow speckling with webbing underneath is spider mites, by far the most common pest on indoor ivy; and yellow leaves on soil that is still wet mean overwatering. Below, each symptom has its likely causes in order, how to tell them apart, and the fix.

## English ivy problems: check the room first

Ivy is a hardy outdoor plant. Indoors, [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/houseplants/english-ivy) recommends bright light, cool temperatures and moist soil, and says to inspect for spider mites regularly. So before looking at the leaves, check three things: how warm the spot is, how far it is from a window, and how wet the soil is 2 to 3 cm (1 in) down. A spot near a radiator, a heating vent or on a hot sunny windowsill counts as warm.

## Why are my English ivy leaves turning brown and crispy?

**1. Warm, dry air.** This is the most common cause indoors. Brown, crisp edges and tips, mostly on the leaves nearest a radiator or a heating vent, appearing in autumn and winter. Move the plant somewhere cooler, away from heat sources. A humidifier helps if it has to stay in a warm room; misting does not raise humidity for more than a few minutes.

**2. The soil dried out completely.** If the plant wilted before the browning started, or the soil has pulled away from the sides of the pot, it went too dry. Water thoroughly and check the soil more often. Hanging baskets dry fastest.

**3. Spider mites.** Leaves that first turn dull and finely speckled, then bronze, brown and dry, often with fine webbing between leaves and stems, have spider mites. Look at the undersides with a magnifying glass. See the pest section below.

**4. Too much fertiliser.** Brown, burnt-looking edges on many leaves at once, with a white crust on the soil, point to salt build-up. Run plain water through the pot for a minute or two at the sink and skip feeding for two months.

**5. Sun scorch.** Pale or brown, papery patches only on leaves touching or facing the glass of a sunny window. Move it back a little or add a sheer curtain.

Brown tissue will not recover. Trim it off and judge the plant by its new growth.

## Why are my English ivy leaves turning yellow?

**1. Spider mites.** If the yellow shows as many tiny pale dots rather than whole yellow leaves, it is mite feeding. The [Connecticut Agricultural Experiment Station](https://portal.ct.gov/caes/home/plant-pest-handbook/pphi/ivy-english-hedera) describes the symptom as yellow stippling on the leaves, with webbing in severe cases.

**2. The soil stays too wet.** Several whole leaves turning yellow, starting low on the stems, while the soil is still damp days after watering. Usual causes are watering on a fixed schedule, a pot without a drainage hole, a saucer full of water, or a pot much larger than the root ball. Let the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) dry before watering again.

**3. Too little light.** Yellowing older leaves on long, bare stems with small, widely spaced new leaves. Move it close to a bright window.

**4. Normal ageing.** A few of the oldest leaves at the base of the stems yellowing now and then is normal. Pull them off.

## Why is my English ivy drooping or wilting?

**1. Dry soil.** The pot is light and the soil dry. Water slowly until it drains, and the stems should firm up within hours.

**2. Root rot.** Wilting while the soil is wet means the roots cannot take up water. Check them as described below.

**3. Heat.** A plant on a hot windowsill in summer can wilt in the afternoon even with damp soil, then recover overnight. Move it out of the hottest spot.

## Why is my English ivy losing leaves?

Leaf drop is usually the result of one of the problems above, so look for the other symptoms:

- **Leaves fall dry and speckled:** spider mites.
- **Leaves fall yellow from the base of the stems:** too much water or too little light.
- **Leaves fall crisp, in winter, near heating:** warm, dry air.
- **Leaves drop after a move:** a sudden change in light or temperature, such as bringing it home from a bright shop or moving it from a balcony indoors. It should settle within a few weeks if conditions are good.

## Why is my variegated ivy turning green?

Variegated ivies sometimes produce shoots with plain green leaves. The RHS notes that these shoots have reverted and advises removing them as soon as you see them. Low light makes it worse, so move a variegated plant to the brightest spot you have.

## What are the brown or black spots on my ivy leaves?

**1. Bacterial leaf spot.** The Connecticut handbook describes bacterial leaf spot (Xanthomonas) as pale green, irregular, water-soaked spots with yellow or translucent borders that later turn dark. It spreads in water splashed from leaf to leaf.

**2. Fungal leaf spot.** Larger, round to irregular tan spots, sometimes with rings of tiny dark dots.

For both, remove the spotted leaves, water the soil rather than the foliage, and give the plant more air around it. The RHS says leaf spots on ivy are unsightly but have little effect on the plant's vigour. Our guide to [houseplant fungal diseases](/en/blog/houseplant-fungal-diseases) covers leaf spots in more detail.

**3. Cold or sun damage.** Dark, water-soaked patches after a frost on a balcony plant, or papery brown patches on the sunny side, are damage rather than disease. Cut them off.

## How do I know if my English ivy has root rot?

The signs are wilting on wet soil, several yellow leaves at once and stems that go soft and dark at the soil line. Slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and white to tan; rotten roots are brown, mushy and smell sour.

1. Shake or rinse off the old soil.
2. Cut every soft, dark root back to firm tissue with clean scissors.
3. Repot in fresh potting mix with some perlite, in a clean pot with a drainage hole, only slightly larger than the remaining roots.
4. Water once, let it drain, and wait for the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) to dry before watering again.

If few healthy roots remain, take a few stem cuttings as insurance. Ivy roots easily in a glass of water. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers severe cases.

## What pests attack English ivy indoors?

[NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/hedera-helix/) lists aphids, mealybugs, mites and scale among the pests of English ivy. Indoors, spider mites are the one to watch.

- **Spider mites.** Tiny, eight-legged, barely visible. Signs are pale speckling, then bronzing, and fine webbing between leaves and stems. They multiply fastest in warm, dry air. The Connecticut handbook says control should begin with keeping the air in the home somewhat humid. Rinse the whole plant in the shower, tops and undersides, then spray with insecticidal soap, repeating every 5 to 7 days for three or four rounds, since soap does not kill the eggs. Then move the plant somewhere cooler. Our guide to [spider mite treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) has the full routine.
- **Aphids.** Small green or black insects clustered on new shoot tips, with sticky honeydew below. A strong spray of water removes most; insecticidal soap deals with the rest.
- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony clumps in the leaf joints. Dab each one with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then treat with insecticidal soap every 7 to 10 days until they stop coming back.
- **Scale.** Small, flat, brown or pale oval bumps on stems and leaf undersides that do not move, often with sticky leaves below. Scrape them off with a fingernail or soft toothbrush, then treat as for mealybugs. Soap only kills what it touches, so repeat it.

Keep an infested ivy away from your other plants until it has been clean for a few weeks. Ivy is dense, so pests hide in the middle; trimming crowded stems makes treatment easier. Our article on [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers each one in more depth.

## Quick answers

**Is English ivy toxic to cats and dogs?** Yes. The ASPCA lists it as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. It contains triterpenoid saponins and can cause vomiting, abdominal pain, drooling and diarrhoea. NC State notes that the sap can also irritate skin.

**How do I keep these problems from coming back?** Give it a bright, cool spot away from heating, water when the top of the soil is dry rather than on a schedule, and look under a few leaves every week or two. The full routine is in our [English ivy care guide](/en/blog/english-ivy-care-guide).
