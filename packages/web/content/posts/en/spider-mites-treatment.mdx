Spider mites treatment on a houseplant takes about three weeks: isolate the plant, rinse the undersides of the leaves hard, then spray an insecticidal soap or horticultural oil every 5 to 7 days, covering every leaf underside, until no new speckling appears. Mites breed fastest in warm, dry air, which is why heated rooms suit them. Sprays only kill the mites they touch, so one spray is never enough.

## How do you know it is spider mites?

Spider mites are tiny arachnids, related to spiders and ticks rather than insects. The [UC Statewide IPM Program](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/PMG/PESTNOTES/pn7405.html), the University of California's pest management service, describes adults as less than 1/20 inch (about 1 mm) long, living in colonies on the undersides of leaves. The species most often found on houseplants is the two-spotted or glasshouse red spider mite. The [Royal Horticultural Society](https://www.rhs.org.uk/biodiversity/glasshouse-red-spider-mite) notes that despite the name, it is usually yellowish green with two dark patches, and only turns orange-red in autumn and winter.

Signs, in the order you are likely to see them:

1. **Fine pale speckling on the upper side of the leaves.** UC IPM calls this stippling: light dots where mites have sucked out cell contents. Leaves can also look dull, dusty or bronzed.
2. **Tiny moving dots under the leaves**, with white cast skins and round eggs, easiest to see with a 10x hand lens.
3. **Fine silk webbing** between leaves, along stems and at leaf joints. UC IPM notes that the webbing is the easiest way to tell spider mites from other small pests, such as aphids and thrips, that also live under leaves.
4. **Yellowing and falling leaves** in heavy infestations.

**The paper test.** Hold a sheet of white paper under a leaf and tap the stem. UC IPM suggests shaking a few mites onto white paper to see them better: once disturbed, they move around quickly. Do it before you treat. UC IPM's advice is to make sure mites are present first, because sometimes they are gone by the time you notice the damage, and the plant recovers on its own.

Speckling can be confused with other problems. Thrips leave silvery streaks and black dots of droppings, but no webbing; our guide on [how to get rid of thrips](/en/blog/how-to-get-rid-of-thrips) shows the difference. Sunburn and thirst cause brown or pale patches without any moving specks.

## Why do houseplants get spider mites?

The RHS says the mite does best in warm, dry conditions and that severe damage can develop quickly in them. It gives the speed: at 10°C (50°F) one generation takes about 55 days, at 21°C (70°F) only 12. A heated living room in winter is close to ideal for it.

UC IPM adds that water-stressed plants are the most susceptible, and that broad-spectrum insecticides used against other pests often trigger mite outbreaks by killing their natural enemies. UC IPM notes that as an infested plant declines, females ride air currents to other plants, and the RHS notes that very few plants are immune. The NC State plant pages for calathea, pilea, hoya and cactus all list spider mites among the pests to watch.

## Which spider mites treatment works on houseplants?

Work through these steps in order. The first four are the treatment for a single plant; the last two matter for a larger collection.

### 1. Isolate the plant

Move it away from your other plants as soon as you suspect mites, and check its neighbours with the paper test. Mites crawl from leaf to leaf and travel on hands and tools, so wash your hands after handling it.

### 2. Cut off the worst leaves

Remove leaves that are heavily speckled, yellow or covered in webbing, and bag them straight away. They will not recover, and UC IPM notes that a single colony can hold hundreds of mites.

### 3. Rinse the plant

Take the plant to the shower or sink and spray it with lukewarm water, concentrating on the undersides of the leaves and the leaf joints. Hold the pot on its side or cover the mix so you do not flood the roots. UC IPM recommends a water spray or mist on the undersides of the leaves at least once a day for infested plants, and notes that forceful spraying often reduces numbers enough on its own in gardens. Indoors, a full rinse every few days and a daily mist of the leaf undersides in between follows that advice.

### 4. Spray insecticidal soap or horticultural oil

UC IPM recommends insecticidal soap or oil, or a mix of the two, when water alone is not enough. Use a ready-made product labelled for houseplants rather than washing-up liquid, which is not made for plants and varies in strength.

- **Coverage is everything.** UC IPM notes that soaps and oils only kill mites they touch. Spray until the undersides of every leaf are wet, as well as the tops and the stems.
- **Test first.** UC IPM warns these products can injure some plants. Spray a few leaves and wait several days before treating the whole plant.
- **Do not spray a dry or hot plant.** UC IPM advises against using soaps and oils on water-stressed plants or above 32°C (90°F). Water the day before, and keep a treated plant out of direct sun until it dries.
- **Repeat.** UC IPM notes that repeat applications may be needed. With a new generation every 12 days or so at room temperature, by the RHS figures, spray every 5 to 7 days for at least three weeks to catch each new hatch.

### 5. Consider predatory mites for a large collection

The RHS lists *Phytoseiulus persimilis* as the most commonly used biological control, with *Amblyseius* mites as another option. They eat spider mites and do not harm plants. They work best in a conservatory, a greenhouse or a large group of plants where they can spread. UC IPM notes they do not feed on plants, so they starve or move on once the pest mites are gone. UC IPM gives a guideline of one predator for every 10 spider mites. Do not combine them with soap or oil sprays, which kill predators too.

### 6. Avoid general-purpose insecticides

UC IPM warns that some common insecticides, such as certain pyrethroids and organophosphates, are not very effective against mites, kill their predators and can make outbreaks worse.

## How do you know the treatment worked?

Damaged leaves stay speckled, so judge by new growth and by the paper test. After three weeks, if new leaves come in clean, no webbing reappears and nothing crawls on the paper, the plant can return to its spot. Keep checking it weekly for another month, and check the plants that stood next to it.

If speckling and webbing keep coming back after a month of treatment, the plant may not be worth saving. Take clean cuttings from the healthiest growth, rinse them well, and discard the rest. ## How do you prevent spider mites?

- **Raise the humidity in winter.** The RHS notes that spraying plants with water and keeping humidity high reduces the risk, but will not control mites on its own. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the methods.
- **Avoid letting plants dry out completely.** UC IPM links water stress to more mites and more damage. Plants that are meant to dry out, such as cacti, should still be watered properly when they need it.
- **Quarantine new plants** for two to three weeks and check their leaf undersides before they join the others. Our guide on [how to acclimate new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) includes a quarantine routine.
- **Check undersides when you water**, especially from autumn to spring, and wipe dusty leaves with a damp cloth. Our [winter houseplant care guide](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers dry air and heating.

## Quick answers

**Are spider mites harmful to people or pets?** No. They feed only on plants.

**Can spider mites live in the soil?** They feed on leaves and stems, but the RHS notes that resting females can shelter over winter in cracks, canes, soil and plant debris. Clean pots, stakes and windowsills when you treat.

**Is misting enough to get rid of spider mites?** No. Higher humidity slows them down, but the RHS says it will not control them on its own.
