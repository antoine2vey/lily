Most ZZ plant problems come from too much water. The ZZ stores water in thick underground rhizomes, so it needs watering only 1 or 2 times a month, and only once the potting mix has dried completely. Yellow leaves, soft stems and drooping usually point back to wet soil. The rest of the list is short: direct sun, cold below 16°C (60°F), very low light, and a handful of sap-sucking pests.

## ZZ plant problems: start with the pot

Before you look up a symptom, check three things.

- **The mix.** Push a wooden skewer to the bottom of the pot and pull it out. Damp soil stuck to it means the plant does not need water, whatever the leaves look like.
- **The drainage.** Is there a hole in the bottom, and is the plant sitting in water in a saucer or a decorative pot? [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/zz-plant-zamioculcas-zamiifolia-indoor-care-growing-tips-plant-guide/) warns never to let a ZZ sit in water and to drain the pot fully before putting it back in its cover pot.
- **The spot.** Is it in direct sun, against a cold window, or in a corner far from any window?

## Why are my ZZ plant leaves turning yellow?

**1. Overwatering.** This is the cause in most cases. Several leaflets yellowing at once, often starting low on the stems, with a pot that stays damp for a week or more after watering, is the typical pattern. Clemson notes that overwatering leads to root rot, and NC State says the ZZ does not tolerate wet feet. Stop watering until the mix is dry all the way through. If stems are also soft at the base, go to the rot section below.

**2. Old leaves.** One old stem slowly yellowing while new shoots come up from the soil is normal. Cut it off at soil level.

**3. Thirst.** It is rare, but a ZZ left completely dry for months can yellow too. The leaflets look thin and wrinkled rather than soft, and the soil is bone dry and pulling away from the pot. Water thoroughly once and let it drain.

**4. A yellow variety.** Some cultivars, like 'Chameleon', put out yellow new leaves that turn dark green as they mature. If only the newest growth is yellow and it looks healthy, it may be the variety, not a problem.

To tell overwatering from underwatering, feel the stems as well as the soil. Firm stems and dry soil point to thirst. Soft stems and wet soil point to rot. Our [overwatering vs underwatering guide](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) covers the signs in more detail.

## Why is my ZZ plant drooping or falling over?

**1. Rot at the base.** A stem that flops over and feels soft or mushy where it meets the soil has rotted. That is the most serious ZZ problem and it starts with wet mix.

**2. Reaching for light.** Stems that lean in one direction, grow long and bend under their own weight are reaching for a window. Clemson says the ZZ survives low light, but bright, indirect light speeds up its very slow growth and keeps it compact. Move it closer to a window and turn the pot every couple of weeks. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot.

**3. A crowded pot.** A ZZ that has filled its pot with rhizomes can push stems outwards so they splay. Repotting into a pot slightly larger, or dividing the plant, solves it.

**4. Cold.** Clemson notes that the ZZ is sensitive to cold and should be kept above 16°C (60°F). A plant next to a cold window or door in winter can droop and darken.

## Why are my ZZ plant stems soft or mushy?

This is root and rhizome rot, and it needs action straight away.

1. Take the plant out of its pot and rinse or brush off the old mix.
2. Look at the rhizomes and roots. Healthy ones are firm and pale. Rotten ones are brown or black, soft and smell bad.
3. Cut away everything soft with a clean knife, back to firm tissue.
4. Let the cuts dry before repotting.
5. Repot into a clean pot with drainage holes and fresh, dry, fast-draining mix. Clemson suggests about 50% peat or coir, 25% perlite and 25% sand.
6. Water lightly, then let the mix dry out completely before the next watering.

If every rhizome is rotten, take healthy leaflets or a healthy stem and start new plants from them (see the end of this guide). Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) goes through the same steps for any houseplant.

## Why are my ZZ plant leaves wrinkled or shrivelled?

**1. A long dry spell.** The ZZ is very drought tolerant, but a plant forgotten for months in a warm room eventually uses up its stored water. Wrinkled leaflets on firm stems with dry soil mean thirst. Water deeply.

**2. Dead roots.** If the soil is wet and the leaflets still wrinkle, the roots have rotted and cannot take up water. Check the rhizomes as described above.

## Why does my ZZ plant have brown or scorched leaves?

**1. Direct sun.** NC State warns that scalding and browning of leaves can occur in direct sunlight. Clemson simply says to place a ZZ anywhere except full sun. Bleached or brown patches on the side facing the window point to sunburn. Move the plant a little back from the glass. Scorched tissue does not recover, but new growth will be fine.

**2. Cold damage.** Dark, soft patches after a cold night near a window or an open door point to cold. Move the plant to a spot that stays above 16°C (60°F).

**3. Overwatering.** Brown, soft leaflet tips with wet soil are another sign of rot.

## Are black spots on ZZ plant stems a problem?

Usually not. Clemson notes that black spots on the stems are normal on a healthy ZZ. They are a problem only if the stem underneath is soft or the spots are spreading and mushy, which points back to rot.

## Why is my ZZ plant not growing?

The ZZ grows slowly by nature, and it may not put out new shoots for months, especially in winter or in low light. A healthy ZZ in a dim corner can sit unchanged for a long time. Bright, indirect light, warmth and a light feed in spring and summer are what speed it up. Clemson suggests feeding once or twice per season.

## What pests attack ZZ plants?

The ZZ is not bothered by much, but it is not immune.

- **Scale.** NC State names scale insects as the pest to watch. They look like small brown bumps on the stems and leaflets that scrape off with a fingernail. Wipe them off with a cotton pad dipped in rubbing alcohol and recheck weekly.
- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony clumps in leaf joints. Remove them the same way.
- **Aphids, thrips and whiteflies.** [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2026-01-09-houseplant-spotlight-zz-plant) lists these alongside mealybugs. Rinse the plant thoroughly in the shower and repeat every week until they are gone.

Sticky leaves or a black sooty film usually mean sap-sucking insects are present. Our [guide to common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers identification and treatment.

## Is the ZZ plant toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. NC State lists it as having medium toxicity to cats and dogs. Like other aroids, it contains calcium oxalate crystals, which irritate the mouth and stomach if chewed. Keep it out of reach of pets that nibble plants, and wash your hands after handling cut stems.

## How to start over from a damaged ZZ

Illinois Extension describes two ways to propagate a ZZ, which also work as a rescue.

- **Division.** At repotting, separate a section of rhizome with at least one healthy stem and some roots, and pot it on its own.
- **Leaf cuttings.** Cut healthy leaflets with the small stalk intact, let them callus for a few days, then push them about 1 cm (½ in) into moist mix. Keep them warm and bright. Roots come first, then a small rhizome, over the following months.
