Mint care is mostly about containment and water: grow it in a pot at least 30 cm (12 in) across, in sun or part shade, and keep the compost moist. Pick the shoot tips often, cut the whole plant down to 5 cm (2 in) after it flowers, and divide it every few years. It is one of the easiest herbs to grow and one of the hardest to get rid of once it escapes into a border.

## Mint care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Full sun or part shade outdoors. Indoors, the sunniest window you have |
| Water | When the top 2 cm (1 in) is dry. Pots in summer may need it daily |
| Humidity | Ordinary outdoor or room air |
| Temperature | Hardy outdoors in most climates; dies back in winter and regrows in spring |
| Soil | Rich, moisture-holding multipurpose compost in a pot with drainage holes |
| Pot | At least 30 to 40 cm (12 to 16 in) across, repotted or divided every few years |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats and dogs in large amounts (vomiting, diarrhoea) |

Most culinary mints, such as spearmint and peppermint, are hardy perennials. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/herbs/mint/grow-your-own) describes mint as extremely easy to grow, happy in sun or partial shade and in a range of soils. The top growth dies back over winter and fresh shoots appear every spring from the underground stems.

## Should you grow mint in a pot?

Yes, nearly always. Mint spreads through rhizomes, underground stems that run sideways and send up new shoots well away from the original plant. In open ground it takes over a bed within a season or two. The RHS recommends growing it in a container for that reason.

[University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/mint) suggests a container about 30 to 40 cm (12 to 16 in) in diameter. If you want mint in a bed, use a bottomless pot or one with large drainage holes, sunk into the soil with about 2.5 cm (1 in) of rim showing. Runners then have to climb over the rim, where you can see them and cut them off before they root on the other side.

Keep different mints in separate pots. Planted together, the strongest variety tends to take over.

## How much sun does mint need?

Mint grows in sun or part shade. UMD notes that peppermint is probably most productive in full sun, but it still grows well in part shade. In very hot, dry summers, a spot with afternoon shade keeps it from wilting between waterings; the RHS suggests moving pots to a shadier spot in hot weather to cut down on watering.

Indoors, give mint your brightest window. Weak light gives long, floppy stems with small, widely spaced leaves and a milder flavour. A grow light a few centimetres above the plant helps in winter. Our [indoor herb garden guide](/en/blog/indoor-herb-garden) covers windows and grow lights for herbs.

## How often should you water mint?

Mint likes compost that stays moist. Water when the top 2 cm (1 in) feels dry, until water runs from the drainage holes. The RHS points out that plants in containers dry out much faster than those in the ground, especially in hot, dry weather, so check pots often in summer. A small pot in full sun can need water every day in a heatwave. In winter, when an outdoor plant has died back, the compost only needs to stay barely damp.

Our guide on [how often to water mint](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-mint) covers intervals by season, pot size and position.

## How to harvest mint so it keeps growing

Pick often. The RHS and UMD both note that regular harvesting keeps mint bushy and productive. Cut or pinch stems just above a pair of leaves, taking the top few centimetres. New shoots grow from the leaf joints below.

Mint flowers in summer or early autumn, with spikes of purple, pink or white flowers that bees visit and that are edible. Leaves for drying are best cut just as the flowers begin to open. Once flowering finishes, the RHS recommends cutting the whole plant down to about 5 cm (2 in) from the base. Fresh shoots with tender young leaves follow within a few weeks.

You can harvest from late spring to autumn. In late autumn, cut away the old faded stems, or leave them until early spring and clear them just before new shoots appear.

## Can you grow mint indoors in winter?

Yes. The RHS suggests bringing a potted plant indoors in autumn and putting it on a sunny windowsill to keep harvesting through winter. You can also take a few stems in autumn and root them in a jar of water on the sill. Growth is slower in winter light, so pick lightly.

## Does mint need feeding?

Mint in the ground rarely needs it. In a pot, the compost runs out of nutrients after a season. Feed potted mint with a balanced liquid fertiliser at the label rate every few weeks from spring to late summer. Never mix it stronger than the label says. Our guide to [fertilising houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) explains the numbers on the label.

## How to divide and propagate mint

Mint is easy to multiply.

- **Division.** Every few years, in spring or autumn, tip the plant out, cut the root ball into sections with a spade or knife, and replant one piece in fresh compost in the same pot. The RHS recommends this to stop plants getting cramped.
- **Cuttings in water.** Cut a 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) stem, strip the lower leaves and stand it in a jar of water. The RHS notes that stems in water soon produce roots. Pot it up once the roots are a few centimetres long.
- **Runners.** Any length of rhizome with a shoot will grow if potted.

Our guide to [propagating plants from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers the same method for other herbs.

## Common mint problems

- **Orange or rusty pustules under the leaves, distorted shoots in spring:** mint rust, a common fungal disease. Cut off and bin affected shoots as soon as you see them. Badly affected plants are best replaced with clean stock in a new spot.
- **Sticky leaves, small insects on shoot tips:** aphids. Rinse them off or pinch out the worst tips.
- **Long, pale stems with small leaves:** too little light.
- **Wilting in a hot spot:** the pot is drying out. Water more often, use a bigger pot, or move it to afternoon shade.
- **Thin, woody clump with few new shoots:** the pot is full of old rhizomes. Divide and repot.

Each of these has its causes and fixes in our article on [mint problems](/en/blog/mint-problems).

## Is mint safe for cats and dogs?

Not in large amounts. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/mint) lists garden mint as toxic to dogs, cats and horses, with vomiting and diarrhoea as the signs. A nibble is unlikely to cause trouble, but keep pots out of reach of a pet that chews plants. Pennyroyal, also a mint, carries a "harmful if eaten" warning in the RHS guide.
