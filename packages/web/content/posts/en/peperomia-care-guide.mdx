Peperomia care is simple once you accept one rule: most peperomias store water in thick leaves and stems, so they need far less water than they seem to. Give them bright indirect light, let the top half of the soil dry before watering, keep them in a warm room above 10 °C (50°F), and use a small pot with fast-draining mix. Overwatering is the cause of most dead peperomias, and normal indoor humidity is enough for the common types.

## Peperomia care at a glance

| Need | What it wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect. Tolerates lower light for months; no strong direct sun |
| Water | When the top half of the soil is dry. About every 1 to 2 weeks in summer, every 3 to 4 weeks in winter as a starting point |
| Humidity | Normal indoor humidity; medium to low is fine. A humidifier only if the air is very dry |
| Temperature | Warm room. Not below 10 to 15 °C (50 to 59°F), away from cold draughts |
| Soil | Fast-draining mix: 2 parts potting mix to 1 part perlite, in a pot with a hole |
| Repotting | Rarely. Every 2 to 3 years, one pot size up |
| Toxicity | Non-toxic to cats and dogs (Peperomia obtusifolia) |

The light, water and humidity guidance comes from NC State Extension's pages on the [peperomia genus](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/peperomia/) and on [Peperomia obtusifolia](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/peperomia-obtusifolia/), the baby rubber plant. The temperature range is the RHS H1b hardiness rating for the species: a plant for indoors that needs 10 to 15 °C at minimum.

## Which peperomia do you have?

Peperomia is a genus of over 1,000 species from the tropics and subtropics, in the same family as black pepper. They are not all equally easy. The ones sold most often are:

- **Baby rubber plant (P. obtusifolia):** upright, thick round leaves, plain green or variegated. The most forgiving.
- **Ripple peperomia (P. caperata):** small crinkled leaves on pink or red stalks. Thinner leaves than the baby rubber plant, so it dries out faster.
- **Watermelon peperomia (P. argyreia):** silver-striped round leaves on red stems.
- **Raindrop peperomia (P. polybotrya):** glossy, pointed, very fleshy leaves. Close to a succulent in its watering needs.
- **String of turtles (P. prostrata):** a tiny trailing plant with patterned coin-sized leaves. It needs a small pot and careful watering.

The care below fits all of them. Adjust the watering to the leaves: the thicker and more succulent the leaf, the longer the plant can go dry.

## How much light does a peperomia need?

Bright indirect light. NC State notes that P. obtusifolia tolerates low light for several months without stress, especially the plain green forms, but that sun that is too strong or direct burns the leaves. Variegated forms need the brighter end of the range to keep their colour.

A spot near an east or north window, or a metre (3 ft) or so back from a south or west window, works well. In low light, peperomias grow slowly, stretch toward the window and stay wet longer after watering, which raises the risk of rot. ## How often should you water a peperomia?

Water when the top half of the pot is dry, then water until it drains and empty the saucer. NC State describes the obtusifolia as preferring dry soil but intolerant of both wet soil and very dry soil, and the genus page warns plainly against overwatering. Very thick-leaved types can wait until the mix is almost dry all the way down.

As a starting point, that means every 1 to 2 weeks in a bright room in summer and every 3 to 4 weeks in winter, when growth slows. The pot, the room and the species change that a lot. A small terracotta pot by a sunny window may dry in five days; a plastic pot in a dim room may stay damp for three weeks.

You can read a peperomia's leaves. Slightly soft, thin or wrinkled leaves that firm up after watering mean it was thirsty. Yellow, drooping leaves on damp soil, soft black stems at the base, or leaves that fall at a touch mean the roots are too wet. ## What soil does a peperomia need?

A light, fast-draining mix. NC State suggests a moist but well-drained mix of loam and sand. A standard houseplant potting mix with about a third perlite does the same job. For raindrop peperomias and other very fleshy types, a mix with half perlite or a cactus mix is safer.

Use a pot with a drainage hole and keep it small. Peperomias have small root systems, and NC State notes they are slow-growing and do not need repotting often. A pot that is too big holds wet soil around the roots for days. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains how to adjust a mix.

## Does a peperomia need high humidity?

No. Old care guides often say peperomias need high humidity and regular misting. NC State's genus page says they are adaptable to the humidity of most homes, and its obtusifolia page lists medium to low relative humidity as the preference. The Missouri Botanical Garden suggests a humid room in summer for P. obtusifolia, but that is a bonus, not a requirement. One of the plant's common names, radiator plant, refers to its liking for warm spots.

Skip misting. It does little for humidity, and NC State recommends avoiding wet leaves to prevent leaf spots. If the air in your home is very dry in winter and the thin-leaved types brown at the edges, a humidifier is the fix.

## How do you propagate a peperomia?

Peperomias are among the easiest plants to propagate. NC State lists leaf cuttings, stem cuttings and division, depending on the species.

1. **Stem cuttings:** cut a stem with 2 or 3 leaves, remove the lowest leaf, and root it in water or straight in damp perlite or potting mix.
2. **Leaf cuttings:** for obtusifolia, argyreia and caperata, a single leaf with a short stalk will root in damp mix. Watermelon peperomia leaves can also be cut in half across the middle, with the cut edge pressed into the mix.
3. **Division:** split clumping plants when you repot in spring.

Keep cuttings warm and in bright indirect light. Roots usually appear within a few weeks. Our guide to [plant propagation from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers aftercare.

## Should you fertilise a peperomia?

Very little. A balanced liquid houseplant fertiliser at half the label strength once a month in spring and summer is plenty. Peperomias are slow growers, and excess fertiliser builds up as salt in a small pot.

## What pests and problems affect peperomias?

NC State lists mealybugs, spider mites, aphids, scale, fungus gnats and whiteflies. Mealybugs, which look like tufts of white cotton in leaf joints, and fungus gnats, which breed in damp soil, are the two you are most likely to see. Both point back to the same habit: soil that stays wet. Root rot and yellow or curled leaves are, according to NC State, signs of overwatering. ## Is a peperomia safe for cats and dogs?

The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/baby-rubber-plant) lists the baby rubber plant, Peperomia obtusifolia, as non-toxic to dogs and cats. Peperomias are generally considered pet-safe, but not every species has its own entry. Our list of [plants safe for cats](/en/blog/plants-safe-for-cats) has more options.
