Most dracaena problems come from three things: soil kept wet for too long, fluoride and salts in tap water, and sudden cold. Brown tips are the most common complaint and are almost never a disease. Yellow lower leaves are often just old leaves being shed as the cane grows taller. Find your symptom below, rule out the causes in the order they are listed, and fix the one that fits.

## Dracaena problems at a glance

| Symptom | Most likely cause | First thing to check |
|---|---|---|
| Brown, dry leaf tips | Fluoride or salt build-up, dry air | Your water source and any white crust on the soil |
| Yellow lower leaves | Normal ageing, or overwatering | Whether only the oldest leaves are affected |
| Yellow or brown edges with dead patches | Fluoride, excess fertiliser | The potting mix (lots of perlite?) and the fertiliser label |
| Drooping, soft leaves | Root rot or a very dry pot | Weight of the pot and smell of the soil |
| Many leaves dropping at once | Cold, a draught, a sudden move | Where the plant was in the last week |
| Pale, dry patches | Direct sun | Which side of the plant faces the window |
| Narrow, pale new leaves, bare cane | Too little light | Distance from the nearest window |
| Speckles, webbing, sticky leaves | Spider mites, scale, mealybugs, thrips | Undersides of leaves with a torch |

Most of the figures here come from two extension fact sheets: [Clemson University's dracaena page](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/dracaena/) and [NC State Extension's Dracaena fragrans profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/dracaena-fragrans/).

## Why does my dracaena have brown tips?

Brown, crisp tips on long dracaena leaves have four common causes. In order of frequency:

1. **Fluoride and salts in the water.** Clemson states plainly that "Dracaena is very sensitive to fluoride." NC State adds that it is sensitive to built-up salts. Fluoride damage starts as yellowing at the tips and edges, then turns into dead, scorched areas with a clear line between brown and green. It shows up on many leaves at once, including newer ones.
2. **Dry air.** Clemson names low humidity as the usual cause of dry tips and edges. This damage is narrower, a few millimetres of papery brown at the very tip, and gets worse in winter when the heating is on.
3. **Irregular watering.** Letting the pot dry out completely, then flooding it, kills root tips, and the leaf tips follow.
4. **Too much fertiliser.** Clemson notes that excess fertiliser burns or yellows leaf tips and margins. You often see a white crust on the soil surface or the rim of the pot.

**How to tell them apart.** Broad tip and edge burn with yellow margins on several leaves points to fluoride or fertiliser. Thin brown tips that appear in winter point to dry air. A crust on the soil confirms salts.

**The fix.**

- Water with rainwater or filtered water if your tap water is fluoridated, as NC State recommends.
- Flush the pot: run plain water through it for a minute or two, let it drain fully, and repeat once a month during the growing season.
- When you repot, avoid mixes with a high share of perlite and fertilisers containing superphosphate. Clemson flags both as fluoride sources and recommends a soil pH between 6.0 and 6.5.
- Feed only in spring and summer, at half the label rate or less.
- Run a humidifier in winter if the tips brown only then.

Brown tissue never turns green again. Trim it with clean scissors, following the natural point of the leaf and leaving a hair of brown so you don't cut into live tissue.

## Why are my dracaena leaves turning yellow?

1. **Normal ageing.** A dracaena grows from the top of its cane and sheds its oldest leaves at the bottom. One or two lower leaves yellowing slowly, while the top stays green and produces new leaves, is normal. Pull them off once they come away easily.
2. **Overwatering.** Several leaves yellowing together, starting low, with soil that stays damp for more than a week. This is the most common real problem. Clemson advises letting the plant dry slightly between waterings and then watering thoroughly once the surface is dry to the touch.
3. **Fluoride or fertiliser.** Yellow starts at the tips and edges rather than across the whole leaf. See brown tips above.
4. **Too little light.** Pale overall colour, slow growth and new leaves narrower than the old ones. NC State notes that too little light produces narrow leaves.

**How to tell them apart.** Push a finger 5 cm (2 in) into the soil. If it is wet and the yellowing is spreading, it is water. If the soil is fine and only the bottom leaves yellow one at a time, it is age. Yellow tips with green centres point to water quality.

**The fix.** For overwatering, stop watering until the top third of the pot is dry, check that water drains out of the hole, and empty the saucer after every watering. For yellowing on many different plants, the general [yellow leaves guide](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) covers the other causes.

## Why is my dracaena drooping?

A dracaena's leaves are stiff, so drooping is a stronger signal than on most plants.

1. **Root rot.** Leaves droop and yellow while the soil is still wet. The base of the cane may feel soft, and the soil can smell sour. Clemson names a poorly draining mix or watering too often as the usual cause.
2. **Severe underwatering.** Leaves droop and curl, lower leaves go brown and dry, and the pot feels very light. This is rarer, because a dracaena stores some water in its cane.
3. **Cold.** Leaves go limp after a night near cold glass or a door left open. NC State recommends keeping the plant above 10°C (50°F), and Clemson recommends 15 to 21°C (60 to 70°F) by day.

**How to tell them apart.** Lift the pot. Light and dry means thirst: water slowly until it drains and the leaves should firm up within a day or two. Heavy and wet means rot: unpot it and look.

## How do you save a dracaena with root rot?

Slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and pale orange or tan. Rotten roots are brown or black, mushy, and the outer layer slips off when you pull. Cut away every soft root with clean scissors, shake off the old soil, and repot into fresh, fast-draining mix in a pot just large enough for the remaining roots. Water once, then wait until the top third is dry before watering again.

If the cane itself is soft at the base, the roots cannot be saved. Cut the cane above the soft section, into firm tissue, and root the top in water or moist mix. Dracaenas root readily from cane sections. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) walks through each step in detail.

## Why is my dracaena dropping leaves?

Clemson lists four causes of sudden leaf loss: heavy insect infestations, draughts, poor drainage and too much water. Sudden temperature changes can also make many leaves drop at once. A plant that has just been moved, bought, or set near an air-conditioning vent or a radiator often sheds a handful of leaves and then settles. Keep it in one spot, away from draughts, and do not repot or fertilise it until new leaves appear at the top.

## Why does my dracaena have spots or pale patches?

- **Dry, pale or tan patches and streaks on the side facing the window** are sunburn. Clemson describes round dry patches and streaks from too much sun. Move the plant out of direct sun; damaged leaves keep their marks.
- **Dull, dark or water-soaked areas** after a cold night are chill damage. Move the plant away from glass.
- **Yellow speckling and fine webbing** under the leaves mean spider mites. See pests below.

## Why is my dracaena leggy, with bare canes?

Losing lower leaves as it grows is how a dracaena grows, so a bare cane with a tuft of leaves on top is its natural shape. If the new leaves are also pale and narrow, it needs more light. Move it to bright, indirect light: Clemson notes that a plant moved from dim light to a brighter spot grows thicker, stronger leaves.

To shorten a cane that has grown too tall, cut it to the height you want. Clemson notes that new leaves will appear below the cut. The top you removed can be rooted as a new plant.

## What pests attack dracaena?

Clemson lists scale and mites as the most common pests on dracaena. NC State adds mealybugs and thrips.

- **Spider mites:** fine yellow speckling, dull leaves, webbing in leaf joints. Common in dry winter air. See [spider mite treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment).
- **Scale:** small brown bumps along leaf midribs and canes, with sticky honeydew below them.
- **Mealybugs:** white cottony tufts where leaves join the cane.
- **Thrips:** silvery streaks and black specks on the leaves.

Isolate the plant, wipe or rinse off what you can, and treat with insecticidal soap or horticultural oil, repeating as the label says. Our overview of [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers identification and treatment for each one.

## How to keep a dracaena healthy

Most problems on this list come back to water. Water when the top quarter of the pot is dry, use rainwater or filtered water if your tap water is fluoridated, and give the plant less water in winter. [Lily](/en) keeps a watering schedule for each plant and stretches it as indoor daylight drops, which is when dracaenas are most often overwatered.

## Is dracaena toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/dracaena) lists *Dracaena* species as toxic to dogs and cats. The toxic compounds are saponins. Signs include vomiting, sometimes with blood, drooling, loss of appetite and depression, and dilated pupils in cats.
