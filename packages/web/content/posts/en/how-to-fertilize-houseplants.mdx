How to fertilize houseplants comes down to three rules: feed only while the plant is actively growing, use a balanced fertiliser, and never mix it stronger than the label says. Most houseplants need far less feeding than people give them. Too much fertiliser does more damage than too little, and no amount of it makes up for a dark corner.

## When to fertilize houseplants

Plants only use extra nutrients when they are making new leaves and roots. [University of New Hampshire Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2018/03/fertilizing-houseplants) puts it simply: most houseplants rest in winter, so put the fertiliser away from October until spring, and start again in March.

[Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-cleaning-fertilizing-containers-light-requirements/) gives the same rule from the other side. Growth, and so feeding, happens mostly in spring and summer, when light is stronger and days are longer. During the short days of winter, many plants that get little or no artificial light go into a resting stage, and a resting plant should get no fertiliser at all.

The months are a guide, not a rule. Watch the plant instead:

- **Feed** when you see new leaves unfurling, new shoots or roots showing at the drainage holes.
- **Stop** when growth stops, usually as the days shorten in autumn.
- **Plants under grow lights** that keep growing through winter can keep getting a light feed.

Our guide to [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers the other changes that come with the resting season.

## How often to feed

Clemson offers three ways to feed during the growing season. Pick the one that fits your routine.

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| Full strength, rarely | The label rate every 2 to 3 months |
| Very dilute, every time | About one-tenth of the label rate at every watering |
| Regular, in between | The label rate every seventh watering |

UNH says most houseplants benefit from a feed every 1 to 3 months, depending on the product. Slow-release products last longer between applications than liquids, so follow the interval on the pack.

Clemson also notes that the right frequency varies with how vigorous and how old the plant is, and that fast new growth is not always a good thing: a plant pushed hard can outgrow its spot. A big, slow-growing plant in a dim room needs much less than a young pothos on a sunny sill.

## Which fertiliser to choose

Every fertiliser label carries three numbers, the N-P-K ratio. They are the percentages by weight of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K).

- **Foliage plants.** Clemson suggests a balanced, complete fertiliser such as 20-20-20. UNH also recommends a balanced ratio for houseplants.
- **Flowering plants.** Clemson suggests one higher in phosphorus, such as 15-30-15, for African violets and other plants grown for their flowers.

Fertilisers come in several forms:

- **Liquids and water-soluble powders or granules.** UNH notes that the nutrients are available to the plant straight away. Clemson says water-soluble products are often preferred because dilute solutions reduce the risk of fertiliser burn.
- **Slow-release granules, sticks and tablets.** These break down gradually over weeks or months. They are convenient, but you cannot stop them once they are in the pot, so use them in spring rather than late summer.

Whichever you choose, UNH's rule applies: follow the directions, and never use a stronger mix than the label recommends.

## How to fertilize houseplants step by step

1. **Check that the plant is growing.** No new growth, no feed.
2. **Water first if the compost is bone dry.** Fertiliser solution poured onto dry roots is more likely to scorch them. Water, let the pot drain, then feed at the next watering or an hour later.
3. **Measure, do not guess.** Mix at the label rate, or weaker. Clemson notes that plants in low light need less, so half strength suits plants away from a window.
4. **Water it in thoroughly.** Pour until a little runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer.
5. **Write down the date.** It is easy to forget whether the last feed was three weeks or three months ago.

Skip feeding for a while after repotting. Many potting composts contain some fertiliser already, and roots disturbed by repotting are probably easier to burn. ## Signs of too much fertiliser

Fertilisers are full of soluble salts, and salts build up in the pot with every feed. UNH notes that too much salt makes it hard for the plant to take up water and can burn root tips and leaf margins. Clemson lists the symptoms of salt build-up:

- A white film on the compost, or a white crust on the rim or around the drainage hole.
- Brown leaf tips.
- Lower leaves dropping.
- Slow growth.
- Wilting, even when the compost is moist.

Clemson adds that a white crust can also point to overwatering, so check both. Overfeeding can also make a plant shoot up and outgrow its pot, or, in severe cases, stunt it and kill it.

## How to flush salts from the pot

UNH recommends leaching pots every four months or so. Take the plant to the sink or bath and pour plenty of fresh water through the compost until it drains freely from the bottom. For a plant too big to move, pour a large amount of water through it where it stands and empty the saucer straight away.

Clemson notes that the best way to prevent salt injury is to stop salts building up in the first place: water thoroughly each time, let the excess drain away, and empty the tray.

## Signs a plant needs feeding

UNH says a plant that is stunted, or whose leaves show yellow or purple tints, probably needs a balanced fertiliser. Yellow leaves have many other causes, though, from overwatering to low light; our guide to [plant leaves turning yellow](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) helps you rule those out first.

Fertiliser cannot make up for poor light. Clemson notes that indoor plants in low light need less fertiliser, and that foliage plants need at least 200 foot-candles (about 2,150 lux) for 12 hours a day before feeding shows any benefit. A plant far from a window will not grow faster with more fertiliser; it will only collect more salt.
