Alocasia care asks for more precision than most houseplants: bright indirect light, water once the top 2.5 cm (1 in) of soil is dry, humidity above 50% and a room that stays above 16°C (60°F). Get those four right and an alocasia produces a new leaf every few weeks through spring and summer. Get one wrong, usually the water or the cold, and it drops leaves fast.

## Alocasia care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light. No direct midday sun |
| Water | When the top 2.5 cm (1 in) is dry in spring and summer. Less in winter |
| Humidity | Above 50%. A humidifier is the reliable way to get there |
| Temperature | 20 to 25°C (68 to 77°F). Never below 16°C (60°F) |
| Soil | Chunky, airy aroid mix with bark and perlite, slightly acidic |
| Feeding | Diluted liquid fertiliser every 2 to 4 weeks, spring and summer |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats, dogs, horses and children (calcium oxalate crystals) |

The figures follow NC State Extension's profiles for [Alocasia](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/alocasia/) and [Alocasia × amazonica](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/alocasia-x-amazonica/), the arrow-leaved hybrid most often sold as a houseplant. Alocasias grow from a thick underground stem, a rhizome or corm, in the humid tropics of Southeast Asia and eastern Australia. That underground storage organ explains most of their behaviour indoors, including the habit of dying back in winter.

## How much light does an alocasia need?

Bright, indirect light: close to an east-facing window, or a metre or two (3 to 6 ft) back from a south- or west-facing one. NC State's Amazonica profile says the plant prefers bright, indirect sunlight and that direct sun can scorch its leaves. Gentle morning sun is fine.

Too little light shows as long, weak leaf stalks that lean toward the window, smaller new leaves and slow growth. Too much direct sun leaves pale or brown, papery patches. An alocasia's leaves turn to face the light, so rotate the pot a quarter turn every week or two to keep the plant upright and even.

## How often should you water an alocasia?

In spring and summer, water when the top 2.5 cm (1 in) of soil is dry. Water slowly until it runs from the drainage hole, then empty the saucer. NC State advises regular watering and fertilising, reduced in winter, and warns that overwatering causes root rot.

That works out to roughly once a week in a warm, bright room in summer, and every two to three weeks in winter. The interval depends on pot size, light and temperature, so always check the soil before you water. The soil should feel like a wrung-out sponge, never soggy and never bone dry.

Alocasias react quickly to both mistakes:

- **Too much water:** yellowing on the oldest leaves, soft stems at the soil line, a sour smell from the pot. NC State also lists leaf spots as a sign.
- **Too little water:** leaves droop, then edges go brown and crisp.

When in doubt, wait a day. A slightly thirsty alocasia recovers in hours. One with rotting roots may not recover at all. [Lily](/en) schedules watering for each plant and lengthens the interval as daylight shortens, which is when alocasias are most often overwatered.

## Humidity and temperature

Humidity is where most alocasias struggle in a home. NC State lists the Amazonica as preferring high humidity. Below about 40%, typical of a heated room in winter, leaf edges brown and spider mites move in.

A humidifier near the plant is the most effective fix. Grouping plants together helps a little. Misting does not: the water evaporates within minutes and wet leaves can encourage leaf spots. Our guide to [increasing humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the methods that work.

Keep the plant between 20 and 25°C (68 to 77°F), which NC State gives as its preferred daytime range. The Amazonica profile sets 16°C (60°F) as the floor. Below that, growth stops and the plant may go dormant. Keep it away from cold windows, external doors and radiators.

## Best soil and pot for an alocasia

Alocasia roots need both moisture and air. Use a chunky aroid mix: roughly equal parts indoor potting mix, orchid bark and perlite. NC State describes the ideal soil as humus-rich, moist, well-drained and slightly acidic. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains how to mix it.

Use a pot with drainage holes, only slightly larger than the root ball. Alocasias tolerate being a bit pot-bound, and an oversized pot holds water the roots cannot reach. Plastic or glazed pots keep moisture more evenly than terracotta, which can dry the mix too fast in summer.

## Repotting and spring care

Spring is the moment to repot, when the first new leaf appears. Repot every 12 to 18 months, or when roots fill the pot or come out of the drainage hole. Move up only 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) in diameter, keep the corm at the same depth, and water lightly for the first week. The [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) covers the steps.

Repotting is also the best time to divide. NC State lists division of offsets as the way to propagate alocasia. Small bulb-like corms and offsets form around the main corm. Separate any that already have their own roots and pot them in small pots of the same mix.

Spring is also when to restart feeding: a diluted balanced liquid fertiliser every 2 to 4 weeks until early autumn.

## Alocasia care through the seasons

Alocasias are seasonal plants. Expect each season to look different.

- **Spring:** growth restarts as days lengthen. Repot or divide, move the plant into its brightest indirect spot and resume feeding.
- **Summer:** the main growing season. Water when the top 2.5 cm (1 in) is dry, feed every 2 to 4 weeks and watch the leaf undersides for spider mites.
- **Autumn:** growth slows. Stop feeding by early autumn and water less often.
- **Winter:** keep it warm, bright and on the dry side. Water only when the top half of the pot is dry.

## Why is my alocasia losing leaves in winter?

Many alocasias drop most or all of their leaves in winter when light falls and temperatures drop. This is dormancy, not death. The [University of Vermont Extension](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/houseplant-care-during-winter-months) notes that alocasias may lose leaves in winter and that this is normal.

To check, gently press the base of the stems or dig a little at the soil line. A firm corm means the plant is alive. A mushy, smelly one means rot.

During dormancy, water very lightly, just enough that the soil does not dry out completely, and stop fertilising. Keep the pot warm. When new growth appears in spring, return to normal care. Our guide to [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers the same principles for the rest of your plants.

## Common alocasia problems

- **One old leaf yellowing while a new one opens:** normal. An alocasia often drops its oldest leaf as a new one unfurls.
- **Several leaves yellowing:** NC State lists overwatering, underwatering and low light.
- **Brown, crispy edges:** dry air or underwatering.
- **Fine speckling and webbing:** spider mites, which NC State says alocasia is prone to. Check the undersides weekly in winter.
- **Drooping:** check the soil first. Wet soil points to rot, dry soil points to thirst.

Each of these, with its causes and fixes, is covered in our article on [alocasia problems](/en/blog/alocasia-problems).

## Is alocasia toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. NC State lists alocasia as a medium-severity poison for cats, dogs, horses and children. All parts contain calcium oxalate crystals. Chewing them causes pain in the mouth, throat and tongue, drooling and vomiting, and the sap can irritate skin and eyes. Wear gloves when you divide or cut it, and keep it out of reach of pets.
