Root rot recovery is possible when the plant still has some firm, healthy roots: cut out every soft, brown root, repot into fresh potting mix in a clean pot with drainage holes, and water less from then on. When the rot has reached the base of the stems, take cuttings from healthy growth instead. Root rot is caused by soil-borne fungi and fungus-like water moulds that, as the [University of Wisconsin's Plant Disease Diagnostics Clinic](https://pddc.wisc.edu/2021/05/29/root-rots-on-houseplants-2/) puts it, prefer wet soil, so the fix always includes drier roots.

## How do you know a plant has root rot?

The clearest sign is a plant that wilts even though the soil is wet. The Wisconsin clinic lists stunted growth and yellow or reddish leaves as other symptoms, which can look like a nutrient deficiency. [University of Maryland Extension](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/root-rots-indoor-plants) adds yellowing, browning and dieback of leaves, poor growth, and browning or blackening of the crown and roots.

The roots confirm it. Slide the plant out of its pot:

- **Healthy roots** are firm and usually white, cream or light tan, depending on the plant.
- **Rotted roots** are soft and brown to black, and they may smell bad.

Maryland Extension describes what the most common culprits look like:

- **Pythium root rot.** Roots are brown to black and mushy. The outer layer pulls away from the core and leaves a fine, hair-like thread. When you lift the plant, much of the root system often stays behind in the pot.
- **Phytophthora root rot.** Root tips turn dark brown and quickly go soft. It can also blacken the base of the stems, so the lower leaves yellow and drop.
- **Rhizoctonia root rot.** It favours warm, moist conditions and forms coarse, reddish-brown threads like spider webs over infected stems and roots.

You do not need to know which one it is. The treatment is the same.

## What causes root rot?

The Wisconsin clinic names *Pythium*, *Phytophthora*, *Rhizoctonia solani* and *Fusarium* as the most common causes. They attack many different plants, and some survive a long time in soil and plant debris. What turns a harmless presence into rot is wet soil, kept wet for too long. The usual reasons:

- Watering on a fixed schedule rather than when the soil is dry, especially in winter
- A pot without drainage holes, or a pot left standing in its drainage water
- A pot much larger than the root ball, so most of the mix stays wet
- A dense, old potting mix that no longer drains
- A layer of gravel or stones at the bottom of the pot. The Wisconsin clinic notes this actually hinders drainage.
- Garden soil used as potting mix, which often carries root rot organisms

## Can the plant be saved?

Be realistic. The Wisconsin clinic says that throwing the plant out is often the best and most cost-effective option. For a plant you want to keep, look at what is left once the rotted roots are cut off:

- **Firm roots remain and the stem base is firm.** Repot it using the steps below. It has a fair chance.
- **Almost no roots remain, or the crown and stem base are soft.** The roots cannot recover. Take cuttings from healthy stems instead. Maryland Extension advises taking cuttings only from healthy tissue. Our guide to [propagating from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) explains how.

## Root rot recovery, step by step

1. **Isolate the plant.** Maryland Extension advises separating infected plants from healthy ones.
2. **Take it out of the pot and remove the old mix.** Loosen it gently, and rinse the roots with room-temperature water if you cannot see them clearly.
3. **Cut off every rotted root.** Use clean, sharp scissors or pruners and cut back to firm tissue. Wipe the blades with alcohol between cuts and when you finish. The Wisconsin clinic recommends disinfecting tools after working on rotted plants.
4. **Throw away the old mix.** The Wisconsin clinic advises against reusing potting mix from affected plants, or the water that drained from them, as both can carry the organisms.
5. **Use a clean pot.** The Wisconsin clinic recommends throwing away plastic pots from plants that had root rot, because they are hard to disinfect. Clay pots can be cleaned with a 10% bleach solution, a detergent solution or alcohol. Choose a pot with drainage holes, only slightly larger than the remaining roots, and with no gravel layer. Our guide to [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) covers sizing.
6. **Repot in fresh mix.** Use a pasteurised commercial potting mix, never garden soil. For heavy mixes, the Wisconsin clinic suggests adding organic material to improve drainage. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) suggests mixes by plant type.
7. **Water lightly, then wait.** Settle the mix with a little water. After that, water only when the top of the mix has dried. The Wisconsin clinic's rule is to give enough water to prevent drought stress, and no more.

## Aftercare while the roots regrow

- **Bright, indirect light.** Keep the plant out of direct sun while it has few roots to supply its leaves.
- **No fertiliser for now.** A reduced root system cannot use much. Wait until you see new growth.
- **Expect some leaf loss.** A plant with fewer roots may shed a few more leaves before it stabilises. That alone is not a sign of failure.
- **Watch for the warning sign.** If the plant wilts again while the mix is wet, the rot is still active. Check the roots again, or switch to cuttings.

Recovery takes weeks, not days. The first sign that it is working is firm new growth.

## Should you use fungicide, hydrogen peroxide or cinnamon?

The Wisconsin clinic does not recommend chemical fungicides for root rot on houseplants, because few products are available to home growers and those that are tend to be expensive. Hydrogen peroxide drenches and cinnamon are popular online. Neither source cited here recommends them, and neither replaces the steps above. What stops root rot is removing the rotted tissue and keeping the roots from staying wet.

## How to prevent root rot

The Wisconsin clinic and Maryland Extension give the same core advice:

- Buy plants from a reputable source, and check the roots of new plants before putting them with your others.
- Use pots with drainage holes and no gravel layer.
- Use pasteurised commercial potting mix, never garden soil, and do not reuse old mix.
- Do not let pots sit in their drainage water. Empty saucers and cachepots after watering.
- Water to meet the plant's needs and no more, so check the soil before each watering and water less in winter. Our guide on [how often to water houseplants](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-houseplants) explains how to judge it.
