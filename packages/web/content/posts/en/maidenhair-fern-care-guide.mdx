Maidenhair fern care comes down to never letting the soil dry out and keeping the air humid. Give it bright light without direct sun, such as a north window, humidity around 50%, and 16 to 21 °C (60 to 70 °F) by day with 10 to 16 °C (50 to 60 °F) at night. One missed watering can crisp a whole flush of fronds, but a fern with healthy roots usually sends up new ones.

## Maidenhair fern care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect. A north window, or well back from an east window. No direct sun from south or west windows. |
| Water | Keep the soil moist at all times. Never let it dry out, never let it stand in water. |
| Humidity | About 50% or more. Bathrooms, terrariums, pebble trays or a humidifier. |
| Temperature | 16 to 21 °C (60 to 70 °F) by day, 10 to 16 °C (50 to 60 °F) at night. |
| Soil | Equal parts potting mix and peat moss or leaf mould, in a pot with drainage. |
| Pets | Not on the ASPCA toxic plant list, and NC State gives no toxicity warning. Keep it out of reach anyway. |

The plant usually sold as maidenhair fern is *Adiantum raddianum*, the delta maidenhair. The [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/adiantum-raddianum/) entry describes it as native to tropical America and the West Indies, 30 to 60 cm (1 to 2 ft) high and wide, spreading by creeping rhizomes, and tags it as high maintenance. The genus name comes from the Greek for "unwetted", after the water-repellent fronds.

## How much light does a maidenhair fern need?

Bright light, but no direct sun. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-ferns/) puts maidenhair ferns in the light from a north window, and warns against direct sun in south or west windows. NC State says the fern needs partial shade to shade, but loses vitality in too much shade or in full sun, and lists sunburn among its problems.

In practice:

- **North window.** The natural spot. Set it close to the glass.
- **East window.** Fine a little back from the glass, where it gets only soft early sun.
- **South or west window.** Too strong on the sill. Set the fern well back or behind a sheer curtain.
- **Dim room.** Growth slows, fronds thin out, and the plant weakens over time.

Scorch shows as pale, bleached or crisp patches on the fronds facing the window. Fronds that thin out and lean toward the light mean the spot is too dark.

## How to water a maidenhair fern

This is the part that decides whether a maidenhair lives. Clemson says to keep the soil moist at all times, and NC State says the roots should not be allowed to dry out. Unlike most houseplants, it should not wait for the top of the soil to dry.

- **Check every two or three days**, more in summer or in a heated room. Water as soon as the surface starts to feel less than moist.
- **Water thoroughly** until it runs from the drainage hole, then empty the saucer. Clemson links root rot to poor drainage and overwatering, and advises never leaving the pot in standing water. Moist is not the same as waterlogged.
- **Use room-temperature water**, not cold.
- **Small pots dry fast.** A maidenhair in a small pot on a warm shelf can dry out within a day or two in summer.

If it does dry out and the fronds shrivel, do not throw it away. Cut the dead fronds back to the soil, keep the soil moist and the air humid, and wait. New fronds may come from the rhizome within a few weeks if the roots survived.

## Humidity

Clemson says maidenhair ferns need high humidity, about 50%, and that ferns grown in low humidity get brown leaves, especially the thin-fronded maidenhairs. Clemson also calls maidenhairs difficult in most homes, mostly for this reason. A heated room in winter often falls well below that.

Ways to raise it:

- **Bathroom or kitchen.** NC State notes it does well in bathrooms, where the air is more humid, as long as the light is good.
- **Double-potting.** Clemson suggests setting the pot inside a larger one and filling the gap with moist sphagnum moss.
- **Pebble tray.** Set the pot on wet pebbles with its base above the water line.
- **Humidifier.** The most reliable option in a dry room.
- **Terrarium.** NC State says it does well in terrariums. The [terrarium guide](/en/blog/how-to-care-for-a-terrarium) covers setup.

The [humidity guide](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares these methods.

## Temperature

Clemson gives 16 to 21 °C (60 to 70 °F) by day and 10 to 16 °C (50 to 60 °F) at night. A slightly cool room suits it better than a warm one. Keep it away from radiators, heating vents and hot, dry air, which dries both the soil and the fronds. The [winter care guide](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers heated rooms.

## Soil and repotting

Clemson recommends a mix of equal parts potting mix and peat moss or leaf mould for ferns. That holds moisture without turning dense. Always use a pot with a drainage hole.

NC State lists division as the recommended way to propagate. Clemson advises dividing ferns in early spring. Slide the plant out, cut the rhizome mass into two or three sections with a clean knife, each with roots and fronds, and pot them in fresh mix. Repot when roots fill the pot, moving to a pot only slightly larger.

## Fertiliser

Ferns need little feeding. Clemson recommends fertilising lightly once a month from April to September, not at all in winter, and not for six months after repotting. Too much fertiliser scorches fronds. Clemson suggests liquid houseplant fertiliser at about half the label rate.

## Brown, crispy fronds

The commonest maidenhair problem, in order of likelihood:

1. **Soil dried out.** Whole fronds crisp within a day or two. Check how often you water and whether the pot is too small.
2. **Dry air.** Tips and edges brown gradually, often in a heated room. Raise humidity.
3. **Direct sun.** Bleached or crisp patches on the side facing the window. Move it back.
4. **Too much fertiliser.** Scorched edges after feeding. Flush the soil and stop feeding for a few months.

Cut brown fronds off at the base. They will not recover, and removing them makes room for new growth. Yellow fronds with wet soil and a sour smell point to root rot from poor drainage. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers the fix.

## Pests

Clemson lists scale and mites as the main insect pests of indoor ferns. NC State also mentions mealybugs and soft scale. Pick off scale and mealybugs by hand or with a cotton swab. Clemson warns that pesticide sprays injure many ferns, so test any spray on a single frond first, or remove badly infested fronds instead.

[Lily](/en) schedules watering and fertilising per plant, which helps with a fern that cannot miss a watering and only wants feeding from spring to late summer.

## Quick answers

**Why is my maidenhair fern dying back in winter?** Usually dry air from heating, or soil drying out faster than you expect. NC State tags it as deciduous, so cut dead fronds back and keep the soil moist. New fronds may follow in spring.

**Is it easier than a Boston fern?** No. Clemson calls maidenhairs difficult in most homes. The [Boston fern care guide](/en/blog/boston-fern-care-guide) covers a more forgiving fern.
