Most pothos problems come from watering before the soil has dried. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/epipremnum-aureum/) notes that overwatering causes root rot and blackened leaf edges, and advises letting the potting mix dry out between waterings. The other common issues are too little light (small leaves, long bare stems, fading variegation), dry soil left too long (crisp brown edges) and two pests, mealybugs and scale. Find your symptom below and check the causes in the order given.

## Pothos problems: check the soil first

Before you treat any symptom, feel the soil 5 cm (2 in) down and lift the pot. Wet soil a week after watering points to the most common cause, too much water. Dry soil and a light pot point the other way.

## Why are my pothos leaves turning yellow?

Yellow leaves are the most searched pothos problem, and they have four common causes. In order of how often they turn up:

**1. Overwatering.** Several leaves yellow at once, often starting low on the plant, and they feel soft. The soil is still damp days after watering, and the plant may smell sour. Let the top half of the soil dry before you water again, and check that the pot has a drainage hole and that no water sits in the saucer or cachepot.

**2. Old leaves dropping.** One or two of the oldest leaves, near the base of the stems, turn yellow and fall while new leaves keep coming at the tips. NC State describes this as normal: over time the leaves concentrate at the ends of the stems. If the bare stems bother you, prune them (see below).

**3. Underwatering.** Leaves yellow and then brown and crisp, the soil has pulled away from the pot, and the whole plant droops. A thorough soak fixes it, but leaves that have already turned fully yellow will not turn green again.

**4. Not enough light.** Leaves pale gradually across the whole plant, new leaves are small and far apart on the stem, and growth is slow. Move the pothos to a brighter spot, out of direct midday sun.

To tell overwatering and underwatering apart, feel the soil 5 cm (2 in) down and lift the pot. Wet and heavy means too much water. Dry and light means too little. Our article on [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) compares the signs side by side.

## Why are my pothos leaves turning brown?

Where the brown appears tells you most of what you need to know.

**Black or dark brown, soft edges** are a sign of overwatering. NC State specifically mentions the blackening of leaf margins together with root rot. Check the roots (see below) and let the soil dry out properly.

**Crisp, light brown tips and edges** usually mean the soil dried out completely between waterings, or the air is very dry, as it often is near a radiator in winter. NC State suggests a humidifier or a tray of wet pebbles under the pot if your air is dry. Moving the plant away from heat sources helps more.

**Brown, dry patches on the leaves nearest the window** are sunburn. Pothos tolerates some gentle morning sun, but strong sun through glass scorches it. Move it back from the window.

**Brown spots with a yellow halo** are usually a leaf spot infection, which spreads on leaves that stay wet. Water the soil, not the leaves, and remove spotted leaves with clean scissors.

Salt build-up from heavy feeding also browns leaf tips. NC State recommends feeding only every other month, and not in winter. If you see a white crust on the soil, flush the pot with plain water for a minute.

## Why is my pothos drooping?

A drooping pothos has leaves that cannot get enough water. That happens for two very different reasons.

**1. The soil is dry.** This is the usual cause, and the easiest to fix. The pot feels light, and the leaves hang limp but are not yellow. Water thoroughly until water runs out of the drainage hole. The plant should perk up within a few hours to a day.

**2. The roots are rotting.** The soil is wet, but the plant still droops. Rotten roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts even in damp soil. Check the roots right away.

A pothos that droops just after being repotted or moved is usually reacting to the change and recovers within a week if the soil is neither soggy nor dry.

## Does my pothos have root rot?

Check if the plant droops in wet soil, if several leaves yellow at once, or if the stems turn black and soft at the soil line. Slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy pothos roots are firm and white to light tan. Rotten ones are brown to black, mushy, and smell bad. The outer layer often slides off when pinched, leaving a thin thread.

If some roots are firm, cut away every soft one with clean scissors, repot into fresh potting mix in a pot with a drainage hole, and water only when the top half of the soil has dried. If nearly all the roots are gone, the easiest fix with a pothos is to start again: cut a few healthy stem pieces with at least one node each and root them in water. Pothos roots easily this way. ## Why is my pothos losing its variegation?

Variegated pothos fade toward plain green when they do not get enough light. NC State lists low light as the cause. The plant makes more green tissue to catch more light, and the new leaves come out greener, smaller and further apart.

Move it to a brighter spot, ideally near an east- or west-facing window, out of strong direct sun. New leaves should show more variegation. Leaves that have already turned green stay green. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) helps you judge how bright a spot really is.

## Why is my pothos leggy, with small leaves?

Long bare stems with small, widely spaced leaves have two causes that often occur together: too little light, and the natural shedding of older leaves along the stem.

Give the plant more light first. Then prune. NC State recommends cutting stems back to keep a pothos bushy. Cut each long stem just above a leaf, and new shoots usually grow from the node below the cut. Root the cut pieces in water and plant them back into the same pot to fill it out. ## What pests attack pothos?

NC State names mealybugs and scale as the pests to watch for. Spider mites and thrips can also turn up, mostly in warm, dry rooms. Signs to look for:

- **White, cottony clumps** in leaf joints and on the undersides of leaves: mealybugs.
- **Small brown or tan bumps** on stems that scrape off with a fingernail: scale.
- **Sticky leaves** or a black sooty film: honeydew from either of those.
- **Fine pale speckling, sometimes with webbing:** spider mites.
- **Silvery streaks with tiny black dots:** thrips.

Isolate the plant, then remove what you can with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol. Follow up with insecticidal soap or horticultural oil, repeating as the label says, because one treatment misses the eggs. Badly infested stems are often easier to cut off and discard. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers each one in more detail.

## Is pothos toxic to pets?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/golden-pothos) lists golden pothos as toxic to dogs and cats. It contains insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, and chewing it causes mouth irritation, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. Hang it out of reach if you have a pet that chews plants.
