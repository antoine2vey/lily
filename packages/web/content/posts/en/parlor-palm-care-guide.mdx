Parlor palm care is mostly about restraint: bright indirect light, water once the surface of the compost has dried, and a room between 10 and 27°C (50 and 80°F). The parlor palm (*Chamaedorea elegans*) grows slowly, reaches about 1.5 m (5 ft) indoors, and is safe around cats and dogs. Most problems come from too much water, too much direct sun or hard tap water, not from neglect.

## Parlor palm care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light, such as a north- or east-facing window. No direct summer sun |
| Water | When the top of the compost is dry; never leave it standing in water. Less in winter |
| Humidity | Average room humidity; away from radiators, which cause brown tips |
| Temperature | 10 to 27°C (50 to 80°F); 20 to 27°C (68 to 80°F) in spring and summer for best growth |
| Soil | 3 parts peat-free ericaceous compost to 1 part perlite, in a pot with drainage |
| Feeding | Balanced liquid fertiliser once a month, spring to autumn; none in winter |
| Toxicity | Non-toxic to cats and dogs |

The temperatures, compost mix and feeding schedule come from the [RHS growing guide for chamaedorea](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/chamaedorea/growing-guide). The light, size and pest details come from [NC State Extension's plant profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/chamaedorea-elegans/). The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/parlor-palm) lists the plant as non-toxic to dogs and cats.

## How much light does a parlor palm need?

Bright, indirect light, and less than most palms. NC State notes it is native to southern Mexico and northern Central America, where it grows in the shade of the forest canopy, which is why it copes with rooms other palms do not. NC State names a north- or east-facing window as the best spot and notes that it tolerates low light.

Tolerating low light is not the same as growing in it. In a dim corner the palm survives but produces few new fronds, and the RHS notes that too little light can turn the leaves yellow. Too much is a problem too: direct summer sun through glass scorches the fronds, leaving pale or brown patches.

A practical rule: put it where you could read comfortably during the day without a lamp, but where the sun does not fall on the leaves for more than an hour or two in the morning. A south- or west-facing room works if the palm stands a couple of metres back from the window or behind a sheer curtain. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot.

## How often should you water a parlor palm?

Water when the surface of the compost feels dry, then water thoroughly and let the excess drain away. The RHS is clear on the main rule: never let the plant sit in water. NC State lists overwatering as the palm's main problem.

In a warm, bright room in summer that often means about once a week. In winter, with less light and growth, the gap typically stretches to two or three weeks. Check the compost rather than following a calendar. Our article on [how often to water a parlor palm](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-parlor-palm) goes through the signs and seasonal intervals in more detail.

The water itself matters. The RHS recommends rainwater at room temperature, and lists hard tap water as one cause of brown leaf tips. If your tap water is hard (it leaves white scale in the kettle), use rainwater, filtered water or water from a dehumidifier when you can.

## Why does my parlor palm get brown tips?

Brown tips are the most common complaint with this palm. The RHS lists four causes: overwatering, underwatering, hard water and direct sun. Here is how to tell them apart:

- **Overwatering.** Compost still damp a week after watering, lower fronds yellowing, and possibly a sour smell. Let it dry further between waterings and check the drainage hole is not blocked.
- **Underwatering.** Compost dry well below the surface, fronds slightly limp, and tips crisp. Water thoroughly and check sooner next time.
- **Hard water.** Tips brown slowly over months even though watering seems right, and a white crust may form on the compost. Switch to rainwater or filtered water.
- **Direct sun.** Pale or brown patches on the fronds facing the window. Move the palm back.

Very dry air, usually near a radiator, also browns the tips. Cut off brown tips with clean scissors if they bother you, following the shape of the leaf. The brown part will not turn green again, and new growth shows whether the fix worked.

## Does a parlor palm need high humidity?

Not particularly. NC State rates its humidity needs as low to medium, which makes it one of the easier palms for a heated home. The RHS notes that misting can help and that dry air causes brown tips.

In winter, central heating can push indoor humidity well below what the palm likes, especially right beside a radiator. Moving it away from the heat source helps more than anything else. Grouping it with other plants or standing it on a tray of damp gravel helps a little. Misting raises humidity for only a few minutes, so treat it as a minor extra rather than a fix. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

Dry air also favours spider mites, which the RHS and NC State both list as a pest of this palm. Fine pale speckling on the fronds and faint webbing between them are the signs. Our [spider mite treatment guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) covers what to do. Mealybugs and scale are the other common pests: look for white fluffy patches or small brown bumps along the stems and the undersides of fronds.

## What soil and pot does a parlor palm need?

The RHS recommends a mix of 3 parts peat-free ericaceous (acidic) compost to 1 part perlite. NC State describes a humus-rich mix. Either way, the compost should hold some moisture but drain freely, and the pot must have a drainage hole. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains the ingredients.

Parlor palms like to be slightly crowded in their pots. Repot in spring only when the roots fill the pot, which NC State puts at about every other year. Choose a pot about 2 cm (1 in) wider than the old one. The RHS warns that a much larger pot holds wet compost around the roots and risks root rot.

Many parlor palms are sold as several seedlings planted together in one pot. Leave them as they are. Separating them damages the roots, and the palm recovers slowly.

## How do you feed a parlor palm?

Feed with a balanced liquid fertiliser once a month from spring to autumn, as the RHS recommends, and stop in winter. A slow-growing palm needs little, and too much fertiliser burns the roots and browns the leaf tips. If you are unsure, dilute to half the label strength.

## How do you care for a parlor palm in winter?

Winter is when most parlor palms struggle, because two things change at once: the light drops and the heating comes on. Adjust four things:

1. **Water less.** Growth slows, so the compost dries more slowly. Keep checking the surface, and expect to water every two to three weeks in a cool room. The RHS says to reduce watering in winter.
2. **Stop feeding** until spring.
3. **Give it more light.** Move it closer to the window, since the winter sun is weak enough not to scorch.
4. **Keep it out of extremes.** The RHS says the palm tolerates cooler conditions in winter, down to about 10°C (50°F). Cold draughts from a door or an icy window pane, and hot dry air from a radiator, cause more damage than an evenly cool room.

A parlor palm that loses a lower frond or two in winter is not necessarily in trouble. Old fronds yellow and die naturally as new ones appear. Worry when the newest fronds yellow, or when several fronds yellow at once.
