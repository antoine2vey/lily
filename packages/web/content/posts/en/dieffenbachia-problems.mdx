Most dieffenbachia problems trace back to watering: the plant wants a thorough soak, then for the top 2.5 cm (1 in) of compost to dry before the next one. Yellow lower leaves on wet compost, a soft, drooping stem and a sour smell all point to too much water. Brown edges usually point to dry compost, cold or too much fertiliser, and a slow loss of the oldest leaves is simply how the plant grows.

## Why are my dieffenbachia leaves turning yellow?

The causes, from most to least common:

1. **Old leaves dropping naturally.** The [Clemson Extension dieffenbachia factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/dieffenbachia/) notes that the lower leaves drop as the plant matures. If it is one leaf at a time, at the bottom of the cane, while new leaves keep coming at the top, nothing is wrong.
2. **Overwatering.** Several leaves yellow together, the compost is still damp a week after watering, and the stem may feel soft at the base. Let the top 2.5 cm (1 in) dry before watering again, and empty the saucer. If the plant also droops, check the roots (next section).
3. **Hunger.** Clemson lists yellowing, smaller new leaves and stunted growth as signs the plant lacks nutrients. A plant that has sat in the same compost for years without feeding is the likely case. Feed from March to September, as described below.
4. **Too little light.** Leaves pale and yellow slowly across the plant, and new leaves are small and far apart. Move it closer to a bright window.
5. **Cold.** Leaves yellow or develop dark patches after a cold night or a spell near a door left open in winter.

To tell them apart, look at the pattern. Only the lowest leaf: age. Many leaves plus wet compost: water. Pale leaves everywhere and weak growth: hunger or light.

## Why are my dieffenbachia leaves turning brown at the edges?

1. **Too much fertiliser.** Clemson notes that overfeeding burns the leaf margins. A white crust on the compost or the pot rim is a giveaway. Flush the pot with plenty of plain water, let it drain, and feed less.
2. **Dry compost.** If the pot was allowed to dry right out, the edges and tips go brown and crisp. Water thoroughly and do not let it get that dry again.
3. **Cold draughts and sudden temperature changes.** Clemson warns that dieffenbachia does not like either. Brown patches on the side facing a door, window or air-conditioning vent point here. Keep it between 16 and 24°C (60 and 75°F).
4. **Direct sun.** Bleached, then brown, dry patches in the middle of leaves facing the glass. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/dieffenbachia-seguine/) advises protecting it from direct sun.
5. **Very dry air.** NC State lists high humidity among its preferences. Crisp brown tips in winter, next to a radiator, point to dry air. A humidifier or grouping plants helps more than misting.

Brown edges do not turn green again. Trim the brown part off with clean scissors, following the leaf's shape, or remove the leaf if most of it is damaged.

## Why is my dieffenbachia drooping?

Drooping on damp compost is the warning sign for root rot. Clemson explains that it follows a slow-draining mix or watering too often. The roots turn brown and soft, can no longer take up water, and the plant wilts even though the pot is wet. The stem may become soft and dark at soil level.

How to tell it from thirst: a thirsty plant has a light pot and dry compost and stands up again within a few hours of watering. A rotting plant droops on wet compost and stays limp.

What to do:

1. Take the plant out of its pot and look at the roots.
2. If some are firm and pale, cut off the brown, mushy ones, repot into fresh, free-draining compost in a pot with drainage holes, and water only once the top 2.5 cm (1 in) is dry.
3. If the base of the stem is soft and rotten, cut the healthy upper part of the cane off and root it as a cutting (see the bare-cane section below).

## Why does my dieffenbachia have spots on its leaves?

Brown or tan spots, sometimes with a yellow edge, that spread across the leaf rather than starting at the margin, are usually a fungal leaf spot. NC State lists fungal leaf spot among the diseases of dieffenbachia. Leaves that stay wet, crowded plants and poor air movement all help it spread.

Remove the spotted leaves, water the compost rather than the leaves, and give the plant more space. Our guide to [houseplant fungal diseases](/en/blog/houseplant-fungal-diseases) covers leaf spots in more detail.

## Why are my dieffenbachia leaves sticky?

Sticky, shiny leaves, sometimes with a black sooty film, mean sap-sucking insects. Clemson names mealybugs and aphids, both of which leave sticky honeydew. Look under the leaves and where they join the stem.

- **Mealybugs:** white, cottony clusters in the leaf joints. Dab them with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol, then treat with insecticidal soap, repeating as the label directs.
- **Aphids:** small green or black insects on new growth. Rinse them off in the shower and follow up with insecticidal soap if they come back.

Fine webbing and pale speckled leaves point to spider mites instead. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) helps you identify each one.

## Why is my dieffenbachia tall with a bare stem?

A tall cane with a tuft of leaves at the top is the shape every dieffenbachia takes with age, because the lower leaves drop as it grows. Low light speeds it up, because the stem stretches toward the window.

You can reset it. Clemson lists cane cuttings, tip cuttings and air layering as ways to propagate it.

1. Wear gloves. The sap irritates skin and eyes.
2. Cut the cane 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) above the compost. The stump usually resprouts.
3. Root the leafy top as a tip cutting in water or moist compost.
4. Cut the bare cane into pieces 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 in) long, each with at least one node, and lay them half-buried on their side in moist compost.

Keep cuttings warm and in bright, indirect light. Our guide to [propagating plants from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers aftercare.

## How to stop these problems coming back

Most of the problems above disappear once the routine is right:

- **Light:** Clemson recommends bright, filtered light from spring to autumn, and bright or even direct light in winter, when the sun is weak.
- **Water:** soak thoroughly, then let the top 2.5 cm (1 in) dry. Water less in winter.
- **Food:** from March to September, a liquid fertiliser at half strength every 2 to 4 weeks, as Clemson suggests. Nothing in winter.
- **Temperature:** 16 to 24°C (60 to 75°F), away from cold draughts and heating vents.
- **Soil:** a loose, free-draining houseplant compost in a pot with drainage holes.

## Is dieffenbachia toxic to pets?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/dieffenbachia) lists dieffenbachia as toxic to dogs and cats. Chewing it causes intense burning and irritation of the mouth, tongue and lips, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. NC State notes it is toxic to children too, and recommends washing your hands after handling it. Keep it out of reach of pets and small children.
