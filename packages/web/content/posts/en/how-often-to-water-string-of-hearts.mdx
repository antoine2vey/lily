How often to water string of hearts comes down to one rule: wait until the mix is dry all the way through, then water thoroughly. In a bright room that is often every 1 to 2 weeks in spring and summer and every 3 to 4 weeks in winter, when the plant rests. String of hearts stores water in its leaves, stems and the small round tubers along its vines, so it survives a missed watering far better than a wet pot.

## How do you know when string of hearts needs water?

[NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ceropegia-woodii/) describes string of hearts (*Ceropegia woodii*) as a tender succulent from southern Africa and gives a clear instruction: be careful not to overwater, and let it dry out completely between waterings. That is stricter than the "top inch is dry" test used for most houseplants, and stricter than older advice on this site, which suggested watering as soon as the top 2.5 cm (1 in) felt dry.

Check before you water:

1. **Test the whole depth, not the surface.** Push a wooden skewer or chopstick to the bottom of the pot and pull it out. If damp soil clings to it, wait.
2. **Lift the pot.** A dry string of hearts pot is very light, especially a hanging one. After a few waterings you can tell by weight alone.
3. **Feel the leaves.** Healthy leaves are firm and slightly thick. When the plant has used its reserve, they turn soft and thin, and may start to pucker.

When the mix is dry, water slowly over the whole surface until water runs from the drainage hole, then let the pot drain completely before you hang it back up or return it to a cachepot.

## How often to water string of hearts in each season

These are starting points for a plant in bright, indirect light in a heated home. Confirm each time with the skewer.

| Season | Starting interval |
|---|---|
| Spring | Every 10 to 14 days as new growth appears |
| Summer | Every 7 to 14 days, more often in a small hanging pot |
| Autumn | Every 2 to 3 weeks as growth slows |
| Winter | Every 3 to 4 weeks, sometimes less |

NC State notes that string of hearts is dormant over winter and that watering should be reduced even further then. [University of Vermont Extension](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/houseplant-care-during-winter-months) makes the same point for houseplants in general: winter care starts with reducing water and holding off until the mix is truly dry. A plant that drank every week in July may need only a small drink once a month in January.

## What changes how often string of hearts needs water?

- **Light.** NC State recommends bright, indirect light. In good light the plant grows and drinks steadily. In low light it uses little water and the mix stays damp for weeks, which is when rot sets in. Large gaps between the leaves on new growth are NC State's sign that light is too low.
- **Pot and soil.** NC State recommends a well-drained, sandy potting mix. A cactus and succulent mix, or houseplant compost with a third of perlite or grit added, dries in days rather than weeks. Use a pot with a drainage hole. NC State notes that the plant likes to be crowded, so keep it in a snug pot: extra mix around a small root system stays wet. Our guides to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) and [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) explain why.
- **Hanging baskets.** A pot near the ceiling sits in the warmest air in the room and dries faster than one on a shelf. Check hanging pots more often in summer, and check that they are not dripping into a closed cachepot after watering.
- **Temperature.** A warm room dries the mix faster than a cool one. In winter, a plant near a radiator may need water a little more often than one on a cool windowsill, but still much less than in summer.

## What are the signs of an overwatered string of hearts?

NC State lists root rot and yellowing leaves as the results of overwatering. Look for:

- leaves turning yellow, often with soft, translucent patches;
- leaves dropping from the vines while the mix is still damp;
- stems that turn brown and mushy near the soil, so whole strands collapse at once;
- vines that look limp even though the pot is heavy and wet.

What to do:

1. Stop watering and let the mix dry out completely.
2. If strands keep collapsing, take the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots and tubers are firm; rotten ones are brown, soft and smell sour.
3. Cut away rotten roots and mushy stems, and repot in fresh, dry, gritty mix.
4. Wait a few days before watering lightly.

If most of the roots are gone, rescue healthy vines. Lay a few strands on a pot of dry mix so the tubers touch the surface; NC State notes that the tubers root and form new plants when they meet the soil. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers severe cases step by step.

## What are the signs of an underwatered string of hearts?

NC State gives wilted leaves as the sign of underwatering. Look for:

- leaves that are soft, thin or wrinkled, on firm, dry stems;
- leaves curling slightly inward;
- a pot that feels feather-light, with mix pulling away from the sides.

Water thoroughly until it drains. If the mix has shrunk and water runs straight down the sides, stand the pot in a bowl of water for 15 to 20 minutes so the root ball soaks up evenly. The leaves should plump up within a day or two. If you are unsure which problem you have, check the mix: soft leaves in wet mix point to the roots, soft leaves in dry mix point to thirst.

## Should you water string of hearts from the bottom?

You can. Bottom watering suits a plant with long vines covering the surface of the pot, because you avoid wetting the leaves and stems where they sit on the soil. Stand the pot in 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of water for 15 to 20 minutes, until the top of the mix darkens, then let it drain fully. Water from the top every few months to flush out fertiliser salts.
