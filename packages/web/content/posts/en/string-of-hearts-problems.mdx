Most string of hearts problems come from water: yellow leaves, falling leaves and mushy stems usually mean the soil stayed wet too long, while thin, wrinkled, deflated hearts usually mean it stayed dry too long. Ceropegia woodii is a succulent vine from southern Africa that stores water in its leaves and in a tuber under the soil, and [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/ceropegia-woodii/) recommends letting the soil dry out completely between waterings, and watering even less in winter. The other common problems are light (long bare gaps between leaves) and mealybugs.

Find your symptom below. Each section lists the likely causes in order, how to tell them apart and what to do.

## Why are my string of hearts leaves turning yellow?

1. **Overwatering.** This is the cause most of the time. NC State names overwatering as the reason for yellow leaves and root rot. The yellow leaves are soft or translucent, often start near the soil, and the soil is still damp days after watering. Let the pot dry out completely before you water again, and check the base of the vines for rot (see below).
2. **Cold, wet soil in winter.** The plant slows down in winter, and a pot that dried in a week in summer may stay wet for three. Yellowing that starts in November or December on a plant watered on its summer schedule is this.
3. **Normal leaf ageing.** A few yellow leaves on older sections, with firm vines and new leaves growing at the tips, are not a problem. Pull them off.
4. **Too much sun.** Leaves in strong direct sun turn pale, bleached yellow or pinkish on the side facing the light, and the soil is not wet. Move the plant back from the window or behind a sheer curtain.

The quick test: press a yellow leaf. Soft and squishy on damp soil means water. Firm, pale and dry on dry soil means light.

## Why is my string of hearts drooping or deflated?

Thin, soft, wrinkled hearts that feel deflated have two opposite causes, and the soil tells them apart.

- **Dry soil, light pot:** the plant is thirsty. NC State notes that wilted leaves are a sign of underwatering. Soak the pot until water runs out of the bottom and let it drain. The leaves should firm up within a day or two.
- **Wet soil, heavy pot:** the roots are rotting and can no longer take up water, so the plant wilts even though it is sitting in water. Watering more makes this worse. Go to the root rot section below.

Remember that string of hearts vines always hang. Limp-looking vines with plump, firm leaves are normal.

## Why is my string of hearts losing leaves?

Leaves falling off when you touch the vines usually mean the plant is stressed at the roots.

1. **Root rot from wet soil.** Leaves drop along with yellowing and soft stems near the soil.
2. **Long drought followed by a heavy soak.** Some leaves shed after a sudden swing. Water when the soil is dry, not when the leaves are already shrivelled.
3. **Cold.** The RHS rates [Ceropegia linearis subsp. woodii](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/74288/ceropegia-linearis-subsp-woodii/details) as H1C, a tender plant that needs at least 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50°F). A plant left against a cold window or near an open door in winter can drop leaves quickly.
4. **Rough handling.** The leaves are attached by thin stalks, and some loss when you move or repot the plant is normal.

## How do you know if a string of hearts has root rot?

Signs of root rot:

- the vines go soft, brown or black where they meet the soil;
- the main tuber under the soil feels mushy instead of firm;
- the soil smells sour, and leaves drop and yellow at the same time.

Rot spreads from the roots up, so act early:

1. Take the plant out of its pot and remove the old soil.
2. Cut away every soft, dark root and tuber. Healthy tubers and roots are firm and pale.
3. If healthy roots remain, replant in fresh, dry, gritty mix (half cactus mix, half perlite works) in a small pot with a drainage hole, and wait about a week before watering.
4. If most of the base is gone, save the plant through its vines. Cut healthy sections, lay them on top of damp gritty mix, and press the small beads along the stem into the soil. NC State notes that these aerial tubers root wherever they touch soil.

## Why does my string of hearts have big gaps between leaves?

Long sections of bare stem between leaves mean the plant is not getting enough light. NC State notes that large gaps between leaves indicate too little light. The new growth comes in thin and pale, and leaves are small.

Move the plant to bright, indirect light: close to an east window, or back from a south or west window behind a sheer curtain. Some gentle morning sun is fine. The gaps already there will not fill in, but new growth will be denser. You can trim the bare vines back and replant the cuttings in the same pot.

## Why is the top of my string of hearts bare?

A sparse top with long, healthy vines is very common. The original stems at the soil line age and lose leaves, and all the growth goes to the tips. It is not a disease.

To fill it in, coil some of the long vines on top of the soil and pin them down with a bent paper clip, so the stem and its beads touch the mix. They root and send up new stems. You can also cut 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) lengths of vine and lay them on the soil surface the same way. Our guide to [propagating cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers the basics.

## Why are my string of hearts leaves brown or crispy?

- **Scorch:** dry, brown or bleached patches on leaves facing a sunny window. NC State notes that too much sunlight scorches leaves. Move the plant back from the glass.
- **Severe drought:** leaves dry up completely after shrivelling for weeks. Water more often in summer.
- **Rot:** brown that is soft and spreads from the soil line is not crispy at all. See root rot above.

## What pests attack string of hearts?

NC State lists aphids, mealybugs and scale.

- **Mealybugs** are the most common. They look like white cotton fluff in the leaf joints, on the beads and sometimes on the roots. Dab them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, rinse the vines in lukewarm water, and repeat weekly for a month.
- **Aphids** cluster on new tips. Rinse them off or rub them off with your fingers.
- **Scale** looks like small brown bumps on the stems. Scrape them off with a fingernail or an alcohol swab.
- **Fungus gnats** are small black flies around the pot. Their larvae live in wet soil, so letting the pot dry out fully between waterings is the main fix.

Insecticidal soap or horticultural oil, used exactly as the label says, works on soft-bodied pests. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) explains how to identify and treat each one.

## How do you prevent string of hearts problems?

Most problems disappear with a few habits:

- water only when the soil is dry all the way through, and much less in winter, when NC State notes the plant is dormant;
- use a gritty, fast-draining mix in a pot with a drainage hole, and keep the pot small (the plant likes to be crowded, so repot only when needed);
- give it bright indirect light;
- keep it above 10 °C (50°F) in winter, away from cold glass and draughts.

## Quick answers

**Is string of hearts toxic to cats?** It is not on the ASPCA's list of toxic or non-toxic plants, so there is no official verdict. Keep it out of reach of pets that chew plants.

**Why are the undersides of the leaves purple?** That is normal. NC State describes the leaves as purple underneath. Brighter light deepens the colour.
