How often to water a cast iron plant depends on the soil, not the calendar: water when the top half of the pot has dried out. In a typical room that works out to roughly every 1 to 2 weeks in spring and summer and every 3 to 4 weeks in winter. A cast iron plant forgives a missed watering far more easily than soil that stays wet.

## How often to water a cast iron plant through the year

The cast iron plant (*Aspidistra elatior*) earned its name by surviving shade, neglect and dry spells. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/aspidistra-elatior/) describes it as drought tolerant and advises watering regularly from spring to autumn and sparingly in winter. It grows from thick rhizomes that hold water, so it has reserves to draw on.

These starting intervals assume a plastic pot with a drainage hole, ordinary potting mix and a room at 18 to 24°C (65 to 75°F). Treat them as the day to check the soil, not the day to water.

| Season | Starting interval | What is going on |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Every 1 to 2 weeks | New leaves unfurl from the rhizomes; water use climbs |
| Summer | Every 1 to 2 weeks, sometimes weekly in a warm, bright room | Fastest drying of the year |
| Autumn | Every 2 to 3 weeks | Growth slows as days shorten |
| Winter | Every 3 to 4 weeks, longer in a cool room | Little growth; NC State says water sparingly |

The cast iron plant grows slowly at the best of times, putting out only a few new leaves a year, so it uses less water than a fast-growing plant of the same size.

## How to tell when a cast iron plant needs water

The calendar only gets you close. These checks tell you whether today is the day. [Clemson's watering factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) describes all of them.

- **Finger test.** Push a finger into the soil. For a cast iron plant, wait until it is dry well past the first knuckle, roughly the top half of a small pot. If you feel moisture, wait a few days and check again.
- **Pot weight.** Lift the pot right after watering and again when it is dry. After a few rounds, you can tell by weight alone.
- **Clay pot tap.** Tap a terracotta pot with your knuckle. A hollow ring means the soil has pulled away from the sides and is dry; a dull thud means it is still moist.
- **Wooden skewer.** For a big pot, push a bamboo skewer to the bottom and pull it out after a minute. Dark, damp soil clinging to it means wait.

Moisture meters are handy but vary in accuracy, Clemson notes, so check them against your finger now and then.

## How to water a cast iron plant

Water slowly over the whole soil surface until water runs out of the drainage hole. Wait 15 to 30 minutes, then empty the saucer or the decorative pot it sits in. Clemson advises never letting a plant stand in water, which keeps the roots short of air.

If the soil has dried so far that water runs straight down the sides of the pot, water twice, about half an hour apart, or stand the pot in a sink of water for 20 to 30 minutes and let it drain. Clemson also recommends water at room temperature, around 17 to 22°C (62 to 72°F), and avoiding softened water.

## What changes how often you water

**Light.** The cast iron plant is often put in the darkest corner of a room, and that is fine for the plant: NC State lists it as preferring partial to full shade and intolerant of direct sun. But a plant in deep shade dries out much more slowly than one near a window. In a dim hallway, the winter interval can stretch past a month.

**Temperature.** A warm room dries the soil faster. A cool room or an unheated entrance hall in winter can mean watering only every 5 or 6 weeks.

**Pot.** Terracotta breathes and dries faster than plastic or glazed ceramic. A large pot holds water longer than a small one, and a plant in a pot much bigger than its roots sits in wet soil for days. See [how to choose a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant).

**Soil.** NC State recommends a rich but well-drained soil. A peat-heavy mix holds more water than one with bark or perlite, according to Clemson.

**Plant size.** A big clump that has filled its pot with rhizomes drinks more and may need water more often, even in winter.

## Signs you are overwatering a cast iron plant

Overwatering is the one reliable way to kill a cast iron plant. Clemson calls overwatering the main cause of death of potted plants. Watch for:

- Yellowing leaves, often starting with the older ones.
- Leaves that go limp, brown or black at the base, where the stalk meets the soil.
- Soil that is still wet a week after watering, or that smells sour.
- Brown, soft, mushy rhizomes and roots when you unpot it, instead of firm, pale ones.

If you see these, stop watering, empty any standing water, and let the soil dry well down. If the base of the leaves is soft, unpot the plant, cut off soft rhizomes and roots, and repot in fresh, free-draining mix. Our [cast iron plant problems guide](/en/blog/cast-iron-plant-problems) covers rot and other symptoms in more detail.

## Signs you are underwatering a cast iron plant

Underwatering takes a long time to hurt this plant, but it does happen:

- Brown, dry leaf tips or edges, often on several leaves at once.
- Leaves that curl lengthwise or lose their gloss.
- Soil that has shrunk from the sides of the pot and is dry all the way down.
- New leaves that come in small, or no new leaves for a whole growing season.

Clemson points out that wilting from too much and too little water can look similar. The soil tells them apart: dry and light means thirst, wet and heavy means the roots are in trouble. Our [overwatering vs underwatering guide](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) has a side-by-side comparison.

Brown tips can also come from salts building up in the pot from fertiliser or hard water. Water heavily enough that some drains out every time, and flush the pot with plenty of water a couple of times a year.

## A simple routine

Check the soil on your chosen day. If the top half of the pot is dry, water until it drains and empty the saucer. If not, check again in a few days. In autumn, stretch the check interval; in spring, shorten it again. [Lily](/en) can keep that schedule for you, and indoors it lengthens the gap automatically as daylight shortens in winter.

The cast iron plant is non-toxic to cats and dogs, according to the [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/cast-iron-plant). For light, soil, feeding and repotting, see our [cast iron plant care guide](/en/blog/cast-iron-plant-care-guide).
