Peace lily care is simple once you know what it wants: bright, indirect light, soil that stays lightly moist but never soggy, and a room between 20 and 29°C (68 and 85°F) by day that never drops below 16°C (60°F). Water when the top 2.5 cm (1 in) of soil is dry, or when the leaves just begin to soften. It tolerates a shady room, but it flowers far more with more light.

## Peace lily care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light. Tolerates shade but flowers less. No direct sun |
| Water | When the top 2.5 cm (1 in) is dry; roughly weekly in summer, every 10 to 14 days in winter |
| Humidity | Average room humidity is fine; very dry air browns the tips |
| Temperature | 20 to 29°C (68 to 85°F) by day, about 5°C (10°F) cooler at night, never below 16°C (60°F) |
| Soil | Any well-drained peat-free or peat-based potting mix, pot with drainage |
| Feeding | Balanced fertiliser at quarter strength every 6 to 8 weeks in spring and summer |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats and dogs (insoluble calcium oxalate crystals) |

The temperatures, feeding and repotting advice come from [Clemson Cooperative Extension's peace lily factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/peace-lily/), and the shade and watering notes from [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/spathiphyllum/).

## How much light does a peace lily need?

A peace lily tolerates low light better than almost any flowering houseplant, but tolerating is not the same as doing well. Clemson notes that it grows best in bright, indirect light, and that too little light is one of the main reasons it stops flowering.

Good spots:

- 1 to 2 m (3 to 6 ft) from an east- or west-facing window.
- Right beside a north-facing window.
- Further back from a south-facing window, or behind a sheer curtain.

Keep it out of direct sun. NC State notes that direct sun scorches the leaves, leaving pale or brown patches and brown tips. If yours sits in a dim corner and has not flowered in a year, moving it closer to a window is the first thing to try. ## How often should you water a peace lily?

Clemson recommends keeping the soil moist but not soggy, letting it dry slightly between waterings, and never leaving the pot standing in water. NC State gives the same advice. A simple rule: water when the top 2.5 cm (1 in) of soil feels dry.

In a bright, warm room that is often about once a week in summer and every 10 to 14 days in winter. A peace lily also tells you when it is thirsty: the whole plant goes limp and the leaves droop. It perks up within a few hours of watering. Using the droop as your signal is fine now and then, but letting it collapse every time stresses the plant and browns the leaf tips.

Water thoroughly until water runs out of the drainage hole, then empty the saucer. NC State notes that bottom watering works well for peace lilies too. Overwatering is the bigger danger: Clemson says root and stem diseases come from soil that is kept too wet. Our article on [how often to water a peace lily](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-peace-lily) goes into the season and pot factors in detail.

[Lily](/en) can take the guesswork out of the interval: it schedules watering per plant and adjusts it for the season's daylight, which matters for a plant that drinks far less in winter.

## Does a peace lily need high humidity?

Not really. A peace lily grows fine in average household humidity. NC State lists low humidity among the causes of brown leaf tips, along with too much sun and letting the soil dry out. In a home with heating, dry air in winter can brown the tips, but it rarely does more than that.

If the tips brown in winter:

- Group it with other plants, which raises the humidity around them slightly.
- Stand the pot on a tray of pebbles with water below the top of the pebbles.
- Use a humidifier if the room is very dry. It is the only thing that raises room humidity meaningfully.

Misting does very little, because the water evaporates within minutes. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

## Temperature

Clemson gives 20 to 29°C (68 to 85°F) during the day with nights about 5°C (10°F) cooler, and advises against letting it fall below 16°C (60°F). NC State says growth slows between 4 and 16°C (40 and 60°F) and that long periods below 4°C (40°F) damage the plant. Keep it away from cold windows and doors in winter and from the blast of a heater or air conditioner.

## How to get a peace lily to flower

The white "flower" is a spathe, a modified leaf wrapped around a spike of tiny true flowers. Clemson notes that a peace lily that does not flower is usually either less than a year old or getting too little light. NC State adds that the spathes turn green after about 10 days and then last about another month.

To encourage flowers:

1. Move it to brighter, indirect light.
2. Feed it lightly in spring and summer, as below.
3. Be patient with young plants.

Cut each spent flower stalk off at its base once it turns green or brown.

## Feeding

Peace lilies are light feeders. Clemson recommends a balanced fertiliser such as 20-20-20 at a quarter of the label strength every 6 to 8 weeks in spring and summer. NC State also recommends a quarter-strength feed and flushing the soil with plain water between feeds so salts do not build up. Too much fertiliser browns the leaf tips. Skip feeding in winter. Our guide on [how to fertilize houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) explains how to dilute.

## When to repot a peace lily

NC State advises keeping a peace lily slightly pot-bound and repotting in late winter or early spring, around February or March. Repot when roots circle the bottom of the pot or the plant wilts within a day or two of watering.

1. Water the day before.
2. Slide the plant out and loosen the outer roots.
3. Move it to a pot only 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider, as Clemson recommends.
4. Fill with fresh, well-drained potting mix and water thoroughly.

Our [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) covers the method in more detail.

## How to propagate a peace lily

Peace lilies are propagated by division, as NC State notes. A single leaf will not grow into a new plant, because the leaves grow from crowns at the base. Do it at repotting time in spring:

1. Take the plant out of its pot and shake off loose soil.
2. Find the separate crowns, the points where groups of leaves join the roots.
3. Pull them apart by hand, or cut through the root ball with a clean knife. Each division needs at least two or three leaves and a good handful of roots.
4. Pot each piece in fresh mix at the same depth it grew before.
5. Water well and keep it out of direct sun. A little drooping for the first week is normal while the roots recover.

## Common peace lily problems

- **Drooping:** usually thirst, sometimes root rot if the soil is wet.
- **Yellow leaves:** overwatering, or simply an old leaf at the base dying back.
- **Brown tips:** dry soil, dry air, too much fertiliser or direct sun.
- **No flowers:** too little light or a young plant.
- **Pests:** Clemson lists fungus gnats, mealybugs, spider mites and scale; NC State says mealybugs are the most common.

## Is a peace lily toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. Despite the name, it is not a true lily (Lilium). The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/peace-lily) lists it as toxic to dogs and cats because of insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which cause mouth irritation, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. Keep it out of reach of pets that chew leaves.
