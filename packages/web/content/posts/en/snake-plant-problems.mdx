Nearly all snake plant problems come from soil that stays wet for too long. Soft, mushy or yellow leaves, and leaves that fall over at the base, usually mean the roots are rotting, and the fix starts with taking the plant out of its pot. Wrinkled, thin leaves on bone-dry soil mean the opposite. In winter a snake plant needs water only every one to two months.

## Snake plant problems: start with the soil and the base

Two checks sort out most cases. Push a finger or a wooden skewer into the soil to the bottom third of the pot: is it wet or dry? Then press the base of the leaves where they meet the soil: firm, or soft and dark?

The snake plant (now classed as *Dracaena trifasciata*, formerly *Sansevieria trifasciata*) stores water in thick leaves and in its rhizomes. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/dracaena-trifasciata/) is blunt about it: "do not overwater, as the roots will rot." It advises letting the soil dry between waterings from spring to autumn, and watering only every one to two months in winter. [Clemson's watering factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) uses the snake plant as its example of a plant prone to rotting from overwatering.

## Why is my snake plant drooping or falling over?

**1. The roots or the leaf bases are rotting.** This is the most common cause by far. Leaves that bend or flop over at soil level, feel soft at the base, or come away when you tug them, with soil that is still damp, point to rot. Do not water. Go to the root rot section below.

**2. Too little light.** A snake plant survives in a dim corner, but it grows thin, pale leaves there that stretch and then bend under their own weight. If the drooping leaves are firm at the base and the plant sits far from a window, light is the likely cause. NC State says it can take direct sun for 2 to 6 hours a day. Move it to a bright spot, close to a window, over a week or two so the leaves do not scorch. Leaves that have already bent will not straighten; new ones will grow upright.

**3. A long dry spell.** Underwatered leaves lose their firmness, wrinkle lengthwise and may curl or lean. The soil is dry all the way down and the pot feels light. This takes months, not weeks, outside of a hot summer. Water thoroughly and the leaves usually firm up over the following days.

**4. The plant is top-heavy.** Tall leaves in a small, light plastic pot can tip and splay. Move it into a heavier pot, such as terracotta, only one size up.

## Why are my snake plant leaves soft and mushy?

Soft, squishy, sometimes translucent leaves are the clearest sign of trouble in a snake plant. There are two causes.

**1. Too much water.** Mushiness that starts at the base of the leaf and moves up, often with yellowing and a sour smell from the soil, is rot from wet soil. The usual reasons: watering on a schedule instead of checking the soil, a pot without a drainage hole, water left in a cachepot or saucer, a pot much bigger than the roots, or dense potting soil that never dries.

**2. Cold.** Mushy, water-soaked patches that appear partway up the leaf, a few days after a cold night by a window or a trip home from the shop in winter, are cold damage. NC State notes the plant tolerates cool temperatures around 10°C (50°F); below that, and especially with wet soil, tissue collapses. Keep it above 10°C and away from cold glass.

Either way, cut damaged leaves off at the base with a clean knife. A mushy leaf does not recover.

## Why are my snake plant leaves turning yellow?

**1. Wet soil.** Yellow leaves together with softness at the base mean overwatering, as above. Check the roots.

**2. Old leaves.** An outer leaf that slowly yellows and dries while the rest of the plant is firm and green is just ageing. Remove it.

**3. Cold or a sudden move.** Yellow, then brown patches can follow cold damage or a jump from shade into strong direct sun. Move the plant gradually.

## Why are my snake plant leaves wrinkled or curling?

**1. Underwatering.** Deep lengthwise wrinkles and leaves that curl inward, on dry soil, mean the plant has used up the water stored in its leaves. Water slowly until it drains, wait 30 minutes, then empty the saucer. Clemson suggests watering twice, half an hour apart, or soaking the pot in a sink when the soil has become too dry to absorb water.

**2. Rotted roots.** If the leaves are wrinkled but the soil is damp, the roots have probably rotted and can no longer take up water. Clemson notes that root damage causes wilting that more water only makes worse. Check the roots before you water again.

## Why does my snake plant have brown tips or brown spots?

**1. Uneven watering.** Dry, brown leaf tips often follow a cycle of very long droughts and heavy waterings.

**2. Physical damage.** The leaf tips are easy to knock or bend, and a damaged tip turns brown and papery. This is cosmetic.

**3. Sun scorch.** Pale or brown dry patches on the side facing a window appear when a plant goes straight from shade into hot sun.

**4. Cold or rot.** Brown spots that are soft rather than dry are cold damage or rot, covered above.

Brown tissue does not turn green again. Trim it with clean scissors, following the shape of the leaf, if it bothers you.

## How do I know if my snake plant has root rot, and can I save it?

Root rot shows above ground as leaves that are soft at the base, lean or fall over, and turn yellow, with soil that stays wet for days. To confirm, slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots and rhizomes are firm, orange to tan or white inside. Rotten ones are brown to black, mushy and smell bad.

To treat it:

1. Remove all the old soil.
2. Cut off every soft root, rhizome section and leaf with a clean knife, back to firm tissue. Wipe the blade with rubbing alcohol between cuts.
3. Leave the plant out of soil for a day or two so the cuts dry over.
4. Repot in a cactus and succulent mix, or regular potting mix with a third grit or perlite, in a pot with a drainage hole that is only a little larger than the roots. Terracotta dries faster than plastic.
5. Wait about a week before the first watering, then water only when the soil is dry most of the way down.

If no healthy roots are left, you can still save the plant from its leaves. Cut a firm leaf into sections 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 in) long, keep track of which end was the bottom, let them dry for a few days, then push the bottom ends into barely moist gritty mix. Roots and new shoots take weeks to months. Plants grown from leaf sections of yellow-edged varieties such as 'Laurentii' usually come back without the yellow edge; dividing a healthy rhizome keeps it.

## What pests attack snake plants?

NC State lists no significant problems but advises watching for mealybugs and spider mites.

- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony clusters deep between the leaves at the base of the rosette, where they are easy to miss. Dab them with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then spray with insecticidal soap, repeating every 7 to 10 days until none return.
- **Spider mites.** Fine pale speckling on the leaves, sometimes with webbing, in warm, dry rooms. Wipe both sides of each leaf with a damp cloth and treat with insecticidal soap. Our [spider mite treatment guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) has the full routine.
- **Fungus gnats.** Small black flies around the pot are a sign the soil stays wet. Let it dry out thoroughly; the larvae need moist soil.

More on each pest in [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests).

## How do I stop these problems coming back?

Water by the soil, not the calendar. From spring to autumn, wait until the soil is dry nearly to the bottom of the pot, then water until it runs out of the drainage hole and empty the saucer. In winter, NC State's one to two months between waterings is a good starting point; in a cool room, lean toward two. Give it a bright spot, a gritty mix and a pot with a hole. ## Quick answers

**Is a snake plant toxic to cats and dogs?** Yes, mildly. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/snake-plant) lists it as toxic to both because of saponins, which cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea.

**Can a snake plant recover from droopy leaves?** A leaf that has folded or gone soft will not stand back up. If the base is firm, the plant will replace it with new upright leaves once the light or watering is fixed.
