Most African violet problems come from two causes: too much water, which rots the crown and roots, and too little light, which stops the plant flowering. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/saintpaulia-ionantha/) calls overwatering the number one issue. Check those two first before you look for pests or disease, then use the symptom sections below.

## Why are my African violet leaves turning yellow?

The causes, from most to least common:

1. **Old leaves dying off.** The outer, lowest ring of leaves yellows one at a time while the centre stays green and the plant keeps growing. This is normal. Pull or cut each leaf off at the base.
2. **Overwatering.** Several leaves yellow at once, the compost stays wet for days, and the leaves feel limp rather than firm. Let the top of the compost dry before you water again and check the crown, as described in the next section.
3. **Hungry plant.** The [Clemson Extension African violet factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/african-violet/) notes that lower leaves turning yellow while growth slows means the plant needs fertiliser. Feed with an African violet fertiliser at the label rate while the plant is growing.
4. **Too much sun.** Leaves facing the window turn yellow-white or bleached, often with dry brown patches. Move the plant back from direct afternoon sun.

To tell them apart, look at where the yellowing is. Only the oldest outer leaves points to age or hunger. Leaves all around the plant on wet compost points to water. One side only, facing the glass, points to sun.

## Why is my African violet drooping?

Drooping on wet compost is the classic sign of crown and root rot, the most serious African violet problem. Clemson's [African violet diseases and pests factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/african-violet-diseases-insect-pests/) explains that the water moulds behind it (*Pythium* and *Phytophthora*) take hold after overwatering, poor drainage or planting too deep. The older leaves droop, the centre leaves stay small, and young leaves at the heart can blacken and die. The crown and roots feel soft and mushy.

How to tell it from thirst: a dry plant has a light pot and dry compost, and perks up within hours of watering. A rotting plant droops on compost that is already damp and stays limp after you water.

What to do:

1. Take the plant out of its pot and look at the roots. Healthy roots are pale and firm. Rotten ones are brown, soft and slide apart.
2. If only some roots are bad, trim them off, repot into fresh, light African violet mix in a pot no larger than the root ball, with the crown above the compost line, and water only when the surface has dried.
3. If the crown itself is soft, the plant is unlikely to recover. Take a healthy leaf from the outer middle ring as a cutting to start a new plant, and throw the old one away.

Other causes of drooping are cold (the leaves go limp after a night by a cold window) and a pot left to dry right out. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers the repotting steps in more detail.

## Why is my African violet not blooming?

Clemson states plainly that lack of flowering is generally due to inadequate light. The RHS adds that too little light, in intensity or in hours per day, reduces or stops flowering. A plant that is otherwise healthy but has thin, dark green leaves on long, stretched stalks is telling you the same thing.

The fixes, in order:

1. **More light.** Move it to a bright east or west window, or a bright north window, out of direct afternoon sun. In winter it often needs the brightest spot in the house.
2. **A grow light.** Clemson recommends fluorescent tubes 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in) above the plants for 15 to 18 hours a day. Many people use LED grow lights the same way.
3. **A snug pot.** The RHS recommends a pot only slightly larger than the root ball. Violets in big pots put their energy into roots and leaves.
4. **Warmer nights.** NC State notes that plants become stunted below 16°C (60°F) at night.
5. **Food.** A fertiliser for flowering plants, at the label rate, during active growth.

Flower buds that form and then shrivel before opening are a different problem. Clemson lists low temperature, wet compost, dry air, poor air movement and fumes from gas cookers as causes of bud drop.

## Why does my African violet have white or yellow rings on its leaves?

Pale rings, lines or blotches on the upper surface of otherwise healthy leaves are ring spot. Clemson explains that it happens when cold water touches the leaves; the cells just under the surface are damaged and lose their colour. It does not spread and it does not harm the plant, but the marks are permanent.

The fix is in how you water: use water at room temperature and keep it off the leaves. Water from below by standing the pot in about 2.5 cm (1 in) of water until the surface of the compost is moist, then let it drain. Our guide on [how often to water an African violet](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-african-violet) covers bottom watering step by step.

Brown or bleached patches that are dry and crisp, on the side facing the window, are sun scorch rather than ring spot.

## What is the white cotton on my African violet?

White, cottony clumps where the leaves join the stem, along the leaf stalks or on the roots are mealybugs. Clemson describes them as about 6 mm (¼ in) long, white and waxy, and notes that they leave sticky honeydew. They also live in the compost, so check the roots and the bottom of the pot.

1. Isolate the plant from your other violets.
2. Dab each cluster with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol.
3. Follow up with insecticidal soap, repeating as the label directs, because eggs hatch after the first treatment.
4. Check every new plant before it joins the others. Infestations usually arrive on a new plant.

A badly infested plant with mealybugs on the roots is often better replaced from a clean leaf cutting. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers mealybugs on other plants.

## Why are the centre leaves of my African violet tight and hairy?

A crown of small, twisted, very hairy greyish leaves in the centre, with buds that never open, is the sign of cyclamen mites. Clemson describes them as about 0.25 mm (1/100 in) long, too small to see without a hand lens, and notes they favour cool, very humid conditions. The Clemson African violet factsheet calls them incurable once established.

What you can do:

1. Isolate the plant at once. The mites spread to neighbouring plants on hands and touching leaves.
2. Throw away badly infested plants with their compost.
3. Clean pots you want to reuse by soaking them for 30 minutes in 1 part household bleach to 9 parts water.
4. On a lightly affected plant you want to save, Clemson suggests insecticidal soap or sulfur, two or three applications three days apart.

Spacing plants so their leaves do not touch slows the spread.

## Why is there grey fuzz on my African violet?

Grey, fuzzy mould on flowers, leaves or stalks is grey mould (*Botrytis*). Clemson notes that it starts as water-soaked spots, often on the undersides of leaves, and frequently follows mite damage. Remove dead flowers and leaves as they fade, cut away affected parts, keep water off the leaves and give the plant more space and air.

## Why are the leaf stems rotting where they touch the pot?

A rusty brown, sunken spot on a leaf stalk exactly where it rests on the rim of the pot is petiole rot. Clemson explains that fertiliser salts collect on the rim and burn the stalk. Wrap the rim in foil or tape so the stalks rest on a clean surface, remove the damaged leaves, and flush the compost with plenty of plain water to wash out excess salts. ## Quick answers

**Should I mist my African violet?** No. Misting puts water on the hairy leaves, which marks them and invites rot. NC State notes that it does not tolerate dry air, so if your air is very dry, use a humidifier or a pebble tray instead.

**Are African violets safe for cats?** Yes. The ASPCA lists them as non-toxic to cats and dogs.
