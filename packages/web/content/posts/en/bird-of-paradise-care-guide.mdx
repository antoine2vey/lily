Bird of paradise care starts with light: put it in the brightest spot you have, ideally by a large south- or west-facing window where it gets a few hours of direct sun. Keep the soil moist but not soggy in spring and summer, let it dry much more between waterings in winter, and never let the room drop below 10°C (50°F). Indoors it grows as a big foliage plant; it only flowers when it is mature and gets a lot of light.

## Bird of paradise care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | As much as possible. A few hours of direct sun daily, by a large south or west window |
| Water | When the top 5 cm (2 in) is dry in spring and summer; much drier in winter |
| Humidity | Ordinary room humidity. No misting needed |
| Temperature | Normal room warmth by day, 13 to 18°C (55 to 65°F) at night, never below 10°C (50°F) |
| Soil | Peat-free or regular potting compost with a fifth to a quarter perlite or bark, pot with drainage |
| Feeding | Liquid fertiliser every 2 weeks in spring and summer, monthly or none in winter |
| Toxicity | Mildly toxic to cats and dogs, mainly the seeds and fruit |

This guide is about Strelitzia reginae, the orange-and-blue flowered species native to South Africa. The giant white bird of paradise, Strelitzia nicolai, is sold as a large foliage plant and takes the same light, water and temperature care. Be careful with the name: a different shrub, Caesalpinia gilliesii, is also called bird of paradise, and its toxicity and care are different.

## How much light does a bird of paradise need?

More than almost any other common houseplant. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/strelitzia-reginae/) lists full sun to part shade, and the [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/strelitzia/how-to-grow-strelitzia) recommends a bright spot close to a large sunny window. University of Wisconsin Extension says it needs nearly full sun in summer and as much light as possible in winter to bloom.

A practical order of spots, best first:

1. Right by a south-facing window, or a west-facing one, where the sun reaches the leaves for several hours.
2. By an east-facing window with morning sun.
3. A metre or two back from a sunny window. The plant survives here but rarely flowers.

A north-facing room is not enough. In low light the leaf stalks stretch and flop, new leaves come slowly and the plant never flowers.

If you move a plant from a shop or a dim room into direct sun, do it over a couple of weeks, or the leaves can scorch. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to read a window.

## How often to water a bird of paradise

NC State says to water freely in spring and summer and keep it drier in winter. The RHS says the same: moist but never saturated in spring and summer, then from late November allow the compost to get fairly dry between waterings.

In practice:

- **Spring and summer:** water when the top 5 cm (2 in) of compost feels dry. In a sunny window that is often once a week. Water until it drains out of the bottom, then empty the saucer.
- **Autumn and winter:** let the top half of the pot dry before watering. That can be every 2 to 3 weeks in a cool room.

Signs of too much water are yellowing lower leaves, a sour smell from the pot and dark, soft stems at the base. NC State notes that overwatering leads to root rot. Signs of too little are leaves that curl lengthwise along the midrib and crisp brown edges.

## Humidity and temperature

Bird of paradise copes with normal room humidity. Brown leaf edges are more often caused by uneven watering or cold air than by dry air. Misting the leaves does little, as the water evaporates within minutes.

Keep it warm. NC State gives night temperatures of 13 to 18°C (55 to 65°F) and the RHS says never to let it drop below 10 to 12°C (50 to 54°F). Wisconsin notes it can survive a brief dip to about -2°C (28°F) outdoors, but that is damage control, not a growing temperature. Indoors, keep it away from cold draughts in winter and from the direct blast of a radiator.

## Why are bird of paradise leaves split?

Splits along the leaf, running from the edge toward the midrib, are normal. They probably help the big leaves survive wind in the wild. The RHS notes that wind shreds the leaves, and indoors the same thing happens slowly from people brushing past or from an open window. It does not harm the plant. Split leaves never heal, so if the look bothers you, move the plant out of busy walkways and cut off the worst leaves at the base.

## How to get a bird of paradise to flower

Patience and light. The RHS says plants usually flower when they are 5 to 10 years old, and Wisconsin says seed-grown plants take 4 to 7 years. A small plant from a garden centre will not flower for several years whatever you do.

Once it is old enough, these help:

- **Maximum light** in every season, as above.
- **A snug pot.** Wisconsin notes that plants bloom more when they are a little pot-bound. Do not move it into a much bigger pot.
- **Feeding.** Wisconsin and the RHS recommend a liquid feed every 2 weeks in spring and summer; Wisconsin suggests monthly in winter. Use a balanced houseplant fertiliser at the label rate.
- **A cooler, drier winter**, as the RHS describes, followed by more water and feed in spring.

## Soil and repotting

NC State calls for a fertile, well-drained loam. Indoors, a good-quality potting compost with a fifth to a quarter of perlite or fine bark mixed in works well. A pot with drainage holes matters more than the exact recipe.

The RHS suggests repotting every couple of years in spring, into a pot only slightly larger. The roots are thick and fleshy, and big plants are heavy, so get help or cut away a plastic pot rather than pulling the plant out by its stems. Our [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) covers the method.

## How to divide a bird of paradise

NC State lists division as the way to propagate. In spring, when you repot, look for side shoots with their own roots and three or more leaves. Cut them away from the main clump with a clean knife, keeping as many roots as possible, and pot each one up in fresh compost. Divisions flower sooner than seed-grown plants because they are already mature.

## Seasonal care

- **Spring:** repot or divide if needed, start feeding every 2 weeks and increase watering as growth picks up.
- **Summer:** you can put it outside on a sunny, sheltered patio once nights stay above about 13°C (55°F). Acclimatise it over a week or two to avoid scorch, and keep it out of strong wind. Check it often, as it dries quickly in sun.
- **Autumn:** bring it back in before nights fall below 10°C (50°F). Check for pests before it comes indoors.
- **Winter:** give it the brightest window in the house, water much less and feed monthly or not at all. Wisconsin advises against leafshine products; dust the leaves with a damp cloth instead.

## Common bird of paradise problems

- **Yellow lower leaves:** usually too much water, especially in winter.
- **Leaves curling lengthwise:** thirst, or very dry, hot air near a heater.
- **Brown, crisp edges:** uneven watering, cold draughts or a build-up of fertiliser salts.
- **No flowers:** too little light, a young plant or a pot that is too large.
- **Pests:** NC State and the RHS list mealybugs, scale and spider mites. Check where the leaf stalks wrap around each other at the base. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers treatment.

## Is bird of paradise toxic to pets?

The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/bird-paradise-flower) lists Strelitzia reginae as toxic to dogs, cats and horses, with mild nausea, vomiting and drowsiness, mainly from the fruit and seeds. Indoor plants rarely set seed, so the risk is low, but keep it away from pets that chew leaves.
