Most echeveria problems come down to two causes: too much water and too little light. Mushy, translucent or yellow leaves and a soft stem point to overwatering and rot, while a rosette that stretches upward with gaps between the leaves needs more light, ideally a few hours of direct sun a day. Wrinkled leaves on dry soil mean thirst, and white cottony tufts between the leaves are mealybugs.

## Echeveria problems: check water and light first

Echeverias are desert succulents. The [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/echeveria/) entry notes that they come from dry regions from southwest Texas to northwest Argentina, need little water, tolerate full sun, and colour up better with more of it. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/6220/echeveria-agavoides-moulded-wax-succulent/details) advises cactus compost, bright light, moderate watering while the plant is growing, and almost none in winter.

So before anything else, answer three questions. Is the soil wet or dry right now? How many hours of direct sun does the plant get? Does the pot have a drainage hole? Most of the fixes below follow from those answers.

## Why are my echeveria leaves mushy or translucent?

**1. Overwatering.** By far the most common cause. The RHS warns that too much water leads to rot in succulents, and that cold makes rot more likely. Leaves swell, turn soft and see-through, then yellow or black, starting at the base of the rosette. The soil is wet, often days after the last watering.

**2. Water sitting in the rosette.** Water left pooling between tight leaves, especially in cool or still air, can rot the centre. Water the soil, not the rosette.

**3. A pot without drainage, or a dense mix.** Ordinary potting compost holds water around the roots for too long. A cachepot that collects runoff does the same.

To tell them apart: if the soft leaves are at the bottom and the soil is wet, the cause is the watering. If the rot starts in the centre and the lower leaves are firm, suspect water sitting in the rosette.

The fix: stop watering. Pull off any mushy leaves. Check the stem and roots as described in the root rot section below. Then water only when the mix is completely dry. The [echeveria watering guide](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-echeveria) gives intervals by season.

## Why is my echeveria stretching?

Low light. In too little light, the stem lengthens, the gaps between leaves grow, the rosette loosens and leans toward the window, and the leaves often turn pale green. The RHS notes that most succulents are sun-lovers and are not happy in poor light away from windows.

Move the plant to your sunniest window, usually south- or west-facing. Increase direct sun gradually over a week or two so the leaves do not scorch. Stretched growth will not shrink back. Once the plant is in good light, you can cut off the rosette with a short stem, let the cut dry for a few days, and set it on dry, gritty mix to root. NC State lists this, along with offsets and single leaves, as a way to propagate echeverias. The [cuttings guide](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers rooting in general, and the [light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a window.

## Why are my echeveria leaves wrinkled or shrivelling?

**1. Underwatering.** The RHS notes that too little water makes succulents shrivel. If the soil is bone dry and the leaves feel thin and wrinkled, give the pot a thorough soak until water runs from the bottom. Leaves usually plump up within a few days.

**2. Root rot or damaged roots.** If the leaves wrinkle while the soil is wet, the roots may have rotted and can no longer take up water. Watering more will make it worse. Check the roots.

**3. Root pests.** The RHS notes that vine weevil grubs eat roots, and Clemson notes that some aphids feed on roots. Either leaves the plant thirsty even with water in the pot. See the pests section below.

**4. Old lower leaves.** A few bottom leaves shrivelling and drying to a papery brown while the centre stays plump and keeps growing is normal. Pull them off gently once they are dry.

To tell them apart, check the soil first. Dry soil with wrinkled leaves all over the rosette means water. Wet soil with wrinkled leaves means roots. Only the lowest leaves wrinkling means age.

## Why does my echeveria have brown or white patches?

Sunburn. Pale, bleached or brown dry patches on the leaves facing the light, after a move to a sunnier spot or outdoors, are scorch. Clemson Extension notes in its [houseplant pest factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/common-houseplant-insects-related-pests/) that even plants that grow well in sun can burn when moved outdoors before they adapt, and advises starting them in shade. Scorched patches do not heal, but new leaves will be clean. Next time, increase sun over one to two weeks.

Do not confuse scorch with the natural powdery coating, called farina, on many echeverias. It rubs off as finger marks when you handle the leaves. That is cosmetic. Hold plants by the pot, not by the leaves.

## Why is my echeveria losing its colour?

Pink, red or purple edges fade toward plain green or grey in low light. NC State notes that leaf colour improves with sunlight. Give the plant more direct sun and the new leaves will come in brighter.

## Does my echeveria have root rot?

Signs: leaves that turn mushy or drop when touched, a soft or black stem at the soil line, wrinkling in wet soil, and a sour smell. Slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and pale. Rotten roots are brown or black and soft.

To save it:

1. Remove all the old soil and cut away every soft root and any dark tissue at the base of the stem with a clean blade.
2. If the stem is rotten, cut above the rot into clean, firm tissue. Pull off a few healthy leaves too, as a backup.
3. Let the cut surface dry and callus for a few days in a shaded spot.
4. Set it on dry, gritty cactus mix in a pot with a drainage hole. Wait about a week before the first light watering.

Healthy leaves laid on dry mix can each form a new rosette, as NC State describes. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) covers rot in other plants.

## What pests attack echeveria?

The RHS lists mealybugs, vine weevil and aphids. NC State reports no significant problems, so pests are less frequent than watering trouble, but they do happen.

- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony tufts tucked between leaves and at the base of the rosette. Clemson describes them as small pale insects that cover themselves in white wax, which helps them resist sprays. Dab them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol. Clemson adds that if pests have reached the roots, the surest fix is to take a cutting and start a new plant in a clean pot with fresh mix.
- **Vine weevil.** The RHS says the grubs do the most damage, eating roots over autumn and winter, which can make plants wilt and die. Plants in containers are the most at risk. The grubs are white, C-shaped and legless, up to 10 mm (about ⅜ in) long, with brown heads. Adults notch leaf edges in summer. A plant that collapses for no clear reason, with roots missing, is worth checking for grubs.
- **Aphids.** Small soft insects on flower stalks and new growth. A spray of water or a swab of alcohol removes small numbers.

Clemson advises isolating new plants for six weeks and checking them regularly before they join the rest. The [common pests guide](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers each pest in more detail.

## Quick answers

**Is echeveria toxic to pets?** NC State tags echeveria as non-toxic for cats, dogs and horses.

**Why is my echeveria growing a tall stalk?** If the stalk carries buds and the rosette stays tight, it is flowering. NC State notes that rosettes produce one or more flower stalks once a year. If the whole rosette is stretching, it needs more light.
