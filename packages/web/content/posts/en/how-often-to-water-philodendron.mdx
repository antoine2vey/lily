How often to water philodendron comes down to the soil, not the calendar: water when the top 2.5 cm (1 in) is dry, which in a bright, warm room often means every 7 to 10 days in spring and summer and every 2 to 3 weeks in winter. Water until it runs from the drainage hole, then empty the saucer. Most philodendron trouble comes from watering too often, not too little.

## How to tell when a philodendron needs water

[Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/philodendron/) asks for soil kept evenly moist but not soggy, and warns never to let the plant stand in water. The [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/philodendron-hederaceum/) entry for the heartleaf philodendron says the same in fewer words: keep the soil slightly moist and slow down in winter.

In practice that means checking before you water.

- **Finger test.** Clemson's [watering factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) calls touch the most accurate gauge. Push a finger into the mix up to the first joint, about 2.5 cm (1 in). Dry at the fingertip means water. Moist means wait and check again in two or three days.
- **Pot weight.** Lift the pot right after watering and again when it is dry. After a few rounds, the difference tells you more than any schedule.
- **Clay pots.** As a clay pot dries, the mix shrinks away from the sides. Clemson notes that tapping a dry clay pot gives a hollow sound.

A moisture meter can help in a large pot where your finger cannot reach far, but read it as a second opinion.

## How often to water philodendron by season

These are starting points for a philodendron in a plastic pot with drainage, in bright indirect light. Your own plant will settle into its own rhythm within a few weeks of checking.

| Season | Typical interval | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Every 7 to 10 days | Growth picks up with longer days. |
| Summer | Every 5 to 10 days | Warmth and light dry the mix fastest. |
| Autumn | Every 10 to 14 days | Growth slows as days shorten. |
| Winter | Every 2 to 3 weeks | Little growth, so the mix stays wet longer. |

Clemson notes that a plant uses less water while it rests, and NC State advises watering less in winter. A plant near a radiator may still dry quickly in winter, which is why the finger test beats the table.

## What changes how often you water

**Light.** A philodendron in bright indirect light grows and drinks more than one in a dim corner. Clemson says most philodendrons prefer indirect or curtain-filtered light and tolerate low light, and the heartleaf type tolerates very low light. In low light, the soil can stay wet for weeks, so water much less often.

**Pot material and size.** Water evaporates from the sides of porous clay, so clay pots need water more often than plastic or glazed ones. A large plant in a small pot also dries faster than a small plant in a large pot. An oversized pot holds a lot of wet mix around few roots, a common route to rot.

**Soil.** Clemson traces root rot to a mix that does not drain quickly or to watering too often. A standard potting mix lightened with perlite or bark drains well. The [soil guide](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) covers the options.

**Temperature.** Clemson's ideal is 18 to 21 °C (65 to 70 °F) at night and 24 to 29 °C (75 to 85 °F) by day. Warmer rooms dry the mix faster, cool rooms slow it down.

**Climbers and large plants.** A big self-heading philodendron or a climber on a moss pole has more leaves to supply than a small trailing heartleaf, and usually needs more water per session.

## How to water a philodendron

Water slowly over the whole surface of the mix until water runs from the drainage hole. Clemson's watering factsheet explains why: a thorough watering reaches the bottom two-thirds of the pot, where most roots are. Let it drain, then empty the saucer or cachepot so the pot never sits in water.

Use water at room temperature. Clemson suggests 17 to 22 °C (62 to 72 °F) and advises against softened water, which adds sodium to the mix. If the mix has dried so hard that water runs straight through, water once, wait half an hour, and water again, or set the pot in a basin until the surface is moist and then let it drain.

## Signs of overwatering

- **Yellow lower leaves.** Clemson lists yellowing lower leaves and dying growing tips as signs of overwatering or too little light. The two often go together, since a plant in low light uses less water.
- **Wilting in wet soil.** Rotting roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts as if dry. Clemson warns that watering at this stage makes it worse.
- **Mushy stem base and a sour smell.** These point to root rot, which NC State says can occur in overly wet soil.
- **Soil that never dries.** If the top of the mix is still damp a week after watering in summer, the pot is too large, the mix too dense, or the light too low.

Stop watering and let the top few centimetres dry. If the plant still droops, slide it out and check the roots. Healthy roots are firm and pale. Brown, mushy roots need cutting out and a repot into fresh, free-draining mix. The [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) has the full steps.

## Signs of underwatering

- Leaves droop and feel soft, but the soil is dry and the pot is light.
- Leaf edges or tips turn brown and crisp.
- Older leaves yellow and drop after a long dry spell.

A thorough watering usually lifts drooping leaves within a day. If it keeps happening, check more often rather than adding more water each time. ## Quick answers

**Should I mist my philodendron?** It is not required. Clemson says philodendrons prefer high humidity but tolerate the lower humidity of most homes.

**Is tap water fine?** Usually, if it is not softened. Let cold tap water warm to room temperature first.
