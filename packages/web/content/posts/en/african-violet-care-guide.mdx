African violet care rests on bright light and careful watering. Place it within about 1 m (3 ft) of a large east, south or west window, out of direct afternoon sun, or 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in) under grow lights for 15 to 18 hours a day. Water with lukewarm water when the surface feels dry, keep water off the leaves, and never let the pot sit in water. With enough light, a healthy plant flowers several times a year, each flush lasting 3 to 6 weeks.

The African violet, long called *Saintpaulia ionantha* and now classified as *Streptocarpus ionanthus*, comes from eastern Tanzania. It stays small, about 15 to 23 cm (6 to 9 in) across, according to [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/saintpaulia-ionantha/). The ASPCA lists it as [non-toxic to cats and dogs](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/african-violet).

## How much light does an African violet need?

More than most people give it. A violet that grows leaves but no flowers almost always needs more light. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/african-violet/) puts it in a bright spot within 1 m (3 ft) of a large south, east or west window, without direct afternoon sun. In practice:

- **East window.** Usually the best spot. Gentle morning sun, bright the rest of the day.
- **South or west window.** Bright enough, but set the plant back from the glass or behind a sheer curtain so afternoon sun does not scorch the leaves.
- **North window.** Often too dim for good flowering, especially in winter. Put it right at the glass, or add a grow light.

Clemson describes the two signs of too little light. Leaves turn thin and dark green on long leaf stalks, and flowering slows or stops. Leaves that reach upward are another hint. Too much light shows the opposite way: pale, yellowish leaves bunched tightly in the centre, sometimes with bleached or brown patches.

### Growing African violets under lights

African violets grow and flower well under artificial light, which is why so many collectors grow them on shelves. Clemson's figures: place plants 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in) below the tubes and keep the lights on 15 to 18 hours a day. Those figures were written for fluorescent tubes. Modern LED shop lights and grow bars work the same way. Start at the higher end of the distance range and move the plants closer if leaves reach up or flowering is sparse. A plug-in timer makes the schedule automatic.

Plants still need a dark period every day. Do not leave lights on around the clock.

## How to water an African violet

Clemson calls overwatering one of the most common problems. African violets are very prone to crown and root rot, and a plant that sits in wet soil collapses quickly. Water when the surface feels dry, and let the excess drain away.

You can water from the top or from below.

- **From the top.** Water the soil, not the leaves, until water runs out of the drainage hole. Empty the saucer.
- **From below.** Clemson suggests setting the pot in a tray with about 2.5 cm (1 in) of water and taking it out once the surface of the soil is moist. Do not leave it soaking for hours.

Use lukewarm water. Clemson explains that cold water touching the leaves leaves white blotches and yellow or white ring patterns on them. NC State adds that the plant is intolerant of very cold water, and recommends watering from below to keep the leaves dry.

As a starting point, that often means every 5 to 10 days, depending on the pot and the room. The [African violet watering guide](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-african-violet) goes through the intervals season by season.

## Humidity and temperature

NC State says African violets prefer high humidity, and suggests setting the pot on a tray of wet pebbles or running a humidifier, since they do not like dry air. The pebble tray only works if the base of the pot sits above the water line. The [humidity guide](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

Temperature is easy indoors. Clemson's range is 18 to 21 °C (65 to 70 °F) at night and up to 27 to 29 °C (80 to 85 °F) by day. Most homes fall within that. Keep plants off cold windowsills on winter nights and away from heating vents, and avoid sudden swings.

## Soil and pots for African violets

The mix needs to drain fast and hold some moisture. Clemson suggests a mix of equal parts potting soil, peat and perlite. Ready-made African violet mixes are similar. Heavy, dense mixes stay wet and invite crown rot.

Always use a pot with drainage holes. Clemson warns never to use pots without them. Clay pots dry faster than plastic, so plants in clay need water more often. Clay has another drawback specific to violets: leaf stalks resting on the salt-crusted rim of a clay pot can be killed. If you use clay, cover the rim with foil or switch to plastic.

African violets have small root systems and are usually grown in small, shallow pots. Repotting into a much larger pot leaves a lot of wet soil around few roots.

## How to make an African violet flower

If your violet has healthy leaves but no flowers, work through this list in order:

1. **Light.** The first fix in almost every case. Move it to an east window or under a light for 15 to 18 hours a day.
2. **Feeding.** Clemson notes that yellowing older leaves and slower growth usually mean the plant needs fertiliser. Use a water-soluble fertiliser made for African violets at the label rate, and water the soil first so the fertiliser does not hit dry roots.
3. **Crowding.** Side shoots, called suckers, form new crowns beside the main one. A plant with several crowns crowds itself and flowers less. Pinch suckers out while small.
4. **Old plants with a long bare neck.** Over time the lower leaves fall and the crown sits on a bare stem above the soil. Repot deeper, or start over from a leaf cutting.

With good light and regular feeding, African violets can flower on and off year-round. Clemson notes each set of flowers lasts 3 to 6 weeks.

## How to propagate an African violet from a leaf

NC State lists leaf cuttings as the recommended method.

1. Choose a healthy, mature leaf from the middle row, not the oldest outer leaves or the tiny centre ones.
2. Cut it with 2.5 to 4 cm (1 to 1.5 in) of stalk.
3. Push the stalk into moist, light mix at a slight angle, about 1 cm (½ in) deep.
4. Cover loosely with a clear bag or container to keep the air humid, and keep it in bright, indirect light.

Small plantlets appear at the base of the stalk after several weeks. Separate them once they have a few leaves of their own. The [cuttings guide](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers rooting in general.

## Common African violet problems

The most frequent problems, briefly. The [African violet problems page](/en/blog/african-violet-problems) covers each in detail.

- **Mushy crown and collapsing leaves.** Crown or root rot from overwatering. Hard to save. Root healthy leaves as cuttings.
- **White or yellow rings on leaves.** Cold water on the leaves. The marks stay, but new leaves will be clean.
- **No flowers.** Usually too little light.
- **Tight, crinkled, hairy centre leaves.** Clemson warns this may be cyclamen mite, which cannot be cured. Discard the plant and keep it away from your other violets.
- **White cottony tufts.** Mealybugs. Clemson lists them as another common pest.

Clemson also notes that pests and diseases spread easily between violets, so keep a new plant apart from the others for a few weeks.

## Quick answers

**Should I mist an African violet?** No. Water on the hairy leaves can leave spots. Raise humidity with a pebble tray or humidifier instead.

**Can I cut off a damaged leaf?** Yes. Snap or cut it off at the base of the stalk. Removing old outer leaves also tidies the rosette.
