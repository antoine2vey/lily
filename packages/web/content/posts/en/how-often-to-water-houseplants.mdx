How often to water houseplants has no single answer, but most leafy tropical houseplants need water when the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of soil is dry. In a warm, bright room that is often about once a week in summer and every 2 to 3 weeks in winter. Succulents and cacti go much longer, while ferns and herbs need it more often. Check the soil first and water only when the plant needs it, never on a fixed day.

## How to tell when a houseplant needs water

[Clemson Cooperative Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) is blunt about it: there is no set schedule, so water when the plant needs it. These checks tell you when that is.

- **Finger test.** Push a finger into the soil. Clemson suggests going to the first knuckle; [University of Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/ilriverhort/2018-01-28-how-water-houseplants) suggests about 5 cm (2 in). If it feels dry at that depth, most foliage plants are ready.
- **Weight.** Lift the pot just after watering and again a few days later. A dry pot is clearly lighter, and after a few weeks you can judge it in one lift.
- **Colour.** Most potting mixes turn paler as they dry.
- **Sound.** Clemson notes that a clay pot sounds hollow when you tap it and the soil inside is dry.
- **Moisture meter.** Useful for deep pots, but Clemson warns their accuracy varies. Treat the reading as a hint and check with your finger.

## How often to water houseplants, by type of plant

These are starting intervals for a room at 18 to 24°C (65 to 75°F), in pots with drainage holes. Your own plants will settle on their own rhythm.

| Plant type | Water when | Summer | Winter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leafy tropicals (monstera, pothos, philodendron) | Top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) dry | About weekly | Every 2 to 3 weeks |
| Moisture lovers (ferns, peace lily, basil) | Surface just dry | Every 3 to 7 days | Every 7 to 10 days |
| Drought-tolerant (snake plant, ZZ plant) | Soil dry most of the way down | Every 2 to 3 weeks | Every 4 to 6 weeks |
| Succulents and cacti | Soil completely dry | Every 1 to 2 weeks | Every 3 to 6 weeks, or less |

Drought tolerance comes from water storage. Succulents hold water in their leaves, ZZ plants in thick underground rhizomes, cacti in their stems. Those plants survive a missed watering easily but rot quickly in soil that stays wet. Thin-leaved plants like ferns and peace lilies have no reserve, so they wilt fast when dry.

## What changes how often you water

Clemson lists the factors that change how fast a pot dries:

- **Light and warmth.** A plant in a warm, sunny spot uses far more water than the same plant in a cool, dim corner.
- **Pot material.** Unglazed clay loses water through its walls and dries faster than plastic or glazed ceramic.
- **Pot size.** A large plant in a small pot runs out of water quickly. A small plant in a big pot stays wet for a long time, which is a root rot risk. Our guide on [how to choose a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) explains sizing.
- **Soil.** Mixes high in peat hold more water than mixes with lots of bark, sand or perlite.
- **Growth stage.** Clemson notes that plants need less water after a flush of growth, after flowering and while dormant.

**Season matters most.** In winter, shorter days mean less light, so plants grow slowly and use less water, even in a heated room. That is why a schedule that works in July can drown the same plant in December. Our guide to [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers the switch. [Lily](/en) builds a watering schedule per plant and stretches or shortens each interval with the season's daylight, so you do not have to recalculate every plant twice a year.

## How to water a houseplant properly

1. **Water thoroughly.** Pour slowly over the whole soil surface until water runs out of the drainage hole. Small sips wet only the top layer and leave the lower roots dry.
2. **Empty the saucer.** Clemson recommends not leaving the pot standing in drained water. Tip it out after 10 to 15 minutes, including any water inside a decorative cachepot.
3. **Rewet very dry soil gently.** Dry peat-based mix shrinks and repels water, which then runs straight down the gap at the edge of the pot. Clemson suggests watering twice, about 30 minutes apart, or soaking the pot in a basin of water until bubbles stop rising.
4. **Use room-temperature water.** Clemson recommends water at about 17 to 22°C (62 to 72°F). Cold water can shock tropical roots.
5. **Avoid softened water.** Clemson advises against water from a water softener, because of the sodium it adds. Illinois Extension suggests letting tap water stand for 24 hours if you are concerned about chlorine.

**Bottom watering** is a good alternative for plants that dislike wet leaves or crowns, such as African violets. Stand the pot in a few centimetres of water for 15 to 30 minutes until the surface feels damp, then let it drain. Water from the top every few weeks to flush out fertiliser salts.

## Signs of overwatering

Both Clemson and Illinois name overwatering as the most common way houseplants die. Roots need air as well as water, and soil that stays wet suffocates them until they rot. Signs include:

- Soil that is still wet several days after watering.
- Yellowing lower leaves, often soft rather than crisp.
- Wilting even though the soil is wet. Clemson notes this can mean the roots have rotted and can no longer take up water.
- A sour or musty smell, dark mushy roots and fungus gnats around the pot.

If you see these, stop watering, let the soil dry and check the roots. Our [root rot recovery](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) guide shows how to save the plant.

## Signs of underwatering

- Soil pulling away from the side of the pot.
- Leaves that droop and then recover within hours of watering.
- Dry, crisp brown edges or tips, and older leaves dropping.
- A pot that feels very light.

Underwatering is easier to fix: soak the pot thoroughly, as in step 3. If you are not sure which problem you have, our comparison of [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) goes through the differences side by side.

## Quick answers

**Is it better to water in the morning or the evening?** For indoor plants it matters little. Morning watering gives wet leaves time to dry during the day, which is a small advantage.

**What if I go away for a week or two?** Water thoroughly the day before, move plants away from hot windows and group them together. Our guide to [watering plants while on vacation](/en/blog/how-to-water-plants-while-on-vacation) covers wicks and other options.
