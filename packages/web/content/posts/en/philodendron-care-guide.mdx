Philodendron care is mostly about light and water: bright, indirect light, soil kept evenly moist but never soggy, and a warm room between 18 and 29°C (65 and 85°F). Most philodendrons tolerate lower light than other tropical houseplants, and the heartleaf philodendron grows even in a dim room. The usual problems, yellow lower leaves and dying tips, come from too little light or too much water.

## Philodendron care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect or curtain-filtered light. Heartleaf types tolerate a north window |
| Water | Keep the soil evenly moist. Water when the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) feels dry, never leave it standing in water |
| Humidity | Normal home humidity (30 to 50%) works; higher gives bigger leaves |
| Temperature | 18 to 21°C (65 to 70°F) at night, 24 to 29°C (75 to 85°F) by day. No cold drafts |
| Soil | Peat- or coir-based potting mix rich in organic matter, with bark or perlite for drainage |
| Feeding | Dilute liquid fertiliser, about once a month from spring to late summer |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats, dogs and horses (insoluble calcium oxalate crystals) |

The light, water and temperature figures come from [Clemson's philodendron factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/philodendron-pothos-monstera/) and [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/philodendron/).

## Climbing or self-heading: which philodendron do you have?

Philodendrons come in two growth habits, and the difference changes how you care for them.

**Climbing (vining) types** grow long stems with aerial roots that grab onto bark or a pole. The heartleaf philodendron (*Philodendron hederaceum*, sold also as *P. scandens*), 'Brasil', *P. erubescens* and its hybrids such as 'Pink Princess' belong here. They trail from a shelf or climb a support.

**Self-heading types** grow upright from a very short stem, with leaves held in a rosette. The tree philodendron (*P. bipinnatifidum*, often sold as 'Selloum'), 'Xanadu', 'Birkin' and 'Moonlight' belong here. They get wide rather than long. Clemson warns that the tree philodendron's leaves can reach 90 cm (3 ft) and that its long leaf stalks are brittle, so give it space out of walkways.

Philodendron is often confused with pothos. Clemson's tell: philodendron leaf stalks are not grooved and each new leaf emerges from a papery sheath that later dries and falls off, while pothos leaves have a grooved stalk and no sheath. The monstera, sometimes called "split-leaf philodendron", is a close relative but not a philodendron.

## How much light does a philodendron need?

Clemson puts it simply: most philodendrons prefer indirect or curtain-filtered sunlight and will tolerate low light. NC State adds that direct sun can scorch the leaves. In practice:

- **Best spot:** within a metre or two (a few feet) of an east window, or set back from a south or west window, or behind a sheer curtain.
- **Low light:** the heartleaf philodendron grows under room lighting or near a north window. It grows slower there, with smaller leaves and longer gaps between them.
- **Too little light:** long bare stems, small leaves, and lower leaves that yellow and drop. Coloured varieties such as 'Prince of Orange' or 'Pink Princess' fade or revert to green.
- **Too much sun:** pale, bleached or brown, crispy patches on the leaves facing the window.

## How often should you water a philodendron?

Clemson's advice is to water often enough to keep the soil evenly moist but not soggy, and never to let the plant stand in water. NC State notes that overwatering causes root rot. A workable routine:

1. Push a finger into the soil. When the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) is dry, water.
2. Water slowly until it runs out of the drainage hole.
3. Empty the saucer or cachepot after 15 to 30 minutes.

In a bright, warm room in summer that may mean once a week. In winter, in a cooler room with shorter days, the same plant may go two weeks or longer. ## Humidity and temperature

Clemson notes that high humidity is ideal for best growth but that philodendrons tolerate the low humidity in most homes. If your heating dries the air to well under 30% in winter and new leaves come in crisp at the edges, a humidifier or a group of plants nearby helps more than misting, which wets the leaves for a few minutes and does little else. NC State advises keeping the leaves dry to prevent leaf spot.

The ideal range is 18 to 21°C (65 to 70°F) at night and 24 to 29°C (75 to 85°F) by day. NC State calls philodendrons very sensitive to cold. Keep them away from cold windows in winter, from doors that open onto the street, and from air-conditioning vents.

## What soil and pot does a philodendron need?

NC State recommends moist, well-drained soil that is high in organic matter. A general-purpose potting mix works, as Clemson notes. Mixing in a third bark chips or perlite makes it drain faster, which matters if you tend to overwater or the plant sits in a cooler spot. Always use a pot with a drainage hole.

Clemson says you can repot an overcrowded plant in any season. Spring and early summer are still the easiest time, because the plant is growing fast and recovers quickly. Go up only 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) in diameter: a pot much larger than the roots holds water the roots cannot use. Signs it is time: roots circling the bottom or coming out of the drainage hole, and water running straight through. See [our soil guide](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) for mix recipes.

## How should you feed a philodendron?

Clemson recommends feeding regularly with a dilute water-soluble houseplant fertiliser, or using a slow-release one. About once a month at half the label rate, from spring to late summer, is a sensible starting point. Stop in winter, when growth slows. Too much fertiliser makes the leaf tips curl and brown, according to Clemson; if you see that, flush the pot with plenty of plain water and feed less often.

## How do you prune a philodendron?

Climbing philodendrons need the most pruning, because their stems keep getting longer. Clemson notes that vining types are kept in check by the height of their support and by training and pruning.

- **When:** spring and summer, when the plant regrows quickly. Removing a yellow or damaged leaf can happen any time.
- **Where:** cut just above a node, the bump on the stem where a leaf joins and aerial roots form. New growth comes from the node below the cut.
- **How much:** you can cut long, bare stems back hard. Leave at least a couple of leaves on each stem.
- **Tools:** clean, sharp scissors or pruners.

Self-heading philodendrons need little pruning. Cut old or damaged leaves off at the base of the leaf stalk.

Every piece you cut from a climbing type can become a new plant. A stem section with one or two nodes roots in a glass of water or in moist mix within a few weeks. Clemson also lists air layering, and some philodendrons make offsets at the base that you can separate when you repot. Our [guide to propagating from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers the steps.

## Does a philodendron need a moss pole?

A climbing philodendron grows fine trailing from a shelf, but its leaves stay small. Clemson notes that some climbing philodendrons do not reach their full leaf size unless trained to grow upward. A moss pole or a bark-covered board gives the aerial roots something to hold, and each new leaf tends to come in larger than the last. Tie the stem loosely to the pole with soft ties until the aerial roots attach. Our guide on [how to make a moss pole](/en/blog/how-to-make-a-moss-pole) shows how to build one.

Self-heading types do not climb and do not need a pole.

## Common philodendron problems

Clemson lists too much or too little water, insects and mites as the main problems, and says diseases are rare indoors.

- **Yellow lower leaves and dying growth tips:** too little light or overwatering. Check the soil first.
- **Brown, curled leaf tips:** too much fertiliser, or very dry soil between waterings.
- **Leggy stems with small leaves:** not enough light.
- **Pests:** aphids, mealybugs, scale, thrips and spider mites. Check the undersides of leaves and the leaf joints when you water.

## Is philodendron toxic to pets?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/heartleaf-philodendron) lists the heartleaf philodendron as toxic to cats, dogs and horses. The insoluble calcium oxalate crystals cause mouth irritation, pain and swelling of the mouth, tongue and lips, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. Keep trailing stems out of reach, especially from cats that chew leaves.
