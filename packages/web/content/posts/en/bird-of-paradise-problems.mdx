Most bird of paradise problems come down to light, water and humidity. Split leaves are largely normal for the plant, leaves that curl inward usually mean thirst or dry air, yellow lower leaves with wet soil point to overwatering, and a plant that never flowers is almost always short of direct sun. Diseases are rare indoors; scale and mealybugs are the pests to check for.

## Bird of paradise problems: what to check first

[Clemson Extension's bird of paradise factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/bird-of-paradise/) says plant diseases are very rarely a problem in the home, and that too much or too little water, plus insects and mites, are the main issues. Before you change anything, look at:

1. **The soil.** Clemson's advice is to wet the mix thoroughly, then let it dry slightly before watering again, and to water less in the colder, darker months. Is the soil soggy, slightly dry, or dry all the way down?
2. **The light.** Clemson says the plant prefers full sun and tolerates lower light such as a south-facing window. Many indoor plants sit far from any window.
3. **The leaf undersides and stem bases.** Look for brown bumps, white fluff or fine speckling.

Two species are sold under the name. The orange-flowered *Strelitzia reginae* grows to about 1.5 m (5 ft). The large plants sold for living rooms are often the white-flowered *Strelitzia nicolai*, which Clemson calls the giant bird of paradise and which grows into a tree outdoors. Both have the same leaf problems.

## Why are my bird of paradise leaves splitting?

**1. It is normal.** The leaf blades of a bird of paradise tear along the side veins as they age. The split runs from the edge toward the midrib and the leaf stays green on both sides. An outdoor plant in wind ends up with leaves torn into strips, and that is how the plant is usually seen in its native South Africa. The splits do not harm the plant and do not need treatment.

**2. Physical damage.** Leaves brushed by people, pets or a door split faster. Move the plant somewhere it is not knocked.

**3. Dry air and dry soil.** Splits seem to appear sooner when new leaves unroll in very dry air or from a thirsty plant. This link is often repeated by growers but we found no extension source that measures it, so treat it as a likely contributor rather than a rule.

You cannot repair a split leaf. If a leaf becomes too ragged for your taste, cut the whole stalk off at the base. New leaves come from the centre of each fan.

## Why are my bird of paradise leaves curling?

**1. Thirst.** The leaf halves fold up toward the midrib when the plant is short of water. Check the soil: if it is dry well below the surface, water thoroughly until water runs out of the pot. The leaves usually open again within a day or two.

**2. Dry air.** Clemson says the plant prefers moderate humidity, around 60%, which is well above most heated homes in winter. Leaves that curl even though the soil is moist often point to dry air. A humidifier nearby is the most effective fix. Clemson suggests daily misting in dry winter months, but misting raises humidity only briefly. Our [humidity guide](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the options.

**3. Root damage.** Curling with soggy soil can mean the roots have rotted and cannot take up water. See the drooping section below.

## Why does my bird of paradise have brown edges or tips?

**1. Dry air or underwatering.** Brown, crisp edges on a plant whose soil dries out completely, or in a dry heated room.

**2. Salt buildup.** Fertiliser and hard tap water leave salts in the pot that burn leaf edges. A white crust on the soil or the pot rim is the clue. Water heavily enough that some runs out every time, and flush the pot with plenty of water every few months.

**3. Too much fertiliser.** Clemson recommends feeding every 2 weeks in spring and weekly in summer, and none in autumn or winter. Feeding through the winter, or at full strength more often than that, leads to salt damage. Follow the dilution on the label.

**4. Cold.** Brown patches that appear a few days after a cold night by a window or a door. Clemson gives 18 to 21°C (65 to 70°F) by day and 10 to 13°C (50 to 55°F) at night as preferred, and says to bring outdoor plants in before temperatures drop below about 16°C (60°F).

## Why are my bird of paradise leaves turning yellow?

**1. Old leaves dying back.** One outer, lower leaf yellowing at a time while new leaves keep coming from the centre is normal. Cut it off at the base once it is mostly yellow.

**2. Overwatering.** Several leaves yellowing at once, with soil that stays wet for days. Clemson notes that root rot usually comes from a mix that does not drain quickly or from watering too often. Let the soil dry more between waterings and empty the saucer.

**3. Too little light.** Pale, yellowish growth with long, weak stalks that flop. Move the plant closer to a bright window. Our [light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot.

**4. Hunger.** In a plant that has not been fed or repotted for years, older leaves turn evenly pale yellow. Feed in spring and summer as above.

## Why does my bird of paradise have brown or yellow spots?

**1. Leaf spot.** [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/strelitzia-reginae/) notes that leaf spot may cause yellow or brown spots on the leaves. Remove badly spotted leaves, water the soil rather than the foliage, and give the plant good air circulation.

**2. Sunburn.** Pale, bleached or brown dry patches on the side facing the glass after a move into stronger sun. Clemson recommends full sun for this plant, but a plant moved from a dim room or from a shop needs a week or two of gradual exposure first. The burned patches stay; new leaves will cope.

**3. Pests.** Fine pale speckling all over a leaf is the sign of spider mites rather than disease.

## Why is my bird of paradise drooping?

**1. Thirst.** Stalks that lean and leaves that curl, with dry soil. Water thoroughly.

**2. Root rot.** Drooping with wet soil, stalks soft at the base, and a sour smell. NC State lists root rot from overwatering or poorly drained soil as a problem for this plant. Unpot it: healthy roots and rhizomes are firm and pale, rotten ones are dark and mushy. Cut away the rot, repot in fresh, free-draining mix, and water less often. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) has the steps, and our [overwatering vs underwatering guide](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) helps you tell the two apart.

**3. Not enough light.** Long stalks with nothing to lean toward will flop. Staking hides it, but more light fixes it.

## Why is my bird of paradise not flowering?

**1. Not enough sun.** This is the most common reason indoors. Clemson says the plant prefers full sun, and NC State calls for a well-lit, sunny spot. A plant a few metres from a window produces leaves but no flowers. Give it the sunniest window you have, or a summer outdoors in sun once nights stay above 16°C (60°F).

**2. Too young.** Clemson says a divided plant should flower in two to three years, while a plant grown from seed can take four to 10 years or more. Many young plants bought in shops are not yet old enough.

**3. Too much fertiliser.** Clemson warns that overfertilisation leads to plenty of foliage and little or no flowering.

**4. Too much room.** The [University of Florida](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/FP563) notes that plants seem to flower more freely when crowded and are best left undisturbed. Do not move a flowering-age plant into a much bigger pot, and divide it only when it has to be.

**5. Warm nights all year.** Clemson's preferred night temperature of 10 to 13°C (50 to 55°F) is cooler than most living rooms, and flowers open from late winter into spring. No source we read ties cool nights directly to flowering, so try it only once light is sorted.

## What pests attack a bird of paradise?

Clemson lists scale, mealybugs, whiteflies and aphids; NC State adds spider mites, and UF names scale as the main pest.

- **Scale.** Brown or tan bumps on stalks and along the leaf midribs, often with sticky honeydew below. Scrape them off and treat repeatedly.
- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony clusters deep in the leaf bases at the centre of each fan. Dab them with rubbing alcohol on a cotton swab, then follow up with insecticidal soap.
- **Spider mites.** Fine speckling and faint webbing on the leaf undersides, mostly in warm, dry rooms.

Our [common houseplant pests guide](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers how to identify and treat each one.

## Is a bird of paradise toxic to pets?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/bird-paradise-flower) lists bird of paradise flower (*Strelitzia reginae*) as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. It causes mild nausea, vomiting and drowsiness, mainly from the fruit and seeds.

## How to prevent bird of paradise problems

- The sunniest window in the house, or full sun outdoors in summer.
- Water thoroughly, let the mix dry slightly, and water less in the colder, darker months.
- Feed every 2 weeks in spring and weekly in summer at the label dilution, and not at all in autumn and winter.
- Humidity as close to 60% as you can manage in winter.
- Keep it in a pot that is snug rather than roomy.
