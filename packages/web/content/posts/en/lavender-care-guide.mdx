Lavender care is mostly about what you leave out. Give it full sun and free-draining, rather poor soil, water it regularly only in its first summer, and trim it every year straight after flowering. Established lavender in the ground rarely needs watering at all. Most lavender that dies is killed by wet, heavy soil in winter, or by being cut back into bare wood.

## Lavender care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Full sun, as many hours of direct sun as possible |
| Water | Regularly in the first summer; rarely once established. Pots: once or twice a week in hot weather |
| Humidity | Low to average. Good airflow; no misting |
| Temperature | English lavender is frost hardy; French and Spanish lavender need a frost-free winter in cold areas |
| Soil | Free-draining, poor to moderately fertile, chalky or alkaline is fine; never heavy, waterlogged clay |
| Feeding | Rarely needed in the ground |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats, dogs and horses |

## Which lavender do you have?

The type decides how hardy your plant is, so it is worth knowing. The [Royal Horticultural Society](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/lavender/growing-guide) describes three main groups:

- **English lavender** (*Lavandula angustifolia*) and **lavandin** (*L. × intermedia*) are the hardiest. They grow 30 to 90 cm (1 to 3 ft) tall and survive cold winters in the ground.
- **French or Spanish lavender** (*L. stoechas*), with the "rabbit ear" bracts on top of each flower head, is less hardy and reaches about 60 cm (2 ft). It does well in containers and needs a frost-free spot in winter where winters are cold.

If the label is gone, the flower shape is the quickest clue: tufted petals on top mean *L. stoechas*.

## How much sun does lavender need?

Full sun. Lavender comes from dry, sunny Mediterranean hillsides, and the RHS recommends a sunny, open spot. In shade it grows soft and floppy, flowers poorly and is more prone to rot. Choose the sunniest part of the garden, or a south-facing balcony for pots.

Lavender is a poor houseplant. Even a bright windowsill gives far less light than an open garden, and the plant stretches and declines indoors within months. If you want to grow herbs inside, our [indoor herb garden](/en/blog/indoor-herb-garden) guide covers the ones that cope better.

## How often should you water lavender?

It depends on how long the plant has been in place and whether it is in the ground or a pot.

- **New plants:** water regularly through the first summer, whenever the soil is dry a few centimetres down, so the roots grow out into the surrounding soil.
- **Established plants in the ground:** the RHS notes that they rarely need watering. They cope with drought far better than with wet soil. Water only in a long, severe drought, and then deeply.
- **Lavender in pots:** pots dry out fast. The RHS suggests watering once or twice a week in hot summer weather, when the compost has dried out. Keep the compost fairly dry in winter and never leave the pot standing in a saucer of water.

Signs you are overwatering: yellowing lower leaves, grey or blackened stems from the base, and a plant that wilts while the soil is wet. Underwatering shows first on young or potted plants, as drooping tips and crisp, dry leaves. Our article on [lavender problems](/en/blog/lavender-problems) covers each symptom.

## Best soil for lavender

Drainage matters more than anything else. The RHS recommends a free-draining soil of poor to moderate fertility, and notes that lavender is happy in chalky and alkaline soils. It struggles in heavy clay and dies in waterlogged ground.

Do not dig in rich compost or manure. Rich soil makes soft, leafy growth that flowers less and rots more easily. On heavy soil, the RHS suggests planting on a mound or raised bed 20 to 30 cm (8 to 12 in) high so winter rain drains away from the crown, or growing lavender in pots instead.

## How to grow lavender in pots

Choose a pot 30 to 40 cm (12 to 16 in) across with large drainage holes. The RHS suggests a peat-free multipurpose compost or a loam-based mix such as John Innes No. 2, with up to 25% coarse grit added for drainage. Terracotta suits lavender because it dries out faster than plastic.

In winter, move pots against a sunny wall out of the worst rain, and raise them on pot feet so they drain. Bring French and Spanish lavender into a cold, bright, frost-free place, such as an unheated porch or greenhouse, where winters are cold.

## When and how to plant or move lavender

Plant in spring. The RHS suggests April or May, when the soil is warming and the plant can root in before winter. Avoid planting in winter, when cold, wet soil rots the roots before they establish.

- **Spacing:** about 90 cm (3 ft) apart for single plants or groups, and 30 cm (1 ft) apart for a hedge, or 45 cm (18 in) for the larger varieties.
- **Depth:** plant at the same depth the plant sat in its pot, and firm the soil gently.
- **Watering in:** water well after planting, then keep watering through the first summer whenever the soil dries.

To move an established lavender, do it in spring too. Water the plant the day before, dig wide to keep as much of the root ball as possible, replant at the same depth and water it through its first summer in the new spot as if it were a new plant. If an old plant is already woody and past its best, taking cuttings and starting again is a better use of the effort.

## How to prune lavender

Prune every year, or lavender becomes woody and splits open in the middle.

1. **After flowering, in late summer:** cut off all the spent flower stalks, plus about 2.5 cm (1 in) of the leafy growth below them. The RHS gives this as the main trim for English lavender.
2. **In spring:** tidy any shoots damaged over winter once new growth starts.
3. **Never cut into bare, brown wood.** Lavender rarely regrows from old wood. Always leave some green, leafy growth on every stem.

Plants that have become woody and gappy cannot be rescued by hard pruning. The RHS advice is to replace them. Our guide on [how to prune houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-prune-houseplants) covers clean cuts and tools; the same principles apply to lavender in pots.

## How to propagate lavender

Take cuttings from non-flowering shoots. The RHS suggests softwood or semi-ripe cuttings in early to midsummer, or hardwood cuttings in late autumn.

1. Cut a non-flowering shoot 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) long, just below a leaf joint.
2. Strip the leaves from the lower half.
3. Insert the cuttings around the edge of a small pot of gritty, free-draining cutting compost.
4. Keep them in bright light out of direct sun and water lightly. Rooted cuttings start to grow new leaves after several weeks.

Pot them up individually once rooted, and plant them out the following spring. Our [plant propagation guide](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) has more on cuttings.

## Common lavender problems

- **Grey, dying stems from the base:** usually root rot from wet, heavy soil, especially in winter. The RHS warns that lavender rots in wet, heavy ground.
- **Woody, open plant with few flowers:** age and missed pruning. Replace it with a young plant or rooted cuttings.
- **Frothy white blobs on stems:** cuckoo spit, from froghopper nymphs. It is harmless and can be rinsed off.
- **Shiny metallic green and purple beetles:** rosemary beetle, which eats the leaves. Pick them off by hand.
- **Leaves with pale mottling:** leafhoppers. The damage is cosmetic.

The RHS also advises buying lavender from reputable growers, because imported plants can carry *Xylella fastidiosa*, a serious plant disease. For a herb with similar needs, see our [rosemary care guide](/en/blog/rosemary-care-guide).

## Is lavender toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/lavender) lists lavender as toxic to cats, dogs and horses. It contains linalool and linalyl acetate. Signs include nausea, vomiting and loss of appetite. See our list of [plants safe for cats](/en/blog/plants-safe-for-cats) for alternatives.
