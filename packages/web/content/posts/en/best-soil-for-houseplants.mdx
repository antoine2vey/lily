The best soil for houseplants is not soil at all. It is a soilless potting mix, usually peat or coconut coir with perlite, vermiculite or bark, sold as "indoor" or "houseplant" potting mix. A good bag suits most foliage plants as it is. Climbing aroids such as monstera do better with a third bark and a third perlite added, cacti and succulents with up to 30% grit, and orchids with bark and no soil at all.

## Why can't you use garden soil for houseplants?

Garden soil works in the ground because it sits on more soil below it. Water drains down through it, and worms and roots keep it open. In a pot, the same soil packs down into a dense block after a few waterings. It stays waterlogged at the bottom, the roots run out of air, and they rot.

There is a second problem. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-soil-mixes/) notes that garden soils "contain too many bacteria and are generally not recommended for plants grown in containers," and that native soil would need to be pasteurised to kill disease organisms, insects and weed seeds. Bagged soilless mixes are sterile, lightweight and ready to use.

The words on bags are not consistent. "Potting soil" and "potting mix" usually mean the same soilless product. "Garden soil," "topsoil" and "soil improver" are for beds, not pots.

## What is in a good potting mix?

Every potting mix balances two things: holding water and letting air reach the roots. Each ingredient pushes that balance one way or the other.

| Ingredient | What it does | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Peat moss | Holds a lot of water and nutrients, stays light | The base of most mixes. Bogs regrow very slowly, so many gardeners now choose peat-free |
| Coconut coir | Holds water like peat, rewets more easily when dry | The usual peat substitute. Clemson advises rinsing loose coir before use to avoid salt problems |
| Perlite | White volcanic granules that make air pockets | Does not break down or hold nutrients. The easiest way to lighten a mix |
| Vermiculite | Holds water and nutrients | Keeps a mix moister than perlite does; useful for seedlings and thirsty plants |
| Pine or fir bark | Large air spaces, slow to break down | The main ingredient of orchid mixes and a large part of aroid mixes |
| Coarse sand or grit | Adds weight and drainage | Only coarse grades. Fine sand fills the gaps between particles and makes drainage worse |
| Compost | Adds nutrients and organic matter | Fine in small amounts. Too much makes a dense mix that stays wet |

A good bag of indoor potting mix feels springy and light when you squeeze a damp handful. It falls apart again when you open your hand. If it stays in a sticky ball, or if you can see mostly fine black dust with no granules or fibres, it will hold too much water for most houseplants.

## Best soil for houseplants, by plant type

These recipes are by volume, so "1 part" can be a cup, a jug or a bucket. The foliage, aroid and cactus mixes come from Clemson Extension and the RHS, simplified to ingredients you can buy at a garden centre. The others are common practice.

| Plants | Mix |
|---|---|
| Most foliage plants: pothos, peace lily, spider plant, dracaena, calathea, ferns | Indoor potting mix as it comes, or 2 parts peat or coir to 1 part perlite and 1 part coarse sand |
| Climbing aroids and epiphytes: monstera, philodendron, hoya, peperomia, dieffenbachia | Equal parts fine bark, peat or coir, and perlite |
| Cacti, succulents, snake plant, jade plant | Cactus mix, or loam-based compost with up to 30% grit or fine gravel |
| Orchids (Phalaenopsis) | Bark-based orchid mix only, no potting soil |
| African violets | African violet mix, or indoor potting mix with extra perlite |
| Mediterranean herbs: rosemary, lavender, thyme | Potting mix with a third grit or perlite |

Clemson lists its "Cornell epiphytic mix," equal parts bark, peat and perlite, as suited to monstera, philodendron, hoya, peperomia and dieffenbachia, among others. Our [monstera care guide](/en/blog/monstera-care-guide) uses it.

For cacti and succulents, the [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/cacti-succulents/houseplants/growing-guide) recommends "very free-draining, gritty compost that will not get waterlogged": either a ready-made peat-free cactus compost, or a loam-based compost (John Innes No 2 in the UK) with up to 30% grit or fine gravel by volume. Our [cactus care guide](/en/blog/cactus-care-guide) covers the rest.

Orchids sold in supermarkets grow on tree bark in the wild. Their roots need air around them and rot quickly in ordinary potting mix. Our [orchid care guide](/en/blog/orchid-care-guide) explains how to repot them in bark.

## Do you need a drainage layer of gravel?

No. It is one of the most repeated tips in houseplant care, and it makes drainage worse. Washington State University horticulturist [Linda Chalker-Scott explains](https://s3.wp.wsu.edu/uploads/sites/403/2015/03/container-drainage.pdf) that water does not move easily from a fine material into a coarser one below it. It stays in the fine soil until that layer is saturated. A layer of gravel, clay pebbles or broken pot at the bottom therefore raises the waterlogged zone closer to the roots instead of draining it.

What does help:

- **A pot with a drainage hole.** This matters more than any soil recipe. If you love a pot without one, use it as an outer cover and keep the plant in a plastic pot with holes inside it.
- **Fill the whole pot with the same mix**, from the bottom to the top.
- **Empty the saucer** 15 minutes after watering.
- **Choose a pot close to the size of the root ball.** Extra soil around the roots holds water that nothing uses. See our guide on [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant).

## How to tell your potting mix is wrong

The mix is probably too dense or worn out if:

- The soil is still wet 10 days after watering, even though the plant is in good light.
- Lower leaves yellow and the soil smells sour, both early signs of root rot. If you see them, check the roots with our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery).
- Small black flies (fungus gnats) hover around the pot. Their larvae live in soil that never dries at the surface.
- The soil has sunk well below the rim and feels hard.

The mix is too open or has dried out completely if:

- Water runs straight through and out of the hole within seconds.
- The soil has shrunk away from the sides of the pot.

Dried-out peat repels water. To rewet it, stand the pot in a bowl of water for 20 to 30 minutes until the surface feels damp, then let it drain.

If you change to a more open mix, the plant will dry out faster than before. Check the soil more often for the first few weeks. [Lily](/en) schedules watering per plant, which makes it easier to keep one freshly repotted plant on a different rhythm from the rest.

## When should you replace potting soil?

Peat, coir and bark slowly break down. Over one to three years the mix gets finer, drains less and holds less air. Perlite and grit do not break down, which is why mixes with more of them last longer.

Repot most houseplants every one to two years in spring, or when roots circle the pot or grow through the drainage hole. When you repot, remove as much of the old mix from around the roots as comes away easily, and use fresh mix. For a large plant you cannot repot, scrape off the top few centimetres of old mix each spring and replace them. Our [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) walks through it step by step.

## Quick answers

**Can I reuse old potting soil?** You can mix it into garden beds or compost, where it does no harm. For houseplants, fresh mix is safer, because old mix has broken down and may carry fungus gnat larvae or root disease.

**Does potting mix contain fertiliser?** Many bags do. Clemson notes that good mixes usually contain a slow-release fertiliser that lasts a few months, often low in trace elements. Wait until that runs out before feeding a newly repotted plant; the bag usually says how long it lasts.

**Should I add activated charcoal?** There is little reason to in a pot with a drainage hole. It is mainly used in closed terrariums, which have no drainage at all.
