Pothos care comes down to letting the soil dry between waterings and giving the plant more light than its reputation suggests. Water when the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of soil is dry, keep it between about 18 and 27°C (65 and 80°F), and put it in bright, indirect light if you want large, well-marked leaves. It survives a dim corner for a long time, but it grows slowly there and the variegation fades.

## Pothos care at a glance

| Need | What it wants |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect. Tolerates low light for months, with slower growth and less variegation |
| Water | When the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of soil is dry, then until water drains from the pot |
| Humidity | Ordinary room air is fine. A humidifier or wet pebble tray if the air is very dry |
| Temperature | 21 to 27°C (70 to 80°F) by day, 18 to 21°C (65 to 70°F) at night |
| Soil | Any free-draining houseplant mix, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Balanced liquid fertiliser every other month, none in winter |
| Toxicity to pets | Toxic to cats and dogs (insoluble calcium oxalates) |

Golden pothos (*Epipremnum aureum*) comes from the Society Islands in the South Pacific, where it climbs trees and can reach 12 m (40 ft). The light, watering and feeding guidance here follows the [NC State Extension plant page](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/epipremnum-aureum/). The temperature range comes from the University of Arkansas Extension [table of houseplant temperatures](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/yard-garden/home-landscape/docs/Temperature%20Requirements%20of%20Selected%20House%20Plants.pdf), which lists golden pothos with the plants that grow best warm.

## How much light does a pothos need?

NC State describes pothos as preferring bright, indirect light while being able to survive long periods in low light. Both halves are true, and the difference shows in the leaves.

In bright, indirect light, close to an east window or a metre or two back from a south or west one, a pothos grows quickly, the leaves come in bigger, and the yellow, white or silver markings stay strong. In low light, such as the middle of a room or near a north window, it keeps going but new leaves are smaller and spaced further apart along the vine, and variegated types turn greener. NC State names loss of variegation as the usual sign of too little light.

Direct midday sun through glass can bleach or scorch the leaves. A few hours of morning sun is fine. If you are unsure what your windows give you, our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot by window direction and distance.

The older posts on this site described a pothos in a dark room as "dormant". It is not. Pothos does not have a dormant season. Growth slows in winter because there is less light, and that is the only reason to water and feed less then.

## How often should you water a pothos?

Water when the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of soil feels dry, and let the mix dry out between waterings, as NC State advises. Then water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage hole, and empty the saucer after 15 minutes.

In a bright, warm room that often works out to about once a week in summer and every two to three weeks in winter. Treat those figures as a starting point. A plant in low light, in a plastic pot, or in a large pot dries much more slowly than one in a small terracotta pot by a sunny window. Check the soil before you water rather than following the calendar.

Signs you are watering too much: several leaves yellowing at once, soil still damp a week after watering, and dark or black edges on the leaves. NC State lists overwatering as the cause of both root rot and blackened leaf margins. Signs of too little water: leaves that hang limp and soft, then curl and develop crisp brown edges. A thirsty pothos perks up within a day of a good soak.

## Does a pothos need humidity?

Not much. Pothos grows well in normal indoor air, which is one reason it is such a common first plant. If you want to raise humidity, for instance for a plant trained up a pole, NC State suggests a humidifier or a tray of wet pebbles under the pot.

Two pieces of advice from the old posts are worth dropping. Misting the leaves raises humidity for a few minutes at most and wets the foliage for no gain. And a layer of pebbles at the bottom of the pot does not improve drainage. It raises the level where water sits in the soil, so the roots stay wetter. Put the pebbles in the saucer under the pot, not inside it. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) covers what actually works.

## What soil and pot does a pothos need?

A standard houseplant potting mix works, as long as the pot has a drainage hole. If your mix stays wet for a long time, add a handful of perlite or fine bark to open it up. Our article on the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains the options.

Repot in spring when roots circle the bottom of the pot or the plant dries out within a few days of watering. Go up only one size, about 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider. A pot much larger than the root ball holds water the roots cannot use.

## How do you keep a pothos full instead of leggy?

Long vines with bare stretches between leaves are normal for a trailing pothos. NC State notes that older leaves yellow and fall, and recommends pruning to keep the plant bushy.

1. In spring or summer, choose the longest or barest vines.
2. Cut each one just above a leaf, leaving a short stem with at least one node. A node is the slightly swollen point where a leaf meets the stem.
3. New shoots grow from the nodes below the cut, which fills the top of the pot back in.
4. Root the pieces you cut off and plant them back into the same pot for a denser plant.

You can cut back hard, even to 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) from the soil, as long as each stem keeps a few leaves. Use clean scissors.

## How do you propagate a pothos?

Pothos roots very easily. NC State notes that vines root readily in water.

1. Cut a piece of vine with three or four leaves, just below a node.
2. Remove the lowest leaf so the node is bare.
3. Stand the cutting in a jar of water with the node under the surface, in bright indirect light. Change the water every week.
4. When the roots are 3 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) long, usually after a few weeks, pot the cutting in moist potting mix.

Cuttings also root directly in damp potting mix. Our guide to [plant propagation from cuttings](/en/blog/plant-propagation-cuttings) covers both methods in more depth.

## Why do pothos leaves get bigger on a pole?

On a tree in the wild, pothos leaves grow much larger as the vine climbs. NC State notes that with support and good light, the plant indoors can also produce large, mature leaves. A trailing vine hanging down tends to keep making small leaves. If you want bigger foliage, train a few stems up a moss pole or a slatted support and keep the plant in bright light. Our guide on [how to make a moss pole](/en/blog/how-to-make-a-moss-pole) shows how.

## Is pothos toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/golden-pothos) lists golden pothos as toxic to both. It contains insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which cause mouth irritation, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing if chewed. Hang it out of reach if you have a pet that chews plants.

## Feeding and pests

Feed with a balanced liquid fertiliser at the label dose every other month from spring to the end of summer, and stop in winter. A pothos that gets more food than light grows long and pale rather than full.

NC State lists mealybugs and scale as its main pests. Look for white cottony clumps where leaves join the vine and small brown bumps on the stems. Wipe them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then treat with insecticidal soap. Yellow leaves, brown spots and other symptoms each have their own section in our article on [pothos problems](/en/blog/pothos-problems).
