Most alocasia problems start with water or temperature. Yellow leaves most often mean the soil stays wet too long, a plant that droops and drops leaves in autumn is often going semi-dormant rather than dying, and an alocasia kept below about 10°C (50°F) for long stops growing and starts to decline. Below, each symptom has its likely causes in order, how to tell them apart, and the fix.

## Alocasia problems: check three things first

Before you change anything, check the soil about 5 cm (2 in) down, the temperature where the plant actually sits, and the time of year. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/alocasia/) lists the ideal range as 20 to 25°C (68 to 77°F), with soil that is moist but well drained. The [University of Wisconsin–Madison Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/elephant-ears/) notes that elephant ears falter when kept below 10°C (50°F) for long periods, and that indoor plants slow down in winter. Wet soil, a cold spot or the start of winter explain most cases.

## Why are my alocasia leaves turning yellow?

**1. The oldest leaf is being replaced.** An alocasia carries only a few leaves at a time. It is common for the oldest, lowest leaf to yellow while a new one unfurls. If only one leaf is yellowing and new growth looks healthy, cut it off at the base once it has faded.

**2. The soil stays too wet.** Several leaves yellowing at once, starting low, with soil that is still damp days after watering, points to overwatering. NC State lists overwatering as a cause of yellow leaves and of root rot in alocasia. The usual reasons are watering on a fixed schedule, a pot without a drainage hole, water left in the saucer or cachepot, or dense soil that never dries. Let the top few centimetres dry before you water again, and check the roots if the plant keeps declining.

**3. The soil dried out completely.** NC State names too little water as another cause. Yellowing here comes with limp stems and soil that has pulled away from the edge of the pot. Water slowly until it runs from the drainage hole, then water a little sooner next time.

**4. Not enough light.** Inadequate light is the third cause on NC State's list. Pale, yellowing leaves on long, weak stems that lean hard toward the window mean the plant is too far from the light. Move it close to a bright window with no harsh midday sun.

**5. Spider mites.** Fine yellow speckling that spreads across the leaf, rather than whole leaves turning yellow from the edge, is the typical spider mite pattern. See the pest section below.

For other plants with the same symptom, our guide to [plant leaves turning yellow](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) covers the general causes.

## Why is my alocasia drooping?

**1. Thirst.** A plant that droops with dry soil and a light pot needs water. The stems should firm up within a few hours to a day.

**2. Root rot.** If the plant droops while the soil is wet, do not water it again. Rotten roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts as if it were dry. See the root rot section below.

**3. Cold or a draught.** An alocasia next to a cold window, an outside door or an air-conditioning vent can droop with perfect soil. Move it somewhere that stays in the 20 to 25°C (68 to 77°F) range.

**4. It is going dormant.** In autumn and winter, with shorter days and cooler rooms, many alocasias slow down, lose leaves one by one and can die back to the corm (the swollen underground stem). Wisconsin's advice for overwintering indoors is to keep plants drier while they are semi-dormant and to resume normal care in spring. If the stem base and corm are firm, the plant is resting, not dead. Water lightly, only when the soil is mostly dry, and wait for new growth in spring.

**5. Heavy leaves on weak stems.** Leaves that grow in low light are often too heavy for their stems. More light, not more water, fixes this over time.

If you cannot tell whether your plant gets too much or too little water, our [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) guide compares the two side by side.

## Why are my alocasia leaves turning brown?

**Crispy brown edges and tips** usually come from dry air, uneven watering or salt build-up from fertiliser. Alocasias come from warm, humid parts of tropical Asia and eastern Australia, and Wisconsin notes that they do best in warm, humid conditions. Group plants together or run a humidifier near them in winter, when heating dries the air. Misting wets the leaves for a few minutes and does little for humidity. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares what works. If you feed often, flush the pot with plain water every few months.

**Large dry, pale or brown patches** on the side facing the window are sunburn. Move the plant back from direct midday sun or diffuse the light with a sheer curtain.

**Brown or black spots with a yellow ring** are leaf spots. NC State lists leaf spots, along with root rot, as problems that follow overwatering. Cut off badly spotted leaves, keep water off the foliage, water the soil only, and give the plant more space so air moves around it.

**Brown, soft patches spreading from the stem** usually mean rot is moving up from the roots or corm. Check the roots.

## How do I know if my alocasia has root rot?

Root rot is the most serious alocasia problem, and it nearly always follows soil that stays wet for long periods.

**Signs above the soil:** several leaves yellowing at once, drooping while the soil is damp, stems that go soft at the base, and a sour or rotten smell from the pot.

**Signs below the soil:** tip the plant out. Healthy roots are firm and pale. Rotten roots are brown or black, soft and mushy, and the outer layer slides off when you pull gently. Also squeeze the corm: it should be firm, not soft or hollow.

**The fix:**

1. Remove all the old soil and rinse the roots.
2. Cut away every soft or dark root, and any soft part of the corm, back to firm, pale tissue. Use clean, sharp scissors.
3. Repot into fresh, well-draining mix in a pot only slightly larger than the remaining roots, with a drainage hole. A chunky aroid mix, or potting mix with added bark and perlite, drains well.
4. Keep the plant warm and in bright, indirect light. Water lightly, only when the top few centimetres are dry, until new growth appears.

If most of the roots are gone but the corm is firm, you can still save the plant. Pot the corm in barely moist mix and keep it warm. Many alocasias regrow from the corm alone. Our [root rot recovery](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) guide covers the steps in more detail.

## What pests attack alocasia?

**Spider mites** are the main one. NC State notes that alocasias are prone to them. Look for fine yellow or grey speckling on the leaves, a dull, dusty look, and very fine webbing under the leaves and where the leaf meets the stem. Warm, dry indoor air in winter makes outbreaks worse.

To deal with them, isolate the plant and rinse both sides of every leaf in the shower. Then wipe the leaves, or treat them with insecticidal soap following the label, every 5 to 7 days for several weeks, because new mites keep hatching. Our [spider mites treatment](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) guide explains the full routine.

**Aphids and mealybugs** also appear on alocasias. Aphids cluster on new leaves and leave sticky honeydew. Mealybugs look like small tufts of white cotton in the leaf joints. Rinse aphids off and dab mealybugs with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol.

Check the underside of the leaves every time you water. Spider mites are much easier to clear when you catch them early.

## Why is my alocasia dripping water from its leaves?

Drops of water at the tips of the leaves after watering are guttation. The plant pushes out excess water through pores at the leaf edge when the soil is very wet and the air is humid. It does no harm by itself, but frequent dripping suggests the soil is staying wetter than it needs to.

## Quick answers

**Is my alocasia dead if it lost all its leaves?** Not necessarily. Check the corm. If it is firm, keep the soil barely moist and the plant warm, and wait for new growth in spring.

**How often should I water an alocasia?** When the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) of soil is dry in spring and summer, and less often in winter. NC State advises reducing water and fertiliser in winter. For the rest of its needs, read our [alocasia care guide](/en/blog/alocasia-care-guide).

**Is alocasia toxic to pets?** Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/alocasia) lists it as toxic to cats, dogs and horses because of insoluble calcium oxalate crystals. Chewing it causes mouth irritation, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing.
