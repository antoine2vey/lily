Most anthurium problems trace back to water: soil kept wet for too long causes yellow leaves, drooping and root rot, which [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/anthurium-andraeanum/) lists as the main risk for the plant. The rest usually come from light (scorch from direct sun, or no flowers in a dim room), dry air (brown tips below about 60% humidity) and four sap-sucking pests. Find your symptom below, check the causes in the order given, and fix the first one that matches.

## Anthurium problems: check the soil first

Before you treat any symptom, feel the mix 5 cm (2 in) down, and check that the pot has a drainage hole and an empty saucer. Water is behind most of what goes wrong with this plant, so those two checks rule the biggest cause in or out.

## Why are my anthurium leaves turning yellow?

NC State names three causes: overwatering, poor drainage and lack of nutrients. Age is a fourth, harmless one. In order of how often they turn up:

**1. The soil stays wet.** This is the most common cause by far. Yellowing usually starts on lower leaves and spreads, the leaves feel soft rather than crisp, and the soil is still damp several days after watering. Anthuriums grow on tree trunks and in leaf litter in the wild, so their roots need air as much as water. Let the top 2.5 cm (1 in) dry before you water again, which is NC State's guideline, and check that water drains freely from the pot.

**2. Poor drainage.** The watering frequency is fine, but the water has nowhere to go: a decorative pot without a hole, a saucer that stays full, or dense compost that has broken down into mud. Tip the plant out of its cachepot after every watering. If the mix is old and compact, repot into a chunky, airy mix with bark and perlite.

**3. Lack of nutrients.** Leaves turn evenly pale yellow-green, starting with older ones, on a plant that has not been fed for a long time and is otherwise firm and well watered. NC State recommends a liquid fertiliser during the growing season. Use a balanced houseplant feed at the label's dilution, or weaker, from spring to late summer.

**4. Old age.** One lower leaf yellows slowly while new leaves keep appearing at the top. That is normal turnover. Cut the leaf off at the base once it is fully yellow.

A sudden yellowing of several leaves after a cold night or a move next to a door points to a chill. NC State gives 18 to 27°C (65 to 80°F) as the right range and advises keeping the plant out of draughts.

## Why does my anthurium have brown tips or brown spots?

Brown edges and brown spots look similar at first, but they have different causes.

**Brown, crisp tips and edges** usually mean the air is too dry. NC State gives 60 to 80% humidity as the plant's preference, which is far wetter than a heated room in winter. Move the plant away from radiators and heating vents, group it with other plants, or run a humidifier nearby. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares what works. Underwatering produces the same crisp edges, so check the soil first: if it is dry well below the surface, water more often.

**Pale or brown patches on the side facing the window** are sun scorch. Anthuriums want bright light, but direct sun through glass burns the leaves. Move the plant out of the sun's path or behind a sheer curtain. The damaged patches will not recover.

**Round or irregular brown spots, sometimes with a yellow halo,** are usually a leaf spot disease. NC State links them to poor air circulation and wet foliage. Water the soil rather than the leaves, give the plant some space from its neighbours, and remove the worst affected leaves with clean scissors.

## Why is my anthurium drooping?

A drooping anthurium is short of water in its leaves. The question is whether the roots cannot find water or cannot absorb it.

**1. The soil is dry.** The pot feels light and the soil has pulled away from the edges. Water thoroughly until water runs out of the hole, and the leaves should lift within a day.

**2. The roots are rotting.** The soil is wet, yet the plant still droops. Rotten roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts in damp soil. This is the more serious case: see the root rot section below.

**3. A shock.** A cold draught, a recent repot or a move can make the plant sag for a few days. If the soil and roots are fine, keep conditions stable and wait.

To tell the first two apart, push a finger into the soil 5 cm (2 in) deep. Dry soil means thirst. Wet soil plus droop means look at the roots. Our article on [how often to water an anthurium](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-anthurium) explains how to find the right rhythm afterwards.

## How do you know if an anthurium has root rot?

Root rot is the anthurium problem NC State flags first, and it follows overwatering. The signs above the soil are yellow leaves, drooping in wet soil and a plant that stops growing. Sometimes the base of the stems turns dark and soft.

Slide the plant out of its pot to be sure. Healthy anthurium roots are thick, firm and pale. Rotten roots are brown or black, soft or hollow, and the outer layer slides off when you pinch it. A sour or musty smell from the root ball confirms it.

If some roots are still firm:

1. Rinse the old compost off the roots.
2. Cut every soft or dark root back to firm tissue with clean, sharp scissors.
3. Repot into fresh, airy mix with plenty of bark, in a pot just large enough for the remaining roots, with a drainage hole.
4. Water lightly, then wait until the top of the mix is dry before watering again.

If every root is soft and the stem base is mushy, take a healthy stem section with a few aerial roots and root it as a new plant. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) goes through the whole process, including when it is better to start over.

## What pests attack anthuriums?

NC State lists aphids, spider mites, mealybugs and scale. The signs usually show before the pests themselves:

- **Sticky leaves or a black sooty film:** aphids, mealybugs or scale, which all leave sugary honeydew behind. Look under the leaves and along stems.
- **Fine pale speckling and faint webbing:** spider mites, most common in warm, dry air.
- **White fluffy patches in leaf joints or on roots:** mealybugs.
- **Small brown or tan bumps that scrape off:** scale insects.

Isolate the plant first so the pests do not spread. Wipe off what you can with a cloth or a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then treat with insecticidal soap or horticultural oil, following the label and repeating as it says. One treatment never catches the eggs, so plan on several rounds a week apart. Our article on [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) explains each one in more detail.

## Why is my anthurium not flowering?

NC State gives two reasons: too little light, and a lack of nutrients.

**1. Not enough light.** This is the usual cause. An anthurium can keep healthy green leaves in a fairly dim room but needs bright, indirect light to flower. Move it close to an east-facing window, or a little back from a south- or west-facing one, out of direct sun.

**2. No feeding.** A plant that has sat in the same compost for years without fertiliser runs out of the nutrients it needs to bloom. Feed regularly from spring to late summer.

**3. A pot that is far too big.** Plants in oversized pots often put their energy into roots and leaves first. Repot only when roots fill the pot, which NC State puts at every two to three years, in spring.

Note that the colourful part is not a petal but a modified leaf called a spathe. It lasts for weeks, then fades to green or brown. Cut faded flower stems at the base.

## Is an anthurium toxic to pets?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/flamingo-flower) lists the anthurium (flamingo flower) as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. It contains insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, and chewing it causes mouth irritation, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing.
