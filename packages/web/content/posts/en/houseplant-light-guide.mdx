This houseplant light guide turns vague labels into numbers. "Low light" is roughly 250 to 1,000 lux (25 to 100 foot-candles), "medium" is 1,000 to 5,000 lux, "bright" is 5,000 to 10,000 lux, and "direct sun" is anything above that, according to [UF/IFAS Extension](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/plants/houseplants/light-for-houseplants/). A spot a few metres from a window can get a tenth of the light right beside it, even though your eyes tell you both are bright.

## Why does light matter more than anything else?

Light is a plant's food supply. In photosynthesis, a plant uses light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into the sugars that fuel its growth. Chlorophyll, the green pigment in leaves, absorbs that light. Water and fertiliser are raw materials. Light is the energy that turns them into new leaves.

This has two practical consequences:

- **Nothing makes up for too little light.** Extra fertiliser cannot replace the energy a plant is not getting. Extra water just sits in the pot, because a plant that is barely photosynthesising barely uses water. That is why plants in dark corners so often end up with root rot.
- **Light sets the pace for everything else.** A plant in bright light grows faster, drinks more and can use more fertiliser. The same plant in dim light needs less of all three. When you move a plant, its watering needs change too.

UF/IFAS calls lighting the most important factor in choosing which plants belong where in your home, because temperature, water and feeding are easy to adjust, while the light in a given spot is not.

## Why can't you judge light by eye?

Human eyes adapt very quickly to brightness. You walk from a sunny windowsill to the far side of the room and, within a minute or two, both look "bright". UF/IFAS gives some striking figures: even in winter, the light outside at noon can exceed 4,000 foot-candles (about 43,000 lux). A north-facing window may never reach 400 foot-candles (about 4,300 lux). An office lit only by fluorescent tubes often gets 40 foot-candles (about 430 lux) or less. You can read comfortably in all three.

So when a care label says "bright, indirect light", your eyes alone cannot tell you whether a given spot provides it.

## How do you measure light for houseplants?

A handheld light meter that reads in lux or foot-candles is the most reliable tool, and an inexpensive one is accurate enough for houseplants. Phone apps also measure light. UF/IFAS notes that their accuracy varies by phone, so use them to compare spots rather than trust the exact number.

To get a useful reading:

1. Measure at midday, when the light is strongest.
2. Hold the sensor where the plant's leaves are, facing the window.
3. Measure on a bright day and an overcast one, since both will occur.
4. Measure again in winter. The same spot can lose much of its light between June and December.

## Houseplant light guide: low, medium, bright and direct in lux

These ranges come from UF/IFAS, with lux added (1 foot-candle is about 10.8 lux). The window directions apply to the Northern Hemisphere. In the Southern Hemisphere, swap north and south.

| Light level | Midday reading | Where you find it | Plants that cope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 250 to 1,000 lux (25 to 100 fc) | Far from windows, lit mostly by ceiling lights | ZZ plant, snake plant, cast-iron plant; few plants grow well here |
| Medium | 1,000 to 5,000 lux (100 to 500 fc) | Near a window with no direct sun: north-facing, or a shaded east or west window | Peace lily, parlor palm, pothos, many ferns |
| Bright | 5,000 to 10,000 lux (500 to 1,000 fc) | Near an unshaded east or west window, or a shaded south window; any direct sun is filtered | Fiddle leaf fig, monstera, anthurium, string of pearls |
| Direct | Over 10,000 lux (1,000 fc) | Right in front of a south or southwest window with only glass between the plant and the sun | Cacti, most succulents, herbs such as basil |

UF/IFAS adds that plants needing "direct light" do best with four or more hours of sun.

"Plants that cope" means they keep going there. Most low-light plants grow faster in medium light. For a shortlist of forgiving plants by light level, see our [beginner houseplants guide](/en/blog/beginner-houseplants).

## Which window gives which light?

In the Northern Hemisphere:

- **North-facing:** soft light all day with no direct sun. Medium light right at the glass, dropping off fast.
- **East-facing:** gentle direct sun in the morning, bright the rest of the day. Good for most foliage plants.
- **West-facing:** bright with hot afternoon sun. Good for sun lovers, but it can scorch shade plants in summer.
- **South-facing:** the most light, with direct sun for much of the day. Best for cacti and succulents. Move foliage plants a metre or two back, or behind a sheer curtain.

Distance matters as much as direction. UF/IFAS notes that a desk by a window may be ten times brighter than a workspace across the room. Trees, buildings, balconies, net curtains and dirty glass all cut light further.

## How do you tell if a plant is getting the wrong light?

**Too little light:**

- Long, weak stems with big gaps between leaves, leaning toward the window
- New leaves smaller than the old ones
- Variegated leaves turning plain green
- Lower leaves yellowing and dropping one at a time
- Soil that stays wet for days after watering
- No flowers on a plant that should flower

**Too much light:**

- Pale, bleached or yellow-white patches on leaves facing the window
- Brown, crisp scorch marks, often after a move to a sunnier spot
- Leaves curling or the whole plant wilting in the afternoon even though the soil is moist

When you move a plant into much stronger light, do it gradually over a week or two. Leaves that grew in shade burn easily. Our guide on [how to acclimate new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) explains how.

## How does light change through the year?

In winter the sun is lower, days are shorter and skies are greyer. A spot that gave bright light in summer may only give medium light in December. Move light-hungry plants closer to the window in autumn, and expect them to use less water. Our guide to [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers the rest of the winter routine.

In summer, the reverse applies: a west- or south-facing sill that was fine in March can scorch leaves in July. Pull shade plants back a little, or filter the light with a sheer curtain.

## Do houseplants need grow lights?

Not if you choose plants that match your windows. A grow light helps when you want a plant in a spot that is too dark for it, or to get sun-loving plants through a grey winter. UF/IFAS confirms that many plants can be grown entirely under artificial light.

What to buy, according to UF/IFAS:

- **LED or fluorescent.** Both work well. LEDs cost more up front but use less electricity and last longer. Incandescent bulbs give off too much heat and too little useful light.
- **Broad-spectrum or "cool white".** Plants grow better under a broad spectrum of light. Lamps labelled "cool white" work well, and "warm white" or "daylight" can also work.
- **Long enough hours.** Most plants need eight or more hours a day under the light. A plug-in timer makes this automatic.

Keep the light fairly close to the leaves, as the manufacturer suggests, since its intensity drops quickly with distance. For light-hungry plants such as a [fiddle leaf fig](/en/blog/fiddle-leaf-fig-care-guide), a grow light in winter is often the difference between holding on and growing.
