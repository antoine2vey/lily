How often to water a parlor palm depends on how fast its compost dries, but a good starting point indoors is every 7 to 10 days in spring and summer and every 2 to 3 weeks in winter. Water when the surface of the compost has dried, soak it until water runs out of the drainage holes, then empty the saucer. The goal is compost that stays lightly moist, never soggy and never bone dry.

## How to tell when a parlor palm needs water

The [RHS parlour palm guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/chamaedorea/growing-guide) gives the rule: water when the surface of the compost is dry, and do not water compost that is still damp. In winter, it suggests pushing a finger below the surface to check, because the top can look dry while the compost underneath is still wet.

Three quick checks work well together.

- **Touch.** Push a finger 2 to 3 cm (about 1 in) into the compost. If it feels dry at that depth, water. If it is cool and damp, wait.
- **Weight.** Lift the pot just after watering and again a few days later. A pot that needs water is clearly lighter.
- **Look.** Compost pulling away from the edge of the pot means it has dried too far.

Parlor palms sit between succulents and ferns. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/palms-as-houseplants/) says indoor palms should never dry out completely and never be waterlogged. NC State notes that the parlor palm tolerates short dry spells, so a missed week is not a disaster, but a pot that is always wet is.

## How often to water a parlor palm in each season

These are starting intervals for a parlor palm in ordinary potting compost, in a pot with a drainage hole, in bright indirect light at room temperature. Adjust them once you have seen how fast your pot dries.

| Season | Starting interval | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Every 7 to 10 days | Growth picks up as days lengthen |
| Summer | Every 7 to 10 days | Check more often in a warm, bright room |
| Autumn | Every 10 to 14 days | Stretch the gap as light fades |
| Winter | Every 2 to 3 weeks | Check with a finger below the surface first |

The RHS says to reduce watering in winter. It recommends 20 to 27°C (68 to 80°F) from March to October and slightly cooler conditions in winter, when the plant grows slowly and uses less water. A palm next to a radiator will still dry faster than one in a cool room, so trust the finger test over the calendar.

## How to water a parlor palm

Water thoroughly. Pour slowly over the whole surface until water runs out of the bottom, then let the pot drain. Small sips that wet only the top centimetre leave the roots below dry.

Empty the saucer or cover pot within half an hour. The RHS warns never to leave a parlor palm standing in water, and [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-palms/) names poor drainage and overly frequent watering as the causes of root rot in indoor palms.

The RHS prefers rainwater at room temperature. Hard tap water is alkaline and can brown the leaves over time. If your tap water is hard, rainwater or filtered water is a better choice. Either way, use it at room temperature.

## What changes how often a parlor palm needs water

**Light.** A palm in bright, indirect light uses water faster than one in a dim corner. Parlor palms tolerate low light, which is part of their appeal, but in low light the compost can stay damp for weeks. Water less often there.

**Pot size and material.** Terracotta dries faster than plastic or glazed ceramic. A small pot dries faster than a large one. The RHS advises repotting only into a pot about 2 cm (1 in) wider, because an oversized pot holds more wet compost than the roots can use and leads to root rot.

**Temperature and heating.** Warm rooms and central heating speed up drying. A cool room in winter slows it a lot. Clemson notes that parlor palms need at least 16°C (60°F) in winter, and Penn State warns of cold damage at 7°C (45°F) or below. A cold palm in wet compost is the classic rot setup.

**Humidity.** Dry air does not change how often to water so much as how the leaves look. Penn State notes that browning throughout the plant can mean low humidity or not enough water.

## Signs of overwatering a parlor palm

- Lower fronds yellowing, several at a time.
- Compost that stays wet a week or more after watering, sometimes with a sour smell.
- Soft, dark stems at the base, or fronds that collapse even though the compost is wet.
- Fungus gnats hovering over the compost.

What to do: stop watering and let the top few centimetres dry before the next watering. Check that the pot has a drainage hole and that water is not sitting in the cover pot. If stems are soft at the base or roots are brown and mushy, unpot the plant and follow our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery).

## Signs of underwatering a parlor palm

- Brown, crispy frond tips and edges across the plant, not just on the oldest fronds.
- Fronds drooping or folding, with dry compost.
- A pot that feels light, with compost shrinking from the sides.

What to do: water slowly until it drains. If the compost has dried hard and water runs straight down the sides, stand the pot in a bowl of water for 20 to 30 minutes, then let it drain fully.

Some browning is normal. Penn State notes that browning of the lowest, oldest fronds is usually just age. Our [overwatering vs underwatering guide](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) helps when the signs are mixed.

## Quick answers

**Should I mist my parlor palm?** It is not needed for watering. NC State lists it as fine in low to medium humidity. Misting is no substitute for watering the roots.

**Do other indoor palms follow the same rule?** Broadly, yes. Penn State's advice to keep the compost moist but never waterlogged covers indoor palms in general. For the areca palm, see [how often to water an areca palm](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-areca-palm).
