Most calathea problems come from four things: dry air, minerals in tap water, soil that is too wet or too dry, and cold. Curling leaves usually mean the plant is thirsty or the air is too dry, brown crispy edges point to low humidity or the fluoride in tap water, and yellow leaves with wet soil mean overwatering. Calatheas want 18 to 24°C (65 to 75°F) and suffer below 16°C (61°F).

Many plants sold as calathea have been renamed *Goeppertia* by botanists. The care and the problems are the same.

## Calathea problems: check these three things first

Before you change anything, check the soil, the water and the spot.

- **Soil.** Push a finger 2 to 3 cm (1 in) into the pot. Calatheas like it evenly moist, never soggy and never bone dry.
- **Water.** Do you water straight from the tap? Calatheas are sensitive to it.
- **Spot.** Is the plant in direct sun, next to a radiator, or near a cold window or door?

The answers usually point to the cause before you get to the symptom list.

## Why are my calathea leaves curling?

**1. The soil is too dry.** Curling is the plant's way of cutting water loss. [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/calathea-picturata/) lists leaf curling as a sign of underwatering. If the top few centimetres are dry and the pot feels light, water slowly until it drains. The leaves usually open again once the soil is evenly moist.

**2. The air is too dry.** If the soil is moist and the leaves still curl, look at the air. NC State notes that low humidity causes calathea leaves to curl, and gives 60 to 70% humidity as the target for *Calathea picturata*. Winter heating can drop indoor humidity well below that. See our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants).

**3. Too much sun or heat.** Leaves on the window side that curl and fade are getting more light than they can take. Move the plant back from the glass.

To tell 1 from 2: dry soil and a light pot means water. Moist soil, curled leaves and a heater nearby means air.

## Why does my calathea have brown edges and crispy tips?

**1. Low humidity.** Brown, papery edges are the most common calathea complaint. NC State links brown edges to low humidity. A humidifier in the room, or grouping plants together, raises humidity more than occasional misting, which dries off within minutes.

**2. Fluoride and minerals in tap water.** NC State's fact sheets for two calathea species both name fluoride in tap water as a cause of browning leaf tips and edges, and the [NC State Goeppertia page](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/goeppertia/) recommends distilled water or rainwater instead of tap water. If the browning continues even in a humid room, switch to rainwater, distilled water or filtered water.

**3. Uneven watering.** Letting the pot dry out completely between waterings, then soaking it, also scorches the leaf edges. Water a little more often so the soil never goes fully dry.

**4. Fertiliser build-up.** Heavy feeding leaves salts in the soil that burn the edges. Flush the pot with plenty of water once in a while and feed lightly in spring and summer only.

Brown tips do not turn green again. Trim the dead part with clean scissors, following the leaf's shape, once you have fixed the cause.

## Why are my calathea leaves turning yellow?

**1. Overwatering.** Several lower leaves yellowing at once, with soil that is still wet days after watering, is the classic pattern. NC State lists yellow leaves as a sign of overwatering. Let the top 2 to 3 cm dry before watering again, empty the saucer, and check that the pot has a drainage hole. Our [watering guide for calathea](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-calathea) gives starting intervals.

**2. Underwatering.** NC State also notes that yellowing together with curling can mean the plant is too dry. The difference is the soil. Dry soil and crisp, curled yellow leaves mean thirst. Wet soil and soft, limp yellow leaves mean too much water.

**3. Old leaves.** One outer leaf yellowing now and then, while new leaves keep unfurling from the centre, is normal. Cut it off at the base.

**4. Low light.** A calathea in a dark corner slowly yellows and loses its pattern. NC State recommends medium, indirect light.

For a general approach to yellow leaves on any plant, see [why plant leaves turn yellow](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow).

## Why is my calathea drooping?

**1. Thirst.** A calathea that droops with dry soil will usually perk up within hours of watering.

**2. Root or stem rot.** If it droops while the soil is wet, the roots are probably damaged. NC State warns that overwatering or cold causes stem rot and drooping. Check the base of the stems. Dark, soft, mushy stems at soil level and a sour smell from the pot mean rot. Take the plant out of its pot, cut away black or mushy roots, and repot into fresh, airy mix. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) walks through the steps.

**3. Cold.** NC State says *Calathea picturata* does not tolerate temperatures below 16°C (61°F). A plant against a cold window in winter, or in the path of a draught, can collapse even with good soil. Move it somewhere that stays between 18 and 24°C (65 and 75°F) and away from doors and air vents.

## Why is my calathea dropping leaves?

Whole leaves breaking off at the base usually follow one of the problems above: rot at the stem base, a cold shock, or a long dry spell. NC State advises avoiding draughts and sudden temperature changes. A newly bought plant may also lose a leaf or two as it adjusts to a drier home than the greenhouse it came from. If new leaves keep coming from the centre, the plant is recovering.

## Why does my calathea have faded or scorched patches?

Direct sun scorches calathea leaves, according to NC State. You will see bleached, washed-out patches or dry brown blotches on the leaves facing the window. With NC State recommending medium, indirect light, a spot a metre or two (3 to 6 ft) back from a bright window, or behind a sheer curtain, is about right.

## What pests attack calatheas?

NC State lists aphids, scale insects, mealybugs and spider mites. Spider mites are the one to watch, because they like the same warm, dry rooms that already stress calatheas.

- **Spider mites.** Fine webbing under the leaves and tiny pale speckles on the upper surface. Rinse the leaves thoroughly in the shower, top and underside, and repeat weekly. Our [spider mite treatment guide](/en/blog/spider-mites-treatment) covers what to do if rinsing is not enough. Raising humidity makes the plant less stressed, but misting alone will not get rid of an infestation.
- **Mealybugs.** White, cottony clumps where the leaf stalks meet the stems. Dab them off with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol.
- **Scale.** Small brown bumps on stems and leaf veins that scrape off with a fingernail.
- **Aphids.** Clusters of small green or black insects on new growth. Rinse them off.

NC State also lists gray mould, a fuzzy grey growth on damaged leaves. It goes with cool, damp, still air. Remove affected leaves and improve airflow.

Dusty leaves make every one of these harder to spot. Wipe the leaves with a damp cloth every few weeks.

## Is calathea safe for cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/calathea) lists calathea as non-toxic to dogs, cats and horses.
