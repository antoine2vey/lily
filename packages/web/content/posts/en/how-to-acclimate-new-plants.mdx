How to acclimate new plants comes down to three things: keep them away from your other plants for about 3 weeks, put them in the right light straight away, and then leave them alone while they adjust. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/pest-and-disease-problems-of-indoor-plants) recommends isolating new houseplants for about three weeks, and notes in a [second guide](https://extension.psu.edu/before-the-first-frost-bringing-your-plants-indoors-for-winter) that plants moved indoors can take 1 to 2 months to adjust, with some yellowing or leaf drop along the way. A few lost leaves in the first weeks are normal, not a sign that you did something wrong.

Most houseplants are grown in greenhouses with bright light, high humidity and regular feeding. A living room has less of all three, so a new plant spends its first weeks adjusting the leaves and roots it grew for the greenhouse.

## How to acclimate new plants step by step

### 1. Inspect it before it comes inside

Look at the plant in the shop or as soon as you unpack it. Penn State recommends checking the leaves, the stems and the soil. Turn several leaves over and look along the stems and in the leaf joints for:

- white cottony tufts (mealybugs);
- small brown bumps that do not move (scale);
- fine webbing or pale speckling (spider mites);
- clusters of small soft insects on new shoots (aphids);
- small dark flies over the soil (fungus gnats).

Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) shows how to identify and treat each one. Remove any dead or yellow leaves, which can hide pests and fungal spots.

### 2. Keep it apart for 3 to 4 weeks

Put the new plant in a different room from your other plants, or at least a couple of metres away and not touching them. Penn State suggests about three weeks for new houseplants and three to four weeks for plants coming in from outdoors. That is long enough for eggs hidden on the plant or in the soil to hatch, so a problem shows up before it spreads. Check under the leaves each time you water.

### 3. Put it in its final light straight away

Choose the spot for the plant's needs, not for where it looks best, and put it there during quarantine if you can. Moving a plant again and again makes it adjust over and over. Most common houseplants do best in bright, indirect light, and Penn State notes that plants needing a lot of light do well near a sunny south- or west-facing window. If a sun-loving plant came from a bright greenhouse, avoid putting it straight into hot afternoon sun behind glass; start a little back from the window for a week or two. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot.

### 4. Water by the soil, not by habit

Shop plants are often watered on a nursery schedule, and their compost may be very wet or very dry when you buy them. Check the soil with a finger before you water for the first time, and from then on water when that plant's soil reaches the right dryness: a succulent when it is dry, a fern when the surface starts to dry. Penn State's advice for plants moved indoors applies to new plants too: adjust watering to the soil moisture rather than following a fixed schedule. Our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) shows how to read the signs.

### 5. Do not repot or fertilise yet

Wait to repot. A plant adjusting to new light and humidity copes better without its roots being disturbed at the same time. Repot only if water runs straight through without wetting the soil, roots are circling heavily out of the bottom, or the plant falls over. Otherwise wait until spring and until roots fill the pot; our [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) covers the timing. Skip fertiliser too. Penn State recommends holding off while a plant is adjusting and not producing new growth, which for a new plant usually means the first month or two.

## Is it normal for a new plant to drop leaves?

Yes, within limits. Penn State notes that some yellowing or leaf drop can happen after a plant moves indoors and that it may need one to two months to adjust before new growth follows. Typical signs of normal adjustment:

- one to a few older leaves turn yellow and drop, usually the lowest ones;
- a flowering plant drops some buds or flowers;
- growth stops for a few weeks.

Signs that something else is wrong:

- many leaves yellowing at once, with soil that stays wet (overwatering or poor drainage);
- crisp, brown leaves or patches on the side facing the window (too much direct sun);
- pale, stretched new growth leaning hard toward the light (too little light);
- sticky leaves, webbing, speckling or insects (pests).

Do not try to fix normal adjustment by moving the plant, watering more or feeding it. That adds a second change on top of the first.

## How to acclimate plants coming back indoors

Plants that spent the summer outside face the biggest change of all: from full outdoor light to indoor light, and often from warm nights to dry, heated air. Penn State's advice:

- Do not wait for the first frost. Start preparing when night temperatures approach 4 to 10 °C (40 to 50°F), and bring cold-sensitive tropical plants in earlier.
- Reduce their light gradually for one to two weeks before the move, for example by moving them into shade outdoors.
- Check the leaves, stems and soil and remove damaged or infested parts. Rinse the foliage with a hose.
- Soak each pot up to the rim in lukewarm water for at least 15 minutes to flush out insects hiding in the soil, then let it drain completely.
- Keep the plants apart from your other houseplants for three to four weeks.
- Water less once they are inside, because they grow more slowly, and go by the soil moisture.

Our guide to [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers the rest of the season.

## How long does it take a new plant to adjust?

Plan on two phases. The first 3 to 4 weeks are quarantine and close watching. After that, Penn State gives one to two months for a plant to settle into a new environment, after which new growth is the sign it has adjusted. A new leaf that is a normal size and colour is the best evidence that the light, water and position are right.

Keeping track of when you watered each new plant makes the first weeks easier to read. [Lily](/en) sets a watering schedule per plant and adjusts it indoors for the season's daylight, so a new plant starts on a rhythm that matches its spot.
