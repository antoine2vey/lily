Monstera care comes down to four things: bright light without direct midday sun, watering only once the top quarter to third of the soil has dried, a warm room between 16 and 29°C (60 and 85°F), and a loose, fast-draining mix. Get those right and a Monstera deliciosa grows quickly indoors, often to 2 to 2.5 m (6 to 8 ft) with a support to climb. Most problems people run into trace back to soil that stays wet too long or a spot that is too dark.

## Monstera care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Bright, indirect light. Morning or late sun is fine; no direct midday sun |
| Water | When the top quarter to third of the pot is dry, then water until it drains |
| Humidity | Moderate to high. Normal room air works; very dry winter air browns leaf edges |
| Temperature | 16 to 29°C (60 to 85°F). Keep it above 15°C (59°F) |
| Soil | 1 part bark, 1 part peat or coir, 1 part perlite, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Balanced liquid fertiliser once a month, spring to late summer |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats and dogs (insoluble calcium oxalate crystals) |

The figures for light, water and temperature come from [NC State Extension's plant profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/). Monstera is native from Mexico to Panama, where it starts on the forest floor and climbs up tree trunks toward the light. That habit explains most of what it wants indoors.

## How much light does a monstera need?

It wants as much light as you can give it without direct sun hitting the leaves at midday. NC State describes the ideal as "moderate brightness but not direct sunlight." In practice that means close to a bright window: right beside an east-facing window, or a little back from a south- or west-facing one, or behind a sheer curtain.

Signs it needs more light:

- New leaves come out smaller than the previous ones and have no splits.
- Stems stretch long between leaves and lean hard toward the window.
- The soil takes more than two weeks to dry after watering.

Signs of too much direct sun are pale, bleached patches or dry brown scorch marks on the leaves facing the window. Variegated cultivars such as 'Thai Constellation' need more light than plain green ones, because the white parts of the leaf do not photosynthesise. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot by its window direction.

## How often should you water a monstera?

Water when the top quarter to third of the soil is dry. In a 20 cm (8 in) pot, that is roughly the top 5 to 7 cm (2 to 3 in). Push a finger or a wooden chopstick into the soil to check. When it is dry to that depth, water slowly until water runs out of the drainage hole, then empty the saucer after 15 minutes.

In a bright, warm room that often works out to about once a week in summer and every two to three weeks in winter, but the soil is the guide, not the calendar. The Royal Horticultural Society advises keeping it only just moist in winter, when growth slows. A larger pot, a darker room or a cooler temperature all stretch the interval. Our guide on [how often to water a monstera](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-monstera) goes through those factors one by one.

Overwatering shows as yellowing lower leaves, soil that smells sour and stays wet for a week or more, and sometimes dark, soft stem bases. Underwatering shows as drooping, curling leaves and crisp brown edges, with soil pulling away from the side of the pot. A thirsty monstera perks up within a day of a good watering; an overwatered one does not.

## Humidity and temperature

Monstera does best in moderate to high humidity, but it adapts to normal household air. You will see the effect of very dry air as brown, papery leaf edges, usually in winter when the heating is on. A room humidifier is the only method that raises humidity by a useful amount. A tray of wet pebbles under the pot helps a little, right around the plant. Misting the leaves does almost nothing, because the water evaporates within minutes.

Keep it between 16 and 29°C (60 and 85°F), and above 15°C (59°F) in winter. Cold draughts from a door or a window left open in winter can mark the leaves. Keep it away from radiators and the direct blast of air conditioning, which dry the leaves out.

## Best soil for a monstera

Monstera roots need air as much as water. A dense, peat-only potting compost stays wet in the middle of the pot for too long. A chunky aroid mix fixes that. The Cornell epiphytic mix, which [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-soil-mixes/) lists as suitable for Monstera, is equal parts:

- fine bark (orchid bark works)
- peat moss or coconut coir
- perlite

If you buy a bag instead, an "aroid" or "tropical houseplant" mix is close to this. You can also stretch an ordinary indoor potting mix by adding about one third bark and one third perlite. Use a pot with a drainage hole, and do not add a gravel layer at the bottom: it raises the wet zone instead of draining it. Our [guide to the best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains why.

## When and how to repot a monstera

Repot in spring, every one to two years for a young plant, when roots circle the bottom of the pot or push out of the drainage hole. Move up only one size, about 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider than the old pot. A pot much larger than the root ball holds water the roots cannot use, and that is how root rot starts.

1. Water the day before so the root ball slides out in one piece.
2. Tease apart circling roots and cut off any that are brown and soft.
3. Set the plant at the same depth it was growing before.
4. Fill around it with fresh mix, firm it lightly, and water until it drains.

A large, mature plant that you cannot repot easily can stay in its pot. Scrape off the top 5 cm (2 in) of old soil each spring and replace it with fresh mix. See our [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) for more detail.

## Why does my monstera have no holes in its leaves?

The splits and holes, called fenestrations, only appear on mature leaves. A young monstera makes solid, heart-shaped leaves first, and that is normal. Once the plant is older and has enough light, each new leaf tends to be bigger and more divided than the last.

If an older plant keeps producing small, solid leaves, light is the usual reason. Move it closer to the window. Giving it something to climb probably helps too: in the wild, leaves get larger as the stem climbs, and many growers see the same indoors once the plant is attached to a moss pole. NC State notes that indoor plants need sturdy support to stop the stems from breaking. Our guide on [making a moss pole](/en/blog/how-to-make-a-moss-pole) shows how to build one.

## What to do with aerial roots

The thick brown roots growing from the stem are aerial roots. In the forest they grip tree bark and take in moisture. Indoors you can guide them into the soil, tie them to a moss pole, or leave them hanging. If they are in the way, you can cut a few off without harming the plant. They are also a useful marker when you take cuttings, because a node with an aerial root roots quickly.

## Feeding and pruning

Feed once a month from spring to late summer with a balanced liquid fertiliser at the strength on the label, as the RHS recommends. Stop in autumn and winter, when the plant is barely growing. Too much fertiliser builds up as salt in the soil and burns leaf edges.

Prune in spring. Cut just above a node to control height or remove a leggy stem. Wipe the leaves with a damp cloth every few weeks; dust cuts the light they get.

## Common monstera problems

- **Yellow lower leaves:** usually soil kept too wet. Let it dry further between waterings and check the roots if it keeps happening.
- **Brown, crispy edges:** soil left too dry, very dry air, or fertiliser build-up.
- **Drooping:** dry soil if the pot feels light; root damage if the soil is wet.
- **Pests:** scale insects, spider mites and mealybugs. Check the undersides of leaves and the leaf stalks when you water.

Each of these has its own diagnosis and fix in our article on [monstera problems](/en/blog/monstera-problems).

## Is monstera toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/cutleaf-philodendron) lists Monstera deliciosa as toxic to dogs and cats. The leaves and stems contain insoluble calcium oxalate crystals. Chewing them causes intense burning in the mouth, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. NC State notes that serious effects only follow eating large amounts, but keep it out of reach of pets that chew plants. The sap can also irritate skin, so wear gloves when you cut it.

## How to propagate a monstera

Monstera roots easily from stem cuttings taken in spring or summer.

1. Find a node: the slightly swollen ring on the stem where a leaf stalk joins it, often with a short aerial root.
2. Cut 2 to 3 cm (1 in) below the node with a clean blade, keeping one or two leaves on the cutting.
3. Stand the cutting in a jar of water with the node submerged, or bury the node in moist sphagnum moss.
4. Keep it warm and in bright, indirect light. Change the water weekly.
5. Pot it into the bark and perlite mix once the new roots are 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) long, usually after a few weeks.

A cutting without a node will not grow a new plant, even if it roots.
