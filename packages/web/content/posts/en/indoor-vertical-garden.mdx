An indoor vertical garden works when three things match: plants that share the same light and water needs, a wall that gets real daylight or 14 to 16 hours of grow light, and a system that holds water without letting it reach the wall. Most indoor walls fail on light, because a spot a few metres from a window gets a fraction of what it looks like. Start small, with one panel or a few wall pots, and grow it once the plants settle.

## Which indoor vertical garden system should you choose?

The [Royal Horticultural Society's guide to green walls](https://www.rhs.org.uk/garden-features/green-walls) describes living wall systems built from planter boxes or felt pockets, which often need some form of irrigation, and notes that green walls can be installed indoors with added artificial lighting. For smaller budgets it suggests simple wall bags or one or more small panels that you can add to over time. At home, the choice comes down to four types:

| System | How it works | Watering | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wall-mounted pots or shelves | Each plant stays in its own pot on a rail, bracket or shelf | Lift each pot down and water it at the sink | Beginners, rented homes, mixed plants |
| Fabric or felt pockets | Plants sit in mix inside pockets sewn onto a backing | Pour slowly into the top row; water runs down | Cheap, quick coverage of a wall |
| Modular panels with a reservoir | Plastic cells clip into a frame, with a tank or wicks behind | Fill the reservoir; check the cells by hand | A tidy, dense wall with less weekly work |
| Trellis or moss pole with climbers | One or two large plants trained up a support | Water the pot at the base like any houseplant | The lowest-maintenance green surface |

Wall-mounted pots are the easiest to get right. Each plant keeps its own mix and drainage, a struggling plant can come down without disturbing the others, and nothing drips on the wall if each pot has a saucer or a sealed outer pot. Felt pockets cover the most surface for the money but dry out fast and leak if overfilled. Panels with a reservoir cost more and suit someone who wants a dense wall and is willing to learn its quirks.

## How much light does a living wall need?

More than most walls get. Light falls off quickly with distance from a window, and a wall beside or opposite a window sits well back from it. The [University of Illinois Extension houseplant lighting guide](https://extension.illinois.edu/houseplants/lighting) gives rough distances for each light level:

| Light level | North window | East or west window | South window |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | About 1 m (a few feet) away | 1 to 3 m (3 to 10 ft) away | 4.5 to 6 m (15 to 20 ft) away |
| Medium | Directly in front | About 1 m (a few feet) away | 1 to 3 m (3 to 10 ft) away |
| Bright | Rarely enough | Directly in front | Up to 1.5 m (5 ft) away |

Those figures are for the northern hemisphere; swap north and south below the equator. A wall usually spans more than one of these zones. The part nearest the window gets the most light, so put the plants that need it there, and the shade-tolerant ones at the far end or the bottom, where furniture and the window frame cut the light. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot by its shadows.

### When do you need grow lights?

If the wall is further from a window than the low-light distances above, or the plants start stretching toward the window with small, pale new leaves, add a grow light. Illinois Extension says plants in low light benefit from a longer day, that 14 to 16 hours is enough, and that plants should never get more than 16 hours because they need a rest period. It also notes that cool white fluorescent tubes work well for foliage plants. An LED strip or bar mounted above the wall and pointing down across the plants, on a plug-in timer set to about 14 hours, is the simplest setup. Keep in mind that day length affects flowering in some plants, as Illinois Extension notes, so a flowering plant may behave differently under a long artificial day.

## Which plants grow well on a vertical garden?

The RHS advises choosing plants with similar light and moisture needs, so that one watering routine suits the whole wall. That rule matters more than any list of species. Pick one light level for the wall, then choose within it:

| Light on the wall | Plants that fit | Watering style |
|---|---|---|
| Low to medium | Pothos, heartleaf philodendron, peperomia, spider plant, ferns | Most like the mix to dry a little at the top before watering; ferns like it evenly moist |
| Medium to bright | Hoya, string of hearts, tradescantia, rhipsalis | Let the mix dry further between waterings |
| Bright to direct, or strong grow lights | Herbs, succulents | Herbs need steady moisture; succulents need to dry out fully |

Avoid mixing groups in the same pocket system, where water flows from one plant to the next. A fern and a succulent sharing a wall of felt pockets means one of them gets the wrong amount of water every time. Trailing plants are the obvious choice because they cover the structure as they grow; our list of the [best trailing plants for hanging baskets](/en/blog/best-trailing-plants-for-hanging-baskets) works for walls too. Herbs need far more light than a typical living room wall gives; our [indoor herb garden guide](/en/blog/indoor-herb-garden) covers what they need.

If you have cats or dogs, check each plant's toxicity before it goes up. Several popular trailing plants are toxic to pets, and their stems hang within reach.

## How do you build a vertical garden?

1. **Choose the wall.** Check its light first, using the table above. Avoid walls above or beside a radiator: the [RHS houseplant growing guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/houseplants/growing-guide) warns that being close to a radiator can dry the leaves and brown their edges.
2. **Check what the wall can hold.** Wet mix is heavy. Fix the frame or brackets into studs or solid masonry with fixings rated for the load, not into plasterboard alone. Follow the system maker's weight figures.
3. **Protect the wall.** Leave an air gap behind pocket and panel systems, add a waterproof backing sheet if the system does not include one, and fit a drip tray or gutter along the bottom.
4. **Plant small.** The RHS suggests introducing plants as small as possible, ideally as plugs, because they establish better at that stage. Small plants also fit pockets and cells without root damage. Use a light, free-draining houseplant mix.
5. **Plant in spring if you can.** The RHS notes spring is the best time, because plants are coming into active growth and establish quickly.
6. **Water in and wait.** Water each plant well after planting, then leave the wall for a week or two before adding more. Note which plants dry out first.

## How do you water a vertical garden?

Water drains down a wall. In pocket and panel systems, the top row dries first and the bottom row stays wettest, sometimes for days. Check the mix with a finger at the top, the middle and the bottom before you water, and water by zone rather than all at once.

- **Wall pots.** Take each pot down and water it at the sink until water runs from the drainage hole, let it drain, then hang it back. This is the most work and the most reliable.
- **Felt pockets.** Pour slowly into the top row so the water soaks in rather than running over, and stop as soon as the tray starts to catch drips. Top up the top row more often than the bottom.
- **Reservoir panels.** Fill the tank to the line and check that the wicks or cells are actually moist. A full reservoir does not mean every cell is getting water.

A wall full of drooping plants with dry mix needs water; yellowing leaves and mix that stays wet low down point to too much. Our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) covers the signs in detail. For trips away, a reservoir system or a pot-by-pot routine is easier to cover; our guide on [how to water plants while on vacation](/en/blog/how-to-water-plants-while-on-vacation) compares the options.

## How do you keep a vertical garden healthy?

- **Rotate where you can.** In wall pots, swap plants between the top and the bottom every month or two so none sits in the dimmest corner for good.
- **Trim trailing stems** before they shade the plants below. Cuttings can fill gaps elsewhere on the wall.
- **Feed lightly in the growing season.** A liquid houseplant fertiliser at half the label strength once a month, from spring to late summer, is enough for most foliage plants in small pockets. Stop in winter.
- **Expect winter to slow everything.** The RHS houseplant guide warns that too much warmth with low winter daylight forces weak, unhealthy growth. Water less and do not feed until spring.
- **Check for pests.** Plants packed together share pests quickly. Check the undersides of leaves when you water, and treat a problem plant away from the wall. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers the usual ones.

The RHS guide also notes that grouping plants raises the humidity around them, because their leaves give off moisture. A dense wall helps humidity-loving plants like ferns a little, though not enough to replace a humidifier in a heated room in winter.

## Quick answers

**Will a vertical garden damage my wall?** Not if water never touches it. Use an air gap or waterproof backing, a drip tray, and fixings rated for the wet weight.

**Do I need an irrigation system?** No. Wall pots and small pocket systems are easy to water by hand. The RHS notes that larger living walls usually need irrigation, often installed by specialists.
