Houseplant fungal diseases are rarer than most people fear, and most of them start with the same mistake: too much water. Root rot from soggy soil is by far the most common one indoors. Powdery mildew, grey mould and leaf spots come next. All of them are easier to prevent than to cure, because very few fungicides are approved for use on plants indoors. This guide shows how to recognise each disease, what to do about it, and how to stop it coming back.

## Why do houseplants get fungal diseases?

Fungal spores are everywhere, in the air, in potting mix and on new plants. They only cause disease when conditions suit them. Indoors, those conditions are usually:

- **Wet potting mix** that stays wet for days, which suffocates roots and lets rot fungi take hold.
- **Still, humid air** around crowded plants, which suits grey mould.
- **Stressed plants**, for example after drought, a cold draught or a move. A weakened plant is easier to infect.
- **Dead leaves and flowers** left on the plant or the soil, where fungi feed and multiply.

Fix the conditions and most problems stop spreading. Treating the symptoms without changing them rarely works for long.

## How do you recognise root rot?

Root rot is the most common and most serious fungal problem in houseplants. [Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/article/2005/2-11-2005/houseplantdis.html) notes that root and crown rots, caused by *Pythium*, *Phytophthora* and other fungi, can affect nearly all indoor plants.

The signs above the soil look like thirst:

- Leaves wilt, droop or turn yellow, even though the soil is wet.
- Growth stops, and lower leaves drop.
- The base of the stem turns soft, dark or mushy.

The proof is below the soil. Slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are firm and white or pale. Iowa State describes rotted roots as black or brown, with an outer layer that strips off easily and leaves a thin core behind. Rotting roots often smell sour.

Iowa State notes that warm, wet conditions favour these rots, and that stressed plants are more susceptible. Its advice is clear: avoiding overwatering is perhaps the most important step of all.

**What to do:** cut off every soft, dark root with clean scissors, shake off the old wet mix, and repot into fresh, fast-draining mix in a pot with a drainage hole. Water lightly and let the mix dry more than usual before the next watering. If the stem base itself has rotted, take healthy cuttings and start again. Our guide to [root rot recovery](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) walks through each step, and [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) helps you tell the two apart before it gets that far.

## What is the white powder on my plant's leaves?

That is almost always powdery mildew. Iowa State describes it as a white, dusty growth on the leaf surface, in round spots that merge or cover the whole leaf. Badly affected leaves can twist. Jade plant, African violet, begonia and kalanchoe are among the houseplants it lists.

Powdery mildew behaves differently from most fungi:

- It does not need wet leaves. The [Royal Horticultural Society](https://www.rhs.org.uk/disease/powdery-mildews) explains that humid air helps the spores infect, but water on the leaf surface does not.
- Plants under drought stress are more prone to it, so a plant that is often left too dry is a likely target.
- Each powdery mildew species attacks a narrow range of plants. One infected plant does not mean every plant in the room is at risk.
- Iowa State adds that cool conditions and cold draughts favour it.

**What to do:** remove the worst affected leaves and improve air movement around the plant. Keep the plant evenly watered, so it is not stressed by drought, and avoid heavy doses of high-nitrogen fertiliser, which the RHS notes make plants more prone to infection. A badly infected plant may not be worth saving. Iowa State notes that most fungicides for powdery mildew are not labelled for indoor plants.

Skip the homemade sprays. Baking soda and vinegar mixtures are widely shared online, but the RHS does not recommend homemade remedies.

## What is the fuzzy grey mould on leaves and flowers?

Fuzzy grey-brown growth on dying flowers, buds or leaves is grey mould, caused by the fungus *Botrytis cinerea*. The [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/disease/grey-mould) notes that it usually enters through a wound or attacks a stressed plant, though it can also infect healthy plants in humid air. Buds and flowers often shrivel and die. Cyclamen, pelargonium and primula are among the many plants it affects.

**What to do:**

1. Remove dead and dying leaves, buds and flowers promptly. The RHS stresses hygiene as the main control.
2. Handle infected parts gently. Spores scatter easily, so slip a bag over the affected part before you cut it off.
3. Clear dead plant material from the soil surface.
4. Space plants out and improve ventilation to lower humidity around them.

The RHS notes that no fungicides are approved for grey mould for home gardeners, so prevention is the only reliable tool.

## What causes brown or black spots on leaves?

Spots with a darker edge, or a yellow ring around them, can be fungal leaf spot. Spots that look water-soaked or greasy may be bacterial instead. The two are hard to tell apart without a lab, and the response is the same. Leaf spots spread through water splashing from leaf to leaf, and they grow on leaves that stay wet for a long time.

**What to do:** remove spotted leaves, water the soil rather than the leaves, and water in the morning so any wet leaves dry before night. Stop misting. Before you blame a fungus, check the simpler causes. Crisp brown tips and edges usually point to dry air, salts or uneven watering, and pale scorched patches to direct sun. Our guide to [plant leaves turning yellow](/en/blog/plant-leaves-turning-yellow) covers these causes.

## What about black sooty coating on leaves?

A black, dusty layer you can rub off with a damp cloth is sooty mould. It does not infect the plant. It grows on honeydew, the sticky liquid that sap-sucking pests such as scale, mealybugs and aphids leave on the leaves. Wipe it off, then find and treat the pests. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) shows how to identify them.

The small black flies around damp soil are not a fungus either. Fungus gnats are insects whose larvae live in wet potting mix. They are a sign the soil is staying too wet.

## How do you prevent houseplant fungal diseases?

Three habits prevent most problems.

1. **Water by the soil, not the calendar.** Check the mix before every watering, use pots with drainage holes and empty saucers after watering. Iowa State calls avoiding overwatering perhaps the most important step against root rot.
2. **Keep the air moving.** Space plants so leaves do not touch, and keep them out of stagnant corners. Water in the morning, as Iowa State suggests, so leaves dry during the day. Our guide on [how to increase humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) covers ways to add humidity without wet leaves.
3. **Keep things clean.** Inspect new plants before you buy them and keep them apart from your collection for a week or two. Remove dead leaves and flowers. Clean pots and tools before reusing them, and use fresh potting mix for each repotting.

If you keep many plants, watering by habit is where problems start. [Lily](/en) schedules watering per plant and lengthens the gap in winter, when soil dries slowly and rot is most likely.

## When should you throw a plant away?

Discard the plant, or take healthy cuttings and discard the rest, when:

- Rot has reached the stem base and the stem is soft.
- Almost every root is brown and mushy.
- Powdery mildew or grey mould covers most of the plant and keeps returning.

Throw the potting mix away rather than reusing it, and wash the pot in hot, soapy water before you plant anything else in it.
