Here is how to make a moss pole: wrap damp sphagnum moss around a sturdy stake, tie it on firmly with twine, and push the stake to the bottom of the pot next to your plant. The whole job takes about half an hour and costs far less than a ready-made pole. A moss pole gives climbing houseplants something to grip and grow up, as they would climb a tree trunk in the wild. In return, many of them grow bigger leaves and a more upright, compact shape.

## Which plants need a moss pole?

Moss poles suit climbing aroids that hold on with aerial roots:

- **Monstera** (*Monstera deliciosa*). [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/) notes that sturdy support is necessary to stop the heavy stems from breaking.
- **Pothos** (*Epipremnum aureum*). NC State notes that pothos climbs by its brown aerial roots, and that with support and enough light it produces much larger, mature leaves.
- **Climbing philodendrons**, such as heartleaf philodendron and the large climbing species.
- **Arrowhead plant** (*Syngonium*), once it starts to vine.

Plants that do not climb, such as snake plants, peace lilies and most ferns, gain nothing from a pole. Trailing plants such as string of hearts are meant to hang.

## Why use moss instead of a plain stake?

A bare bamboo cane or plastic stake holds a plant up, but its aerial roots cannot grip it, so you tie every stem. Damp moss gives the aerial roots something to grow into. Once they anchor, the plant holds itself up and the ties can come off. A climbing plant that grips its support also tends to grow larger leaves as it climbs, which is the main reason growers use poles.

The moss only helps if it stays damp. A dry pole works like a plain stake.

## What you need

- **A stake for the core.** A wooden stake, a thick bamboo cane, or a length of plastic pipe, about as tall as you want the pole plus the depth of the pot.
- **Sphagnum moss.** Long-fibre dried sphagnum, sold in bags at garden centres. Coir (coconut fibre) also works.
- **Twine.** Garden jute twine, or clear fishing line if you want the ties out of sight.
- **A bucket** for soaking the moss.
- **Soft plant ties** or Velcro tape to attach the stems.
- **Optional:** plastic garden mesh, if you prefer to build a moss-filled tube.

## How to make a moss pole, step by step

1. **Soak the moss.** Put the dried moss in a bucket of water for 15 to 20 minutes, until it is soft and heavy. Squeeze out the excess, so it is damp but not dripping.
2. **Mark the buried end.** Measure the depth of the pot on the stake and leave that section bare. Moss buried in the potting mix only rots.
3. **Lay on the moss.** Starting just above the bare section, press handfuls of damp moss around the stake in a layer about 2 to 3 cm (1 in) thick.
4. **Tie it on.** Tie the twine at the bottom, then wind it up the pole in a spiral, a few centimetres apart, pulling tight as you go. Add moss to fill any thin patches. Tie off at the top.
5. **Check it is firm.** Squeeze the pole along its length. The moss should not slide or bulge. Add another spiral of twine in the opposite direction if it does.
6. **Trim the stray strands** so the pole looks tidy.

**The mesh version.** Roll a strip of plastic garden mesh into a tube, close it with cable ties, and stuff it with damp moss. A mesh tube holds more moss and stays damp longer, and you can extend it later by adding a second tube on top.

## How to put a moss pole in the pot

The best time is when you repot, in spring. Putting the pole in an empty pot first, then adding mix and the plant, avoids stabbing through the root ball.

1. Stand the pole in the empty pot, against the side the plant will lean towards, with the bare end touching the bottom.
2. Add a little potting mix, then set the plant in the pot with its stems against the pole.
3. Fill in around the root ball and firm the mix well, so the pole cannot wobble.

To add a pole to a plant that is already potted, push it in on the side away from the thickest stems, slowly, and stop if you hit heavy resistance. A pot heavy enough to hold the pole and the plant without tipping matters too. Our guide on [spring repotting](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) covers moving up a pot size at the same time.

## How to attach the plant to the pole

1. **Find the aerial roots.** On monstera and pothos they grow from the stem at each leaf joint, on the side opposite the leaf.
2. **Turn the stem** so the aerial roots face the pole and the leaves face out towards the light.
3. **Tie the stem loosely** to the pole just below a leaf, with soft ties or Velcro. Leave room for the stem to thicken.
4. **Push the aerial roots into the moss** where you can. They grip much faster when they are already touching it.

As the plant grows, tie each new section the same way. Once the roots have anchored a section, you can remove its ties.

## How to keep a moss pole damp

The aerial roots only grow into moss that stays damp. Misting wets the surface for a few minutes and does little for the inside. Instead:

- **Water the pole itself** each time you water the plant. Pour water slowly onto the top of the pole so it soaks down through the moss.
- **Use a small watering bottle with a long spout** to reach the top of a tall pole.

Water running down the pole ends up in the pot, so check the potting mix is not staying soggy. Our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) helps you tell. Give the plant bright, indirect light as well. NC State notes that pothos grows its larger, mature leaves only with both support and enough light.

## What to do when the plant outgrows the pole

When the top of the plant reaches the top of the pole, you have two options:

- **Extend the pole.** Tie or screw a second moss pole, or a second mesh tube, to the top of the first.
- **Cut the top off.** Take the top section as a cutting, root it, and replant it in the same pot. The plant branches below the cut and stays a manageable height.
