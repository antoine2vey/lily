Chinese evergreen care comes down to warmth and restraint with water: medium to bright indirect light, a drink only once the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of soil has dried, and a room that stays above 15°C (60°F). Aglaonema copes with dim corners and dry office air better than most houseplants. What it handles badly is cold, and soil that stays wet for days.

## Chinese evergreen care at a glance

| Need | What to give it |
|---|---|
| Light | Medium to bright indirect light. Tolerates low light but grows slowly there |
| Water | When the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) is dry. Less often in winter |
| Humidity | 40 to 60% is comfortable. Normal room air works; very dry air browns the tips |
| Temperature | 21 to 27°C (70 to 80°F) by day. Keep it above 15°C (60°F) |
| Soil | Peat or coir potting mix with about a quarter perlite or fine bark, in a pot with a drainage hole |
| Feeding | Balanced liquid fertiliser once a month, spring and summer |
| Toxicity | Toxic to cats, dogs and horses (insoluble calcium oxalate crystals) |

The light, water and temperature figures follow [NC State Extension's Aglaonema profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/aglaonema/). Chinese evergreens grow wild on the floor of tropical forests in Southeast Asia, under a tree canopy. That is why they accept less light than most houseplants and why they dislike cold.

## How much light does a Chinese evergreen need?

Medium to bright indirect light gives the best growth: a spot a metre or two (3 to 6 ft) back from an east- or west-facing window, or near a north-facing one. NC State lists filtered sun and bright indirect light as ideal, and deep shade as tolerated.

Low light is where this plant earns its reputation. It will hold on in a hallway or an office lit mostly by ceiling lights. The cost is slow growth and fewer new leaves. The pink, red and silver cultivars sold today usually keep their colour better toward the brighter end of the range.

Avoid direct midday or afternoon sun. It leaves pale, bleached patches or dry brown scorch marks on the leaves facing the window. If the plant leans hard toward the light, turn the pot a quarter turn every week or two. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge a spot by its window.

## How often should you water a Chinese evergreen?

Water when the top 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of soil is dry. Push a finger or a wooden chopstick into the pot to check. When it is dry to that depth, water slowly until water runs out of the drainage hole, and empty the saucer after 15 minutes. NC State advises keeping the mix moist from spring to autumn, letting it dry between waterings, and watering less in winter.

In a bright, warm room that often works out to once every 7 to 10 days in summer and every two to three weeks in winter. Treat those numbers as a starting point. A plant in low light uses water much more slowly, and a large pot dries more slowly than a small one. Use room-temperature water: NC State specifically warns against cold water for this plant.

The signs are easy to tell apart once you know them:

- **Too much water:** lower leaves turn yellow, the soil stays wet for more than a week, and stems may go soft at the base.
- **Too little water:** leaves droop and curl, tips go brown and crisp, and the pot feels light when you lift it.

A thirsty Chinese evergreen stands back up within a day of a good watering. An overwatered one does not.

## Humidity and temperature

Aglaonema prefers humid air but tolerates the drier air of a normal home. You will see the effect of very dry air as brown leaf tips and edges, usually in winter with the heating on. A room humidifier is the only method that raises humidity by a useful amount. Grouping plants together or standing the pot on a tray of wet pebbles helps a little, right around the leaves. Misting does almost nothing, because the water evaporates within minutes. Our guide to [increasing humidity for houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-increase-humidity-for-houseplants) compares the methods.

Temperature matters more than humidity for this plant. It grows best at 21 to 27°C (70 to 80°F) by day, and NC State notes it is intolerant of cold. Keep it above 15°C (60°F) all year. Cold glass, an open door in winter or a delivery van on a cold day can mark the leaves with dark, dull patches that do not recover. Keep it away from radiators and air-conditioning vents too, which dry the leaves out.

## Best soil and pot for a Chinese evergreen

Use a peat or coir-based indoor potting mix and open it up with about a quarter perlite or fine bark, so the middle of the pot does not stay wet. NC State notes the plant prefers slightly acidic soil (pH below 6.0) with good drainage, which most indoor potting mixes already provide. Always use a pot with a drainage hole. Our guide to the [best soil for houseplants](/en/blog/best-soil-for-houseplants) explains why a gravel layer at the bottom does not help.

Chinese evergreens grow slowly and do not mind being a little snug. Repot every two to three years in spring, or when roots circle the bottom of the pot. Move up only one size, 2 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider. A pot much larger than the root ball holds water the roots cannot use. The [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) walks through the steps.

## Feeding

Feed once a month in spring and summer with a balanced liquid fertiliser, at the strength on the label or weaker. Stop in autumn and winter, when growth slows. Too much fertiliser builds up as salt in the soil and burns the leaf tips, which is easy to confuse with dry air. If you see a white crust on the soil or the rim of the pot, flush the pot with plenty of plain water.

## Does a Chinese evergreen flower?

It can, but rarely indoors. According to NC State, a mature plant may bloom in late summer or early autumn with a small spathe and spadix, much like a peace lily's but greenish-white and less showy. Red berries can follow. NC State suggests removing the flowers and fruit so the plant puts its energy into leaves. There is no reliable trick to make it bloom. A plant that gets the brighter end of its light range and steady care is the one most likely to flower.

## Getting a Chinese evergreen through winter

Most Chinese evergreens that struggle do so in winter. Short days and cool rooms slow growth, so the soil dries much more slowly. Watering on the summer schedule then leaves the roots sitting in cold, wet mix, and the lower leaves yellow.

- Water only when the top 5 cm (2 in) is dry, which may mean every three weeks or longer.
- Move it closer to a window, but not against the glass.
- Keep it away from draughty doors and cold windowsills at night.
- Skip fertiliser until spring.

[Lily](/en) schedules watering for each plant and lengthens the interval as the days shorten, which is when this plant is most often overwatered.

If your plant comes out of winter looking tired, start in spring. Cut off fully yellow or brown leaves at the base with clean scissors. Slide the plant out of its pot and look at the roots. Firm, pale roots are healthy. Brown, mushy ones mean rot: trim them off and repot into fresh mix. Then move it into brighter indirect light and resume monthly feeding once new leaves appear.

## Common Chinese evergreen problems

- **Yellow lower leaves:** usually soil kept too wet, especially in winter. One old leaf yellowing now and then is normal.
- **Brown tips and edges:** dry air, irregular watering or fertiliser build-up.
- **Dark, dull patches:** cold damage from a draught or cold glass.
- **Pests:** mealybugs, spider mites, scale and aphids, which NC State lists for this plant. Check the leaf undersides and where the leaves join the stems.

Each of these has its own diagnosis and fix in our article on [Chinese evergreen problems](/en/blog/chinese-evergreen-problems).

## Is Chinese evergreen toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/chinese-evergreen) lists Chinese evergreen as toxic to dogs, cats and horses. The leaves contain insoluble calcium oxalate crystals. Chewing them causes pain and swelling of the mouth, tongue and lips, heavy drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. The sap can also irritate skin, so wear gloves when you cut it. If you need a plant that is safe around pets, see our list of [plants safe for cats](/en/blog/plants-safe-for-cats).

## How to propagate a Chinese evergreen

NC State lists division and stem cuttings. Division is the easiest, and spring repotting is the time to do it.

1. Slide the plant out of its pot and shake off loose soil.
2. Look for natural clumps, each with its own stem and roots.
3. Pull them apart by hand, or cut through the root ball with a clean knife.
4. Pot each piece at the same depth it was growing, in fresh mix, and water until it drains.

An old plant that has become tall and bare at the base can be cut back instead. Cut a stem 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) long, remove the lowest leaves, and stand it in water or moist potting mix in bright, indirect light. Pot it once the roots are about 5 cm (2 in) long. The stump left in the pot usually sprouts new leaves.
