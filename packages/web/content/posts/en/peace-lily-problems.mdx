Most peace lily problems come back to water. A peace lily that droops with dry soil is thirsty and usually stands back up within a few hours of watering. Yellow leaves most often mean the soil stays wet too long, or that it was left bone dry several times, and brown leaf tips most often come from fertiliser salts building up in the pot. Below, each symptom has its likely causes in order, how to tell them apart, and the fix.

## Peace lily problems: check the soil first

Before anything else, check two things: how wet the soil is 5 cm (2 in) down, and whether the pot has a drainage hole and an empty saucer. Those two checks narrow down almost every case.

## Why is my peace lily drooping?

**1. The soil is dry.** This is by far the most common reason. Peace lilies wilt sooner and more dramatically than most houseplants when they run short of water. Lift the pot: if it feels light and the soil is dry at 5 cm (2 in), that is the answer. Water slowly until water runs from the drainage hole. The leaves should rise again within a few hours to a day.

**2. The roots are rotting.** If the plant droops while the soil is wet, do not water it again. Roots sitting in soggy mix suffocate and rot, and rotten roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts as if it were dry. Smell the soil and look at the stem bases. A sour or rotten smell, or dark, soft stems at soil level, point to root rot. See the root rot section below.

**3. It is too cold or in a draught.** [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/peace-lily/) gives a comfortable range of 20 to 29°C (68 to 85°F) by day, with nights about 5°C (10°F) cooler, and advises avoiding temperatures below 16°C (60°F). A plant next to a window in winter, an outside door or an air-conditioning vent can droop even with good soil.

**4. It was just repotted or moved.** Some drooping for a few days after repotting is normal while the roots settle. Keep it out of direct sun and do not water again until the top of the soil starts to dry.

If the plant droops every few days, it has probably outgrown its pot. A root ball packed with roots dries out very fast. Move it up one pot size in spring.

## Why are my peace lily leaves turning yellow?

**1. Old leaves are dying off.** One or two of the oldest, outer leaves turning yellow now and then is normal. The plant replaces them from the centre. Cut them off at the base.

**2. The soil stays too wet.** Several leaves yellowing at once, starting low, with soil that is still damp a week after watering, means too much water. The cause is usually watering on a fixed schedule, a pot without a drainage hole, water left standing in the saucer, or a pot much larger than the root ball. Clemson warns that both a saucer of standing water and an oversized pot lead to root rot. Let the top few centimetres dry before the next watering. Our guide on [how often to water a peace lily](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-peace-lily) gives starting intervals by season.

**3. The soil dried out completely, more than once.** Clemson also notes that when the soil dries out too much, the plant wilts and the leaves and their edges turn yellow. If your plant collapses regularly before you water it, the yellowing is from those dry spells. Water a little sooner, when the top 2 to 3 cm (1 in) is dry.

**4. Too much direct sun.** Leaves that turn pale yellow or bleached on the side facing a window are getting more sun than they can take. Peace lilies want bright, indirect light, not direct sun.

**5. A nutrient shortage.** Yellow between the veins while the veins stay green is, according to Clemson, a sign of magnesium deficiency. This usually shows on a plant that has not been fed or repotted for a long time. Feed with a fertiliser that lists magnesium, at reduced strength, during spring and summer.

## Why does my peace lily have brown tips?

**1. Fertiliser salts.** Both Clemson and [NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/spathiphyllum/) name over-fertilising and salt build-up as a cause of burnt, brown leaf tips and roots. A white crust on the soil surface or the rim of the pot confirms it. NC State recommends feeding at one quarter of the label strength and flushing the soil between feeds. To flush, take the plant to a sink and run plain water through the pot for a minute or two, then let it drain completely. If your tap water is hard, it probably adds to the build-up, and flushing every few months helps.

**2. The soil dried out too far.** Brown, crisp edges that follow a dry spell come from water stress. They often appear a week or two after the plant wilted.

**3. Dry air.** NC State lists low humidity as a cause of brown tips. This is most likely in winter, near a radiator. A humidifier in the room helps; misting does not raise humidity for long.

**4. Direct sun.** Brown, dry patches on the leaves nearest a sunny window come from scorch rather than salts.

Brown tissue will not turn green again. Trim the brown tip with clean scissors, following the natural shape of the leaf, and leave a thin line of brown so you do not cut into healthy tissue. What matters is that new leaves come out clean.

## Why are there black or dark brown patches on the leaves?

**1. Sunburn.** Clemson notes that direct sun scorches the foliage. Scorch shows as dry, papery brown or black patches on the leaves facing the light. Move the plant back from the window.

**2. Cold damage.** NC State warns that long periods below 4°C (40°F) damage leaves, stems and roots. Shorter cold, such as a plant pressed against a cold window pane or carried home in winter, can leave dark, water-soaked patches that turn black a few days later. Cut damaged leaves off and keep the plant warm.

**3. Rot spreading from the roots.** Black, soft leaf bases and stems at soil level go with root rot. Check the roots.

## How do I know if my peace lily has root rot?

Root rot shows above ground as drooping on wet soil, several yellow leaves at once, and soft, dark stems at the base. To be sure, slide the plant out of its pot. Healthy roots are white or pale tan and firm. Rotten roots are brown to black, mushy, and the outer layer slips off when you pinch them. The soil often smells sour.

To treat it:

1. Remove the plant from its pot and shake or rinse off the old soil.
2. Cut off every soft, dark root with clean scissors, back to firm white tissue.
3. Remove any leaves that have collapsed or rotted at the base.
4. Repot in fresh, well-draining potting mix, in a clean pot with a drainage hole. Choose a pot only a little bigger than the remaining roots; Clemson suggests 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) wider than the old one when repotting.
5. Water once, let it drain fully, then wait until the top of the soil is dry before watering again.

If most of the roots are gone, divide the plant and keep only the crowns that still have healthy roots. ## What pests attack peace lilies?

Clemson notes that peace lilies have few insect pests. The ones you are most likely to see:

- **Mealybugs.** NC State calls them probably the most common insect indoors. Look for white, cottony clumps where leaves join the stems and along the leaf undersides. Dab each one with a cotton swab dipped in rubbing alcohol, then spray the plant with insecticidal soap, repeating every 7 to 10 days until none come back.
- **Fungus gnats.** Small black flies around the soil. Their larvae live in constantly wet soil. Let the top few centimetres dry between waterings and catch the adults on yellow sticky traps.
- **Spider mites.** Fine pale speckling on the leaves and thin webbing underneath, usually in warm, dry rooms. Rinse the leaves in the shower, then treat with insecticidal soap, covering the undersides.
- **Scale.** Small brown bumps stuck to stems and leaf undersides, often with sticky honeydew below. Scrape them off with a fingernail or a swab, then treat as for mealybugs.

Keep an infested plant away from your others until it is clean, and wipe the leaves with a damp cloth every few weeks: it removes dust and lets you spot pests early. Our article on [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers each one in more depth.

## Quick answers

**Is a peace lily toxic to cats and dogs?** Yes. The ASPCA lists it as toxic to both. It contains insoluble calcium oxalate crystals, which cause mouth pain, drooling, vomiting and difficulty swallowing. It is not a true lily: the [ASPCA's guidance for vets](https://www.aspcapro.org/node/99981) limits the risk of kidney injury in cats to true lilies (Lilium) and daylilies (Hemerocallis).

**Should I cut off yellow or brown leaves?** Yes. A fully yellow leaf will not recover. Cut it at the base, close to the soil. For brown tips only, trim the tip and keep the leaf.

**How do I stop these problems coming back?** Water by the soil rather than the calendar, use a pot with a drainage hole, feed lightly, and keep it in bright indirect light between 20 and 29°C (68 and 85°F). The full routine is in our [peace lily care guide](/en/blog/peace-lily-care-guide).
