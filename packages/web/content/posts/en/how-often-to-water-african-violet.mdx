How often to water an African violet depends on when the soil surface dries, not on a fixed day: in a small plastic pot that is roughly every 5 to 7 days in spring and summer and every 10 to 14 days in winter. Use room-temperature water, keep it off the leaves, and never leave the pot standing in water. Overwatering, not thirst, is what kills most African violets.

## How often to water an African violet, season by season

[NC State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/streptocarpus-ionanthus/) advises letting the surface dry before watering again and calls overwatering the number one issue for this plant. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/african-violet/) agrees that excessive watering is one of the most common problems, because African violets are very susceptible to crown and root rot.

These starting intervals are our own estimate for a standard plant in a 10 to 12 cm (4 to 5 in) plastic pot of African violet mix, in a room at 18 to 24°C (65 to 75°F). Use them as the day to check the soil, not the day to water.

| Season | Starting interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | Every 5 to 7 days | Longer days, steady growth and flowering |
| Summer | Every 5 to 7 days, sometimes sooner in a warm room | Fastest drying of the year |
| Autumn | Every 7 to 10 days | Shorter days, slower growth |
| Winter | Every 10 to 14 days | Low light; the soil stays damp much longer |

A plant grown under lights for 12 hours or more a day grows at a steady rate all year, so its interval changes less with the seasons.

## How to tell when an African violet needs water

- **Touch the surface.** When the top of the mix feels dry to your fingertip, it is time. If it still feels cool and damp, wait a day or two.
- **Lift the pot.** African violet pots are small and light, so the difference between wet and dry is easy to feel after a few waterings.
- **Look at the colour.** Most peat-based mixes go from dark brown to a lighter brown as they dry.
- **Do not wait for wilting.** A violet that droops with dry soil will usually recover after watering, but repeated wilting stresses it and stops flowering. A violet that droops with wet soil is a warning sign of crown rot (see below).

## How to water an African violet

Clemson describes two methods, and both work.

**From below.** Stand the pot in a saucer or tray with about 2.5 cm (1 in) of water. When the soil surface becomes moist, lift the pot out and discard the leftover water. This keeps water off the hairy leaves and the crown, which is why NC State recommends it.

**From above.** Pour water onto the soil, not the leaves, until it saturates the mix and drains from the bottom. Empty the saucer afterwards. Watering from the top now and then also flushes out fertiliser salts that build up when you only water from below.

**Use room-temperature water.** Clemson warns that cold water touching the leaves causes white blotches and yellow or white ring and line patterns. Fill your watering can the night before so the water reaches room temperature.

**Wick watering.** Many growers keep violets on a wick: a synthetic cord runs from the bottom of the pot into a reservoir, and the mix draws up water as it dries. This works best in a very light, airy mix; in a dense mix the soil stays too wet. If you use a wick, refill the reservoir before it runs dry and watch for the overwatering signs below.

## What changes how often you water

**Pot material.** Clemson notes that clay pots need more frequent watering than plastic because water evaporates through their walls. It also warns that salts collecting on the rim of a clay pot can kill leaf stems resting on it.

**Pot size.** African violets are usually grown in small pots, and a pot that is too big stays wet in the centre. A common rule is a pot about one third the width of the leaf rosette. Our guide on [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) explains why oversized pots stay wet.

**The mix.** NC State recommends 2 or 3 parts sphagnum peat moss to 2 parts vermiculite and 1 part perlite, a blend that lets water pass through quickly. Ordinary potting soil holds more water and dries more slowly.

**Light and warmth.** Clemson gives 18 to 21°C (65 to 70°F) at night and up to 27 to 29°C (80 to 85°F) by day as the best temperatures. A violet in a warm, bright window drinks far more than one in a cool room in winter.

**Humidity.** NC State says the plant does best in high humidity and suggests a tray of wet pebbles or a humidifier. Humid air slows how fast the soil dries.

## Signs of an overwatered African violet

Clemson's [African violet diseases factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/african-violet-diseases-insect-pests/) describes crown and root rot, caused by water moulds when plants are watered excessively, have poor drainage or are planted too deeply. Its symptoms:

- Older leaves droop.
- Young leaves in the centre of the plant look stunted, then turn black and die.
- The crown, where the leaves meet the soil, turns soft and mushy.
- Roots turn soft and mushy.

If you catch it early, with only the outer leaves limp, let the mix dry until the surface is clearly dry before watering again, and make sure the pot drains. Once the crown is soft, the plant rarely recovers. Take a few healthy leaves as cuttings and start again in fresh mix. Our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) goes through the steps.

## Signs of an underwatered African violet

- The whole plant goes limp and the leaves feel soft, with dry, light soil.
- Flower buds dry up and drop before opening.
- Leaf edges turn brown and crisp after repeated dry spells.
- The mix shrinks away from the pot sides, so water poured on top runs straight out.

Bottom watering fixes a dried-out pot: leave it in the water a little longer, until the surface is moist, then drain it. The leaves should firm up within a day. If they do not, check the crown and roots. Our [overwatering vs underwatering guide](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) compares the two side by side.

## Watering and flowering

Watering alone will not make an African violet bloom. Clemson says lack of flowering is one of the most common problems and is generally due to inadequate light. Place the plant within about 1 m (3 ft) of a large east-, south- or west-facing window, out of hot afternoon sun. When you feed, Clemson suggests watering from above first so the fertiliser solution goes onto moist soil.
