Air-purifying plants do not measurably clean the air in a real home. A 2020 review of 12 chamber studies found that a typical potted plant removes so little that you would need between 10 and 1,000 plants per square metre of floor space (roughly 1 to 100 per square foot) to match what ordinary air leaking in and out of a building already does. Plants are worth growing for many reasons, but if your air quality worries you, open a window or buy an air purifier.

## Where the air-purifying plants idea came from

Most "best air-purifying plants" lists trace back to a NASA report from 1989, [Interior Landscape Plants for Indoor Air Pollution Abatement](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930073077) by B. C. Wolverton and colleagues. The researchers put plants in small, sealed chambers, added chemicals such as benzene and formaldehyde, and measured how fast the levels fell. They did fall. The report was aimed at closed environments such as space habitats, and it also proposed a filter that pushes air through activated carbon and the plants' roots.

The step from "a plant lowers benzene in a sealed box over many hours" to "a plant on your desk cleans your living room" was made later, mostly in articles and on plant labels, not by the study.

## What the newer research says

In 2020, Bryan Cummings and Michael Waring of Drexel University published [a review in the Journal of Exposure Science and Environmental Epidemiology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41370-019-0175-9). They took 196 results from 12 published chamber studies and converted them into the measure used for air purifiers, the clean air delivery rate: how many cubic metres of clean air a device delivers per hour.

The results:

- The median single plant delivered 0.023 m³ (about 0.8 cubic feet) of clean air per hour.
- A normal building already replaces its indoor air with outdoor air about once an hour, just through gaps, vents and doors.
- To match that, you would need 10 to 1,000 plants per square metre of floor space.

A 20 m² (215 sq ft) bedroom would need at least 200 plants to make a difference, and possibly 20,000. The authors concluded that research should move away from potted plants' ability to clean indoor air passively, and look instead at other benefits such as well-being.

## Do plants add oxygen or humidity to a room?

A little, but not enough to notice. The same reasoning applies: one plant's exchange of gases and water is tiny next to the volume of air in a room and the air that flows through it every hour. A plant also takes up oxygen at night. If a room feels stuffy, the cause is usually too little ventilation, which a plant cannot fix.

## What actually improves indoor air

If you came here because of a real concern, such as a newly painted room, a gas cooker, damp or allergies, these help far more than plants:

- **Ventilate.** Open windows for a few minutes several times a day, and use the extractor fan when cooking and showering.
- **Remove the source.** Air out new furniture, keep solvents and paint in a shed, and do not smoke indoors.
- **Use an air purifier** sized for the room if you need to filter particles such as pollen or smoke.

None of this stops you from filling the room with plants. It only means you should pick them because you like them.

## The plants on "air-purifying" lists are still good houseplants

The same seven or eight names come up on every list, and most of them earned their place because they are tough, not because they clean anything. Here they are, with how easy they are and whether they are safe around pets, according to the [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/spider-plant) poison control database.

| Plant | Why it's on the list | Ease | Cats and dogs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Snake plant | Tolerates low light and missed waterings | Very easy | Toxic |
| Pothos | Fast trailing growth, forgiving | Very easy | Toxic |
| Spider plant | Fast-growing, makes plantlets you can pot up | Easy | Non-toxic |
| Peace lily | Flowers in modest light, droops when thirsty | Easy | Toxic |
| Parlour palm | Small palm that copes with indirect light | Moderate | Non-toxic |
| Boston fern | Full, arching fronds | Harder: needs steady moisture and humidity | Non-toxic |

A few notes on each:

**Snake plant.** The easiest plant on any list. Water only when the soil is fully dry, every one to two months in winter. Old articles say it "makes oxygen at night"; it does open its pores at night to save water, but the amount of oxygen is irrelevant to a bedroom. See our [snake plant care guide](/en/blog/snake-plant-care-guide).

**Pothos.** Grows in most indoor light and shows thirst by drooping. Keep it out of reach of pets that chew leaves. Our [pothos care guide](/en/blog/pothos-care-guide) covers the details.

**Spider plant.** A safe choice for homes with cats and dogs, and one of the fastest to grow. Brown tips are common and mostly cosmetic.

**Peace lily.** Flowers in less light than most flowering plants and wilts dramatically when dry, then recovers within hours of watering. Our [peace lily care guide](/en/blog/peace-lily-care-guide) explains the rest.

**Parlour palm.** The ASPCA lists Chamaedorea elegans, sold as parlour palm and sometimes as bamboo palm, as non-toxic to cats and dogs.

**Boston fern.** Pet-safe but the hardest here, since it sheds fronds quickly in dry, heated air.

## Quick answers

**Did NASA prove that plants clean the air?** No. The 1989 study showed that plants and their potting soil lowered chemical levels in small sealed chambers. It did not test homes or offices with normal ventilation.

**How many plants do I need to purify a room?** Based on the 2020 review, at least 10 per square metre of floor, and possibly 1,000. In practice no number of houseplants replaces opening a window.

**Which plant purifies the air best?** Differences between species are small next to the gap between any plant and normal ventilation. Choose by light, care and pet safety instead.
