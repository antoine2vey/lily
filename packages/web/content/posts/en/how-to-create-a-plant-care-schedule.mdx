How to create a plant care schedule comes down to one shift: schedule when you check each plant, not when you water it. A workable plan has a soil check every 3 to 7 days, feeding once or twice a month from March to October, and repotting every 2 to 3 years, as the [RHS](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/types/houseplants/growing-guide) suggests. The rest of this guide shows how to build that list for your own plants and change it as the seasons turn.

## Why a fixed watering calendar fails

"Water every Sunday" feels organised, but plants do not drink on a calendar. [Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/houseplants/watering) says calendar schedules usually do not work and advises watering when a plant needs it. How fast a pot dries depends on the species, light, temperature, humidity, pot size, potting mix and pot material. The same plant can need water every 5 days in July and every 3 weeks in December.

A schedule still helps. It just needs to remind you to look, then let the soil decide.

## How to create a plant care schedule in five steps

### 1. List every plant and where it lives

Write down each plant, its pot size and its spot: south window, north window, back of the room, bathroom. Light is the biggest factor in how fast a pot dries, so two pothos in different rooms belong on different lines.

### 2. Group plants by how dry they like to get

Illinois Extension sorts houseplants into three rough groups. Use them as your columns:

- **Keep evenly moist, never soggy.** Peace lily, ferns, calathea. Check every 2 to 4 days.
- **Let the top dry between waterings.** Most foliage plants: pothos, monstera, philodendron, dracaena. Check every 5 to 7 days.
- **Let the soil dry right through.** Snake plant, ZZ plant, cacti and succulents. Check every 10 to 14 days.

These check intervals are starting points, not rules. Shorten them for small pots in bright windows and lengthen them for large pots in dim rooms.

### 3. Set check days, not water days

On a check day, push a finger into the soil. Illinois Extension suggests testing to about 5 cm (2 in). Water only the plants that are dry, and water those thoroughly until it runs from the drainage hole. Then empty the saucer. Lifting the pot works too: the RHS notes that a dry pot feels lighter than a wet one.

Grouping plants by check day keeps the routine short. One plant shelf, one look, two minutes.

### 4. Add the slower tasks

Watering is the daily habit; the rest happens far less often.

| Task | Starting frequency | When |
|---|---|---|
| Soil check | Every 2 to 14 days, by group | All year |
| Feeding | Once or twice a month | March to October |
| Pest check | Every check day, a quick look under the leaves | All year |
| Dusting leaves | Every month or two | All year |
| Turning the pot | A quarter turn every week or two | All year, for plants by a window |
| Repotting | Every 2 to 3 years | Spring |

Feeding follows the RHS: liquid feed once or twice a month during the growing season, or a quarter-strength dose at every watering, and never onto dry soil, which can burn roots. Our guide to [fertilising houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) covers types and doses.

Repotting also follows the RHS: every two to three years in spring, into a pot 5 to 10 cm (2 to 4 in) wider. A plant that dries out within a day or two and has stopped growing may be pot-bound sooner. Our [spring repotting guide](/en/blog/spring-repotting-guide) walks through it.

For pests, the RHS lists the usual indoor ones: fine webbing and pale speckling from spider mites, white cottony blobs from mealybugs, brown limpet-like scale, and tiny black fungus gnats. Catching them on a routine check is far easier than treating a full infestation. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) has photos of each.

### 5. Write it where you will see it

A schedule only works if it is in front of you. Paper on the fridge, a repeating calendar reminder or an app all work. Pick the one you already open every day.

## How to adjust a plant care schedule each season

Plan to revisit the schedule four times a year, when the light changes.

- **Spring.** Growth restarts. Shorten check intervals, start feeding again in March, and repot anything that needs it.
- **Summer.** Pots dry fastest. Small pots in bright windows may need checking every 2 or 3 days. Plants moved outdoors need checking after hot spells and can skip a watering after rain.
- **Autumn.** The change most people miss. Days shorten and the soil stays wet longer, but the summer habit carries on. Lengthen intervals as soon as growth slows.
- **Winter.** The [University of Wisconsin Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/keys-to-healthy-houseplants/) advises against feeding most houseplants in winter, when growth slows, and notes that heated indoor air pulls moisture from leaves and browns the tips. Check less often, keep plants away from radiators, and stop or cut back feeding. Our guide to [houseplant care in winter](/en/blog/houseplant-care-in-winter) covers light and heating.

## Keeping the schedule going

- **Tie it to a habit you already have.** Check plants while the kettle boils on the same mornings each week.
- **Note what you did.** A line per plant, such as "watered 3 Oct, top 5 cm dry", shows the real interval after a few weeks. Use that number to correct your starting guess.
- **Watch for drift.** If a plant needs water on every check day, check it more often. If it is never dry, check it less often.
- **Plan for trips.** A week away in a cool room is usually fine; the RHS says plants can go a week or two without water in winter. Summer needs more planning. See [how to water plants while on vacation](/en/blog/how-to-water-plants-while-on-vacation).

## Quick answers

**How often should I water houseplants?** It depends on the plant, the light and the season, which is why a check-based schedule works better than a fixed one. Our guide on [how often to water houseplants](/en/blog/how-often-to-water-houseplants) gives starting intervals by plant type.

**Should I water all my plants on the same day?** Check them on the same day if it helps you remember, but water only the ones that are dry.

**What time of day is best to water?** Morning is convenient and lets wet leaves dry before evening. The RHS advises against leaving foliage wet after dark in cool conditions, which favours grey mould and leaf spots.
