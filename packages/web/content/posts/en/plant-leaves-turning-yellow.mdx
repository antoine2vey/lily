Plant leaves turning yellow are a symptom, not a disease, and the cause is usually in the pot: too much water, too little, or compost that has run out of nutrients or filled up with salts. Before changing anything, check two things. Is the compost wet or dry right now? And which leaves are yellow: the oldest at the bottom, the newest at the top, or leaves all over the plant?

## Plant leaves turning yellow: read the pattern first

| What you see | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| Lower leaves yellow and soft, compost wet | Overwatering |
| Leaves yellow and crisp, compost dry and pulling away from the pot | Underwatering |
| Lower leaves yellow one by one, plant leaning or stretching, far from a window | Not enough light |
| Pale, bleached or scorched patches on the side facing the window | Too much direct sun |
| Brown tips, lower leaves dropping, white crust on the compost | Salt build-up |
| Whole plant paler, growth stunted, yellow or purple tints | Lack of nutrients |
| Many leaves yellow within days of a cold night or a move | Cold, draughts or shock |
| Fine yellow speckles, sticky leaves or webbing | Pests |
| One old leaf at a time, new growth healthy | Normal ageing |

Then work through the matching section below.

## Yellow leaves and wet soil: overwatering

[Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-watering/) calls overwatering the main cause of death of potted plants. Roots need oxygen as well as water. In compost that stays soaked they cannot breathe, start to rot, and stop taking up water. The lower leaves go yellow and soft first, often with brown patches, and the plant may wilt even though the compost is wet.

**How to tell:** the compost is damp days after watering, the pot feels heavy, and the yellow leaves are limp rather than crisp. A sour smell or soft brown roots mean root rot has started.

**Fix:** stop watering until the top few centimetres are dry. Empty any saucer or cachepot that holds water. Clemson warns that a wilting plant in wet compost is not asking for water, and watering it again can make things worse. If the roots are brown and mushy, follow our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery).

## Yellow, crispy leaves and dry soil: underwatering

A plant left dry for too long sheds leaves it cannot support. They yellow, then brown and turn crisp, often starting at the tips and edges. Clemson notes that the symptoms of over and underwatering are similar, because both damage the roots, so the compost is what tells them apart.

**How to tell:** the compost is dry well below the surface, the pot feels light, and the compost may have shrunk away from the sides.

**Fix:** water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes. Clemson suggests a double watering for compost that has dried hard: water once, then again half an hour later, or stand the pot in a sink of water until the surface is moist, then let it drain. Our guide to [overwatering vs underwatering](/en/blog/overwatering-vs-underwatering) compares the two in more detail.

## Lower leaves turning yellow in a dim spot: not enough light

A plant that cannot make enough energy drops the leaves it can least afford, usually the oldest ones at the bottom. Growth gets long and thin, new leaves come in smaller, and the plant leans towards the window. This often shows up in autumn and winter, when the same spot gets much less light.

[Clemson's indoor plant factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/indoor-plants-cleaning-fertilizing-containers-light-requirements/) notes that the light inside a well-lit home is often under 100 foot-candles (about 1,000 lux), while a sunny day outdoors can pass 10,000 foot-candles. A spot that looks bright to us can be dim for a plant.

**How to tell:** the compost and watering are fine, the plant is more than a couple of metres from a window, and the yellowing is slow and from the bottom up.

**Fix:** move the plant closer to a window or add a grow light. Water less while it is in low light, since it uses less. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to read a window.

## Pale, bleached patches: too much sun

Clemson notes that when a plant gets too much direct light, its leaves bleach or scald and sometimes die. This often happens after a plant is moved outdoors, or to a brighter window, without time to adjust. The damage is on the leaves facing the light and shows as pale yellow or whitish patches that later go brown and papery.

**Fix:** move the plant back from the glass or behind a sheer curtain. Clemson advises making any change in light gradual. Scorched patches will not recover, but new leaves will grow in normally.

## Brown tips and dropping lower leaves: salt build-up

Fertiliser and hard tap water leave salts in the compost. Clemson lists the symptoms of salt build-up: slower growth, brown leaf tips, lower leaves dropping and wilting. A white film on the compost or a white crust on the pot rim is the visible sign. Clemson also warns against watering with softened water, as the sodium in it can damage plants.

**Fix:** flush the pot. Pour plenty of water through the compost until it drains freely, empty the saucer, and feed less often afterwards. Repot into fresh compost if the crust keeps coming back.

## Pale, stunted growth: lack of nutrients

A plant that has sat in the same compost for a long time without feeding slowly uses up its nutrients. [University of New Hampshire Extension](https://extension.unh.edu/blog/2018/03/fertilizing-houseplants) notes that a stunted plant, or one with yellow or purple tints in its leaves, probably needs a balanced fertiliser.

**How to tell:** the yellowing is spread across the plant rather than limited to old leaves, growth has stalled, and watering and light are fine. Plants in low light need less feeding, so rule out light first.

**Fix:** feed with a balanced houseplant fertiliser at the label rate during the growing season, and only while the plant is growing. UNH adds that fertiliser alone may not revive a struggling plant, so check light and watering too. Our guide on [how to fertilize houseplants](/en/blog/how-to-fertilize-houseplants) covers timing and strength.

## Sudden yellowing after a cold night or a move

Many leaves yellowing and dropping within a few days usually points to a shock: a cold draught from a window or door, a plant left against cold glass in winter, a blast from a heater, or a move to a new home. Clemson advises keeping plants such as dieffenbachia away from cold and big swings in temperature, and moving spider plants about a metre (a few feet) back from windows in winter to keep them out of draughts.

**Fix:** move the plant to a steady, draught-free spot and leave it alone. Do not repot, feed or overwater a shocked plant. It will probably drop a few more leaves before new growth appears. Our guide to [acclimating new plants](/en/blog/how-to-acclimate-new-plants) covers the first weeks after bringing a plant home.

## Yellow speckles or sticky leaves: pests

Sap-sucking pests such as spider mites, mealybugs, scale and aphids drain leaves and leave yellow speckles or patches behind. Look under the leaves and where they meet the stems. Fine webbing points to spider mites, white cottony tufts to mealybugs, brown bumps to scale, and sticky leaves to scale or aphids.

**Fix:** isolate the plant, wipe or rinse off what you can, and treat. Our guide to [common houseplant pests](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) identifies each one and its treatment.

## One old leaf at a time: normal ageing

Every plant sheds its oldest leaves eventually. Clemson notes that as dieffenbachias mature, their lower leaves naturally drop to reveal a trunk-like stem. If one lower leaf yellows every few weeks while new leaves keep coming in green and healthy, there is nothing to fix.

## Should you cut off yellow leaves?

A leaf that has gone fully yellow will probably not turn green again. Once it is mostly yellow, cut it off at the base with clean scissors or let it fall on its own. Removing it does not cure the cause, but it keeps the plant tidy and makes it easier to see whether new leaves are healthy. Leave partly yellow leaves on the plant while you fix the cause; their green parts probably still feed it.

[Lily](/en) can help with the commonest cause: it schedules watering per plant and, indoors, adjusts the interval for the season's daylight.
