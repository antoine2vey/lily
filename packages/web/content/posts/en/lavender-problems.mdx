Most lavender problems come from wet roots, not from pests. Lavender grown in heavy or waterlogged soil gets root rot, which shows as yellowing, drooping and sudden death, while the few insects that feed on it usually cause only cosmetic damage. Give it full sun, free-draining soil and a fairly dry winter, and prune it once a year, and most of the trouble below never starts.

## Lavender problems: check these three things first

The [RHS lavender growing guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/lavender/growing-guide) calls lavender trouble-free when it grows in the conditions it likes. So before you look for a pest, check the basics.

- **Drainage.** Push a finger into the soil. Lavender sitting in wet, heavy or clay soil is the most common cause of decline.
- **Sun.** The RHS says lavender needs lots of sun and will not survive long in shady, damp or extremely cold conditions. NC State Extension puts full sun at 6 or more hours of direct light a day.
- **Type.** Some lavenders, such as French lavender (*Lavandula stoechas*), are less hardy than English lavender (*L. angustifolia*) and *L. × intermedia*. A tender type left outside in a cold, wet winter often dies.

| Symptom | Most likely cause | First thing to check |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow leaves | Wet roots or root rot | Is the soil wet days after rain or watering? |
| Drooping or wilting | Root rot, or drought in a pot | Is the soil wet or bone dry? |
| Grey or brown dieback after winter | Cold, wet roots, or a tender type | Which lavender is it, and where did it spend the winter? |
| Woody, split, bare in the middle | Age and no yearly pruning | When was it last trimmed? |
| Few flowers | Too little sun | Does it get 6 hours of direct sun? |
| Leaves eaten to stumps | Rosemary beetle | Shiny striped beetles or grey grubs on the shoots |
| Pale speckled leaves | Leafhoppers | Small jumping insects and white cast skins underneath |
| White froth on stems | Cuckoo spit | A small pale insect inside the froth |

## Why are my lavender leaves turning yellow?

**Cause 1, by far the most common: wet roots.** The RHS warns that lavender in wet or heavy soil can suffer root rots that lead to an early death. NC State also lists root rot and says it is caused by overwatering. The soil stays damp long after rain.

**Cause 2: planted at the wrong time.** The RHS advises never planting lavender in winter, when young plants are vulnerable to rotting in cold, wet soil. A plant set out in late autumn that yellows over winter has usually met this problem.

**Cause 3: leafhopper damage.** This looks like yellowing from a distance, but up close the leaves are covered in fine pale speckles rather than turning evenly yellow. See the leafhopper section below.

**How to tell them apart.** Evenly yellow leaves on a plant in wet soil point to the roots. Speckled leaves on a plant in dry soil point to leafhoppers.

**Fix.** Stop watering a plant in the ground. If the soil is heavy, the RHS suggests growing lavender in a raised bed or a container instead. In a pot, empty any saucer and use a gritty, free-draining mix. Check the roots of a badly affected plant. Brown, soft, smelly roots mean root rot, and our [root rot recovery guide](/en/blog/root-rot-recovery) explains what can be saved.

## Why is my lavender drooping or wilting?

Drooping has two opposite causes, so feel the soil before you do anything.

**Wet soil: root rot.** Rotting roots cannot take up water, so the plant wilts even though the soil is wet. This is the commoner cause for lavender in the ground, especially in clay soil or after a wet spell. Treat it as above.

**Dry soil: drought.** Established lavender in the ground is drought tolerant, and the RHS says it rarely needs watering except in severe drought. Two cases are different. Newly planted lavender needs regular water through its first summer. Lavender in containers needs regular water in summer, because pots dry out quickly and the roots have little soil to draw on. Water a dry pot thoroughly and let it drain.

**A rare cause in southern Europe.** The RHS lists lavender among the plants that the bacterium *Xylella fastidiosa* infects. It causes leaf scorch, wilting, dieback and death, and the RHS notes outbreaks in southern France, including Corsica, as well as in Spain, the Balearic Islands and Portugal. The symptoms can look identical to drought or frost damage, and only a laboratory test confirms it. Buy plants from a reputable nursery, and report a suspected case to your national plant health authority.

## Why is my lavender turning grey or brown after winter?

**Cold, wet roots.** The RHS advises against planting even hardy types in cold, damp spots. For pots, the RHS recommends keeping the compost fairly dry in winter, for example at the foot of a wall out of the rain or in a cold greenhouse, which it says helps the plant tolerate cold.

**A tender type.** French and other tender lavenders are short-lived and, according to the RHS, best grown in containers and kept frost-free over winter. Some specialist types from southern Spain or the Canary Islands are not hardy at all. Check the label when you buy.

**Frost-damaged tips.** These are often just the outer foliage. The University of Maryland Extension advises waiting until spring, when new growth appears, before cutting off winter damage, and the RHS says you can clip over untidy or frost-damaged foliage in spring. Cut only into stems that still carry leaves.

## Why is my lavender woody and bare in the middle?

This is age plus missed pruning. The RHS says lavender left unpruned becomes woody and ungainly, and that it does not readily regrow from old, bare stems. Maryland Extension goes further and warns that cutting lavender back hard can kill it.

**Prevention.** Trim it every year in late summer, just after flowering. The RHS suggests removing the spent flower stalks and about 2.5 cm (1 in) of leafy growth, without cutting into the woody stems. Maryland advises not pruning after mid-August.

**Fix.** An old plant that has already split open or gone bare will not recover with a hard cut. The RHS says older plants become straggly and misshapen even with yearly pruning, and that because lavender grows fast and establishes quickly, they are best replaced. Take cuttings from the healthy shoots first.

## Why is my lavender not flowering?

**Too little sun.** This is the usual reason. Move the plant, or its pot, to the sunniest spot you have.

**Feeding.** The RHS says lavender likes soil that is quite low in nutrients and does not generally need feeding. If you have been feeding it like a border perennial, stop.

**Spent flowers left on.** The RHS says cutting off faded blooms encourages more to form, and Maryland notes that deadheading after the first flush can bring a second one.

## What pests attack lavender?

The RHS says few pests feed on lavender, that the damage is usually cosmetic, and that treatment is not normally needed. It lists four.

### Rosemary beetle

The [RHS guide to rosemary beetle](https://www.rhs.org.uk/biodiversity/rosemary-beetle) describes adults 6 to 7 mm (about ¼ in) long, shiny, with metallic purple and green stripes. The larvae are greyish-white with darker stripes. Both eat leaves and flowers, sometimes reducing leaves to greyish-brown stumps. They are most active from August to April.

Healthy plants usually survive them without lasting harm. The RHS advises tolerating them where possible, encouraging birds, frogs and ground beetles, and picking them off by hand. On taller plants, tap the branches over a sheet of newspaper and collect what falls. The same beetle attacks rosemary, sage and thyme, as our guide to [rosemary problems](/en/blog/rosemary-problems) explains.

### Sage and Ligurian leafhoppers

These small sap-feeders cause a coarse pale mottling on the upper side of the leaves. The RHS describes the adults as up to 3.5 mm (⅛ in) long, pale green with brown and black markings, and quick to jump when disturbed. White cast skins, sometimes called ghost insects, often stay stuck under the leaves. They are most active from April to September. By late summer the leaves can be heavily mottled, but the RHS says this seems to have little effect on growth.

### Cuckoo spit

Blobs of white froth on the stems from late spring to midsummer each hide a small froghopper nymph. The RHS says it is harmless, apart from some distorted growth if the nymph feeds at a shoot tip. Froghoppers can carry *Xylella* where that disease is present, which is why the RHS mentions them, but the froth itself does no damage.

### Xylella

See the drooping section above. It is the only one of the four that kills plants.

### A note on older advice

Many articles list aphids, whiteflies, spider mites, mealybugs and root weevils as the main lavender pests, and recommend neem, garlic sprays and diatomaceous earth. The RHS does not list those insects as lavender pests, and for the pests it does list, it recommends tolerance and hand-picking rather than sprays.

## Why does my lavender have spots on its leaves?

NC State lists leaf spot as one of the few diseases of lavender and says good air circulation helps prevent it. Space plants so air can move between them, and remove badly spotted shoots. Spots on a plant that is also yellowing in wet soil point back to the roots first.

## Is lavender toxic to cats and dogs?

Yes, mildly. The [ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants/lavender) lists lavender as toxic to dogs, cats and horses because of linalool and linalyl acetate. Signs include nausea, vomiting and loss of appetite. NC State rates the poison severity as low. Keep pets from chewing it, and call your vet if they eat a lot.

## Quick answers

**Can a lavender with root rot be saved?** Sometimes, if healthy white roots remain. Cut away the rotten roots and replant in a gritty mix in a pot or raised bed. A plant with no healthy roots left will not recover.

**How often should I water lavender in a pot?** Regularly in summer, once the top of the compost is dry, and very little in winter. Our [lavender care guide](/en/blog/lavender-care-guide) covers watering, soil and pruning in full.

**Why is lavender in Provence dying back?** In commercial fields there, INRAE, the French national research institute for agriculture, has linked a disease known as yellow decline to the stolbur phytoplasma, spread by a planthopper. It is a field-production problem, and the countermeasure is healthy certified plants.
