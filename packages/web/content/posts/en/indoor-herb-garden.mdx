An indoor herb garden works when you give herbs as much direct sun as you can: most need 6 to 8 hours a day, which means a south-facing window or a grow light. Plant each herb in its own pot with a drainage hole, let the soil dry between waterings, and start with easy herbs such as chives, parsley, mint and basil. Light, far more than water or feeding, decides whether indoor herbs stay compact and full of flavour.

## Which herbs to grow in an indoor herb garden

[Iowa State University Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-herbs-indoors) says almost any herb can be grown indoors, but some cope better with the lower light, low humidity and cooler temperatures of a home. It splits them into two groups.

**Start with these:** parsley, mint, chives, basil, oregano, cilantro and thyme.

**Try these next:** rosemary, sage, lemon balm, marjoram, chervil and bay laurel. Iowa State notes they can succeed indoors but need more light than usual or attract significant pests, such as spider mites.

[Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/growing-herbs-indoors) adds that rosemary and thyme do best in direct sun, while chives and cilantro tolerate lower light and can manage with 2 to 6 hours of direct sun a day.

Buying small plants is the fastest way to start. Basil, cilantro, chives and parsley grow easily from seed; mint does not come true from seed, according to [Clemson Extension's herb factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/herbs/), so start it from a cutting or a plant.

## Light: the limiting factor

Iowa State calls light the most limiting factor indoors. Most herbs need around eight hours of direct light a day; some tolerate six. Penn State gives 6 hours of direct sun as the minimum for most herbs.

- **Window.** A south-facing window is best. Iowa State says a bright east- or west-facing window can also work. A north window will not grow herbs well without help.
- **Rotate the pots** every few days so each side gets light, as Penn State suggests.
- **Watch for the signs of too little light.** Penn State describes herbs that grow thin and spindly, with smaller leaves and a weaker aroma.

**Grow lights.** In winter, when days are short, most windowsills fall short. Iowa State suggests full-spectrum grow lights in high-output fixtures, placed 15 to 30 cm (6 to 12 in) above the leaves, on a timer for 12 to 16 hours a day. Penn State gives 14 to 16 hours a day under fluorescent tubes. Iowa State's options range from a compact fluorescent bulb in a desk lamp for a few pots to a 1.2 m (4 ft) LED shop light for a larger collection. Our [houseplant light guide](/en/blog/houseplant-light-guide) explains how to judge window light.

## Temperature and humidity

Penn State recommends at least 18 to 21°C (65 to 70°F) by day and 13 to 16°C (55 to 60°F) at night. Most herbs survive brief dips to around 5 to 7°C (low to mid 40s °F), but basil is damaged below 10°C (50°F), so keep it off a cold windowsill in winter. Leaves pressed against cold glass can be damaged even when the room is warm.

Iowa State says typical indoor temperatures suit herbs well. Dry air is the bigger problem in winter. Penn State notes that homes with forced-air heating have the driest winter air, which dries leaves out, while too much humidity encourages root rot and fungal problems. Grouping pots together or setting them on a tray of moist pebbles raises humidity a little; a small fan improves airflow if the air is stuffy.

## Pots and soil

- **One herb per pot.** Iowa State recommends growing each species in its own container, because herbs with different needs are hard to keep happy in one pot. Rosemary and thyme want to dry out; basil and parsley do not. Clemson also advises confining mint, which spreads aggressively.
- **Drainage holes are essential.** Iowa State and Penn State both stress a container with drainage and a free-draining potting mix.
- **Size the pot to the plant.** Iowa State warns that containers that are too large stay wet too long. Our guide to [choosing a pot](/en/blog/how-to-choose-a-pot-for-your-plant) explains why.
- **Terracotta helps.** Iowa State likes clay pots for herbs because they are porous and dry out faster.
- **Use potting mix, not garden soil.** A general-purpose potting mix with some extra perlite suits most herbs. Rosemary, thyme and sage do better with a grittier mix.

## Watering an indoor herb garden

Iowa State's rule is to let the soil dry between waterings, because most herbs do not tolerate wet soil. Penn State refines it by herb:

| Herb group | Watering |
|---|---|
| Rosemary, thyme, sage, oregano, marjoram, bay | Let the mix dry out slightly between waterings |
| Basil | Never let the mix dry out completely |
| Parsley, mint, chives, cilantro | Water when the top of the mix starts to dry |

Check with a finger before each watering. Water until it drains from the hole, then empty the saucer. Small pots on a sunny sill can dry in two or three days in summer, and take a week or more in winter.

## Feeding

Herbs need less fertiliser than most container plants. Iowa State recommends a light feed with an all-purpose fertiliser at half or quarter strength from early spring through summer, and none in winter. Penn State suggests a low dose every 2 weeks and warns that feeding more often can hurt the aroma and taste. Clemson agrees that very rich soil gives lots of leaves with little flavour. Organic fertilisers based on fish or manure can smell strongly indoors.

## Harvesting

Regular harvesting keeps indoor herbs bushy. Iowa State's guidance:

- **Leafy annuals** such as basil, chervil and parsley can be cut back hard. Cut just above a leaf or pair of leaves and leave 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in) of stem to regrow.
- **Perennials** such as chives, mint, oregano and thyme should lose no more than the top third of their growth at a time.
- **Replace tired annuals.** After several harvests, start new basil, cilantro or parsley from seed.

Clemson recommends harvesting leafy herbs before they flower, when their oils are at their peak. Pinching out basil flower buds as they appear keeps the plant producing leaves. Indoor herbs often taste milder than outdoor ones, Iowa State notes, but basil still tastes like basil.

## Pests and problems

Clemson names aphids and spider mites as the main herb pests. Penn State adds fungus gnats, which breed in soil that stays wet.

- **Aphids.** Clusters on soft shoot tips. Penn State suggests trimming off infested stems and discarding them.
- **Spider mites.** Fine speckling and webbing, mostly on rosemary and thyme in dry, warm rooms. Rinse the plant with a strong spray of water, as Clemson suggests.
- **Fungus gnats.** Small flies around the pots. Penn State recommends yellow sticky traps near the pots and careful watering.

Because you eat these plants, avoid systemic insecticides. Penn State mentions neem oil or a *Bacillus thuringiensis* product only for severe cases, following the label. Our [common houseplant pests guide](/en/blog/common-houseplant-pests) covers identification.

Leggy, pale growth means too little light. Yellow lower leaves on wet soil mean too much water. Brown, crisp leaves on rosemary usually mean the pot dried out completely or the air is very dry. Our guides to [basil](/en/blog/basil-care-guide), [mint](/en/blog/mint-care-guide) and [rosemary](/en/blog/rosemary-care-guide) go deeper on each herb.

## Moving herbs outdoors in summer

Iowa State suggests moving perennial and tender herbs, such as rosemary, bay laurel and marjoram, outdoors once the danger of frost has passed. Penn State advises a gradual move: start in partial shade and give them a little more sun every few days, then check whether they need more water or feeding outside.
